# B2B vs B2C Contrast

## Identical for both

- The material-connection principle: payment, commission, free product, employment, family ties - same triggers, same duty to disclose.
- The clear-and-conspicuous standard (16 CFR 255.0(f)) and every per-format placement rule.
- The accepted/rejected wording verdicts.
- The advertiser's guide/monitor/remediate duties (16 CFR 255.1(d)), including non-delegable agency liability.
- The six-layer check runs unchanged for both; only the scenarios below need extra care.

## Where B2B differs

### Scope caveat - be honest about it

- The FTC Guides never say "B2B" or "consumer-only". The nearest textual signal sits _outside_ the endorsement framework.
- 16 CFR 255.0 Example 13 calls it deceptive to misrepresent social-media influence to "potential clients, purchasers, investors, partners, or employees." The same example states that buying fake followers "is not itself an endorsement issue," so the deception theory reaches business audiences through FTC Act Section 5, not through Part 255.
- Read this as evidence that the FTC's deception theory covers B2B audiences, never as proof that the Guides themselves do. Treat application of the Guides to B2B as by-analogy, and add `[COUNSEL]` when stakes are high.
- EU and UK consumer instruments (UCPD, CMA consumer law) protect consumers; Germany's UWG expressly also protects "other market participants". Never skip the check for a pure-B2B audience - flag `[COUNSEL: B2B applicability in <market>]` instead.

### Employee and insider advocacy

- Employee posts praising the employer's product need in-post employment disclosure (§255.5); §465.5 makes undisclosed insider reviews directly civil-penalty-backed - including officers, managers, and their immediate family.
- An employee-advocacy program is active encouragement: the company then owes monitoring of the resulting posts, not just a policy.

### Incentivized software-review-site reviews

- Every incentivized review must disclose the incentive (§255.2) - a gift card is an incentive.
- Aggregate-rating trap: per-review disclosure is not enough if incentivized reviews materially inflate the displayed average rating; the aggregate needs its own disclosure. Ask how the review platform handles this; `[COUNSEL]` if it doesn't.

### Consultant and analyst referral fees

- A consultant or analyst recommending a vendor that pays them a referral fee has a material connection; the disclosure belongs where the recommendation is made - the deck, the call summary, the published comparison.
- No FTC channel-specific guidance exists; the general material-connection principle applies by extension.

### Sponsored webinars and paid newsletter placements

- No channel-specific FTC guidance exists for either - say so rather than implying a rule. The material-connection principle still governs: label the webinar and the newsletter block as sponsored, adjacent to the content, before the audience engages.

### Agency-intermediated content

- Brand liability is non-delegable: hiring an agency does not shift responsibility (FTC FAQ). Require regular compliance reports and review agency-sourced content like any other partner content.
- Agency employees promoting the client carry their own disclosure duty on personal accounts.

## Where B2C differs

- B2C is the instruments' home ground: the UCPD, the CMA's endorsement guidance, France's influencer law, and Italy's Digital Chart are consumer-facing by design - apply them at full strength, no by-analogy caveat.
- Consumer formats - stories, short-form video, livestreams - concentrate the ephemeral-content risk; use the FTC's pre-approval fallback rather than after-the-fact monitoring - the pre-publication gate rung in SKILL.md § Ranked Choices.
