# Attribution Taxonomy and Compensation Neutrality

Citations name the publishing vendor or consultancy. Treat single-source specifics as one practitioner's convention.

## The taxonomy (established industry practice)

- **Partner-sourced**: the partner identified or initiated the opportunity, tested by _"would the deal still exist if the partner had not participated?"_ - if no, it is sourced. Measures pipeline generation. (Forecastable, Unifyr)
- **Partner-influenced**: the opportunity originated elsewhere (direct, marketing) but a partner materially advanced or accelerated it. Measures acceleration, not origination. (Unifyr, Suger)
- **Partner-attached**: some orgs collapse sourced + influenced into one number for executive reporting. Practitioners advise keeping the two separate for actionability - a combined number can't tell you whether partners generate pipeline or speed it up. (Pedowitz Group)

A more granular model adds four dimensions, with attribution **locked within 14 days of deal creation** to prevent backdated credit claims:

- Partner type.
- Motion (sourced/influenced).
- Influence role (tech win / deal access / implementation).
- Influence event.

This is one consultancy's framework (Forecastable), not an industry-wide standard. Present it as an option for orgs with multi-partner deals.

## Attribution governance

- **Co-own the number: partnerships plus revenue operations.** Partnerships alone reporting its own attribution looks self-serving. RevOps co-ownership makes it "an auditable company fact." (Forecastable)
- Reconcile contested or ambiguous attribution on a monthly or quarterly cadence, with deal-registration timestamps as the objective tiebreaker.
- Attribution claimed only after close is "unprovable and political" - require the claim to exist before the outcome is known.
- Adjudicate disputes under a defined SLA. The one concrete number in the literature is 5 business days (Forecastable).

## Comp neutrality (established term and practice)

Definition: the rep earns the same commission and quota retirement whether the deal closes direct, through a partner, or through a marketplace. The partner or marketplace fee is absorbed at company level or built into list price - never passed through to rep commission.

One vendor claims 64% of ISVs now run comp-neutral models and calls neutrality "the single most predictive variable in whether a sales team adopts marketplace channels" (Introw, Invisory). This claim is unverified beyond the citing sources, but the directional logic is corroborated by the failure-mode evidence below.

### Three funding approaches, ranked by value per unit of effort (Forma.ai, SaaStr)

Present them in this order. The axes disagree, so read all four:

- efficiency: `quota retirement > full double comp > secondary channel metric`
- value: `full double comp > quota retirement > secondary channel metric`
- effort: `full double comp > secondary channel metric > quota retirement`
- compliance cost: `full double comp > secondary channel metric == quota retirement`

The most effective rung is not the best ratio. Full double comp buys the most conflict reduction and still ranks second, because it costs a standing budget line and a commitment the field disputes if you ever withdraw it. That is exactly what this order starves: the rung a ratio never selects on its own.

Promote it on the symptom named in rung 2 below, not on ambition and not on a round it can win. The compliance tie is real, not a hedge: all three amend a comp plan, so all three are contractual commitments to the field and all three clear the same finance-plus-CRO sign-off chain. Only double comp adds an open-ended payout that cannot be pulled mid-plan-year.

1. **Quota retirement** - channel/partner revenue retires the rep's quota dollar-for-dollar (or at a partial rate). One clause, one sign-off chain, reversible at the next plan cycle, and it removes the canonical adoption killer: the rep no longer loses credit for attaching a partner. Best ratio, and the recommended default rung.
2. **Full double comp** - pay full commission to both the direct rep and the partner on the same deal, treating a channel deal exactly like a direct one. Cited as most effective at reducing internal conflict. One case study reports doubled sales the following year (single case, not a benchmark).

   Costs a standing budget line and the least reversible commitment on the page. Move up to it when quota retirement is in place and reps still route around partners - that symptom, not ambition, is the trigger. Cheaper variant: pay a fixed % of commission scaled to estimated co-sell effort.

3. **Secondary channel metric** - a channel-volume measure (dollar quota, or % of deals requiring partner involvement) triggers accelerators on top of base comp. Adds positive pull but does not repair the base credit loss, so it buys no neutrality on its own.

   It also needs a channel-volume number RevOps can audit every plan cycle, which is a standing job rather than a one-time clause. Lowest value and second-highest effort - hence last.

Constraints that delete a rung rather than demote it:

- No auditable channel-volume measure in the CRM: **delete the secondary channel metric.** An accelerator nobody can audit becomes a dispute engine, which is the failure this whole section exists to prevent.
- The comp plan already retires quota on partner-attached revenue: **delete quota retirement** as a proposal, confirm it in the charter, and start the menu at full double comp.
- Sales leadership will not reopen the comp plan at all: **delete all three.** Say so plainly, cap the charter at registration and rules-of-engagement, and record the missing neutrality as a known risk rather than proposing a rung that cannot be signed.

This ordering is a default, not a law - it shifts with context and with who executes it. Re-rank it against what you already know about the user before presenting. Consider an existing plan that already credits partner deals, a partnerships leader with real standing in comp reviews, or a single strategic partner whose deals are rare enough that per-deal exceptions beat any plan change.

### Sign-off requirements

Neutrality only holds if three functions align (Partnerled, Partner-Path):

- **Finance** funds the parity - it absorbs the partner/marketplace fee.
- **Sales leadership / CRO** embeds it in comp plans and the rules-of-engagement document. One source calls this co-sign "non-negotiable." The same sources warn that getting executive buy-in to alter field comp is "very difficult" in practice - budget real political effort for this step.
- **Partnerships** defines the registration and approval mechanics that make neutral credit assignable.

## Directional benchmarks - flag every one when quoting

None of these is independent research; use them to frame conversations, never as targets to promise.

- Partner-sourced pipeline share: 20-30% cited for early-stage programs, 40-50%+ for mature channel-led businesses (Introw - vendor content). Concentration-risk flag when one partner exceeds ~30% of channel revenue.
- Industry-wide partner revenue share: one source cites 26-28% of revenue via partners in 2025, up from 18-20% in 2024 - unverified aggregate with no named study behind it (Introw).
- Win-rate context: general B2B baseline ~21% across all opportunities, ~29% for qualified-only. Partner/referral deals reported in the 35-55% range (Landbase, mixed vendor sources). Cross-company win-rate comparison is unreliable because opportunity-creation criteria differ by company - logging opportunities only post-qualification structurally inflates the number.
- The widely repeated claim that partner-sourced deals have "the highest win rate" is unverified: the primary data behind it actually shows partner _referrals_ have the highest sales _velocity_ (3.8x) - a different claim. The headline partner-lift trio (more likely to close, faster, bigger) traces to account-mapping vendor marketing.
- One analyst observation worth keeping (Jay McBain): the average customer works with roughly **seven partners in every buying process** - the structural argument for writing an explicit multi-party split rule instead of assuming one partner per deal.

## Negative example: the comp-misalignment spiral

What the failure looks like in practice, assembled from sourced findings:

- The comp plan rewards booking, not channel discipline. A rep who attaches a partner loses quota credit, or a 3-5% marketplace fee is passed through to their commission. Result: "if a rep loses quota credit when a partner co-sells, they will never say yes" - reps quietly avoid and sandbag the partner motion. (Suger)
- Registrations live in a partner portal the sales team never opens. The direct AE "never saw the registration" and is functionally excluded from the process as neither informed nor consulted. This is where channel conflict originates. (Fullcast)
- With no attribution lock and no dispute SLA, credit is negotiated deal by deal. One consultancy audit found reps spending **50% of their time negotiating splits and justifying influence** rather than selling. (Forma.ai - single audit, directional)
- Multi-party deals (vendor rep + cloud-provider rep + SI) break the tooling: most registration systems were built for one partner per deal. "The single hardest operational problem in co-selling is attribution." (Suger)

Each element is preventable by a charter line: neutrality funding model, registration visibility inside the sales system, a lock window, a dispute SLA, and an explicit multi-party split rule.
