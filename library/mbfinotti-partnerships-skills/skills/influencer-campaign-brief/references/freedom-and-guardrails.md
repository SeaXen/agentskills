# Freedom and guardrails - what to lock, what to leave open

The non-negotiables vs preferences split appears across four unrelated publishers under four different names:

- mandatory vs inspirational
- non-negotiables vs creative freedom
- to-include vs leave-open
- a template split named without mechanics

It is a convergent practice, not a named framework - the most corroborated structural idea in creator briefing.

## Lock these (non-negotiables)

- Discount codes and core offer details: price, dates, terms - exactly as the deal and the offer define them.
- Mandatory legal or regulated wording, verbatim, with "must appear exactly" stated.
- Prohibited claims and comparisons - listed explicitly, with the approved alternative wording where one exists.
- Disclosure wording and placement (own section in the brief; the compliance skill checks the finished content).
- Brand-name pronunciation and product-name spelling.
- Factual accuracy standards: no invented features, results, or customer status.

## Leave these open (preferences)

- Speaking style, delivery, and pacing.
- Setting, personal stories, and how the product enters the narrative.
- The hook - suggest angles from the brainstorm; never dictate the wording.
- Editing approach, cuts, and music (within the licensing constraint).
- Caption phrasing outside the mandatory elements.

"Script the facts, not the voice" (single-vendor phrasing, the clearest articulation found). Lock the first, never the second.

- Facts: claims, dates, prices, specs, regulated statements.
- Voice: framing, hooks, pacing, delivery.

## Freedom rungs, best ratio first (levels single-vendor; the ordering is this skill's)

Ordered by campaign result per unit of briefing effort - the axis lines, the default, and the promotion conditions live in SKILL.md § Non-negotiables vs preferences, where the level is chosen. Do not re-derive a different order here.

| Rung           | Creator controls                                  | Brief locks                                            | Costs you                                                                                                          | Promote when                                                                               |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| High (default) | concept, setting, hook, script                    | messages, disclosure, prohibited claims                | about an hour of briefing, one round                                                                               | awareness goal, repeat creator, near-fixed live date, no in-house reviewer                 |
| Medium         | adapting a given concept direction to their style | the concept direction + all High locks                 | half a day of briefing, a concept-approval step                                                                    | consideration goal, first collaboration, creator new to the category                       |
| Low            | voice and delivery only, within a framework       | the framework: structure, offer, code, CTA + all locks | about a week of coordination, plus the rounds spent enforcing the framework, plus sign-off on the scripted wording | the metric is a redemption or signup count, or a regulated category forces script approval |

State the level in the brief. A low-freedom brief is legitimate for conversion work - what is never legitimate is granting freedom on paper and revoking it at validation. Low is off the menu entirely, not demoted, when the deal includes one revision round or fewer.

## Why over-scripting fails (each formulation single-vendor; the direction is unanimous across all retrieved sources)

- Overly detailed briefs produce content that is technically correct but not something that creator would normally publish - the audience notices instantly.
- The interchangeability test: a brief that could be handed to any creator and produce roughly the same content has destroyed the exact audience-trust relationship the brand paid for.
- Creators are followed for their voice; micromanaging at the validation stage discourages the creator and impoverishes the result - and contradicting the brief's granted freedom damages the relationship more than a tight brief would have.
- Survey context (one vendor-run October 2025 survey of its own creator database, n=403 creators, ±4.9% at 95% confidence, paired with a brand survey, n=222, ±6.6%):
  - 10% of creators report genuine creative control.
  - 53% want more freedom.
  - 34% feel they have little to no influence in shaping campaigns.
  - Brands reuse 31% of creators annually, against an 8% creator retention rate.

  Cite only with this methodology - the figures already mutate in secondary retellings - and never attribute them to a named person.

## Which guardrails to spend the 5-10 rules on, best ratio first (items corroborated; the ordering is this skill's)

Rank guardrails by brand risk removed per unit of creator freedom taken, not by how uncomfortable each one makes the brand feel. Take the top three by default; add a lower rung only when its condition fires.

- efficiency (risk removed per unit of freedom taken): verbatim legal wording == prohibited claims > accuracy standards > competitor mentions > brand-safety adjacency > sensitive-topic list
- effort (drafting, and negotiating the item with the creator): sensitive-topic list > brand-safety adjacency > prohibited claims > verbatim legal wording == accuracy standards == competitor mentions
- compliance cost (review the item triggers before the brief goes out): prohibited claims == verbatim legal wording > brand-safety adjacency > the rest, which trigger none

The two `==` ties are real, not indecision:

- verbatim legal wording and the prohibited-claims list come out of one legal pass on one claim set, and each costs the creator only sentences they were never going to write
- accuracy standards and the competitor rule are each a single line lifted from the deal or the brand's standing policy

1. **Mandatory verbatim legal wording, and disclosure** - one or two sentences of freedom taken, and it removes the liability the advertiser itself carries.
2. **Prohibited claims and comparisons** - list the approved alternative wording beside each. The alternative is what keeps the cost near-zero instead of leaving the creator with a hole in the script.
3. **Factual accuracy standards** - no invented features, results, or customer status. Near-zero to write, and it is the rule that saves a takedown.
4. **Competitor mentions** - cheap to state, but it removes less risk than the three above; include it when the category is crowded enough that a comparison is likely.
5. **Brand-safety and content-adjacency requirements** - an hour to define properly, and worth it only when placement risk is real for this creator's surface.
6. **Topics sensitive for this brand** - highest effort and it costs the creator's own stories, which is the asset being bought. Name each topic explicitly. An unnamed sensitivity silently swallows the freedom granted above it.

What this order starves: the sensitive-topic list is high-value and high-effort, so it never survives a ratio ranking - promote it regardless when the brand has a live controversy, or when the creator's normal content already touches the topic.

Re-rank against what you know about the brand and creator:

- a regulated category promotes claims sign-off to first, and makes it non-optional
- a creator who has worked with this brand before demotes brand-safety definition, because it is already internalized
- no in-house legal makes the prohibited-claims list the only control that can actually be produced

Deleted from the menu, not ranked at the bottom of it (documented as harmful): dictating exact wording, camera angles, music choice, cuts, or caption phrasing, and attaching the complete brand style guide. They take voice - the thing the brand paid for - and remove no risk the six rungs above have not already removed.

Extract the 5-10 rules that bear on this content type. Leave the guide out, and say you left it out rather than listing it as a low-priority option that returns as scope.

## Regulated-category guardrails (single-vendor but concrete - always confirm with legal or compliance before the brief goes out)

- Health/fitness: an explicit "claims you cannot make" list with the approved alternative language beside each.
- Beauty: before-and-after content flagged for compliance review before posting.
- Fashion: a mood board works better than prescriptive instruction.
- Food/recipe: enough runtime (60-90s) that essential steps survive the edit.
- B2B regulated verticals - finance, security, HR/employment (inference): route every quantified claim through compliance sign-off before the brief is sent, and name the approver in the review section.
