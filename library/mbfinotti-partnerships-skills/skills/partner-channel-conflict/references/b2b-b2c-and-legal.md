# B2C Conflict and Legal Boundaries

Everything in the legal sections below is awareness-level: flag it for counsel review before publication, never draft pricing, territory, or termination clauses as if they were purely commercial. This is not legal advice.

## The consumer-brand contrast

The signature B2C conflict is **D2C vs retail/wholesale partner**: vertical conflict in the classic typology, so the theory transfers. The artifacts do not.

In B2B the vendor controls the rules through contract and comp. In B2C the brand's strongest pricing lever must be structured as a _unilateral_ policy to avoid antitrust liability, and marketplaces will not enforce the brand's policies for it.

- **The route-to-market map is the governing artifact** (the B2C counterpart of the RoE): every route - D2C site, marketplace, wholesale, distributors, retail stores, dealers - documented with owner, customer segment, SKU assortment, pricing authority, fulfillment responsibility, return policy, and approved geography.
- **MAP (Minimum Advertised Price) policy** restricts the price resellers may _advertise_, not the price they sell at. In the US it is viable only as a unilateral Colgate policy: announce, distribute, monitor, refuse to deal with violators - **never negotiate levels, never solicit retailer input, never grant private exceptions, never require signatures**. Any of those risks converting the unilateral policy into a bilateral agreement subject to Sherman Act §1.
- **Channel-exclusive assortment / SKU differentiation is the real structural lever**, not price: retailer-specific SKUs/EANs, colorways, configurations, staggered releases, per-account assortment depth. Price parity alone cannot fix D2C conflict because the conflict is over attention, traffic, and trust - with identical prices the dealer still loses every customer who converts on the brand's own domain.
- **Marketplaces refuse to enforce brand policy.** Amazon explicitly will not enforce MAP or reseller policies on a brand's behalf and welcomes "legitimate unauthorized sellers" as increasing selection and lowering prices. Plan enforcement around that fact, not against it.
- **Grey-market enforcement** runs through trademark's **material-difference doctrine**: genuine goods resold without authorization are generally protected by first-sale, but goods lacking the manufacturer warranty or quality controls are "materially different" and lose that protection. Illustrative cases: _Otter Products v. Triplenet Pricing_ (D. Colo. 2021), _Beltronics v. Midwest Inventory Distribution_ (10th Cir. 2009), _Zino Davidoff v. CVS_ (2d Cir. 2009). Selective-distribution / authorized-reseller agreements are what make these theories available - that is their governance function.

## The Nike reversal - the canonical B2C case

Under the 2017 "Consumer Direct Offense," Nike narrowed to ~40 strategic retail partners and exited "undifferentiated" accounts. By September 2021 its CFO said it had exited about 50% of its retail partners.

DTC rose from 16% of brand sales (2011) to ~42% by FY2023, but never reached the 60% goal. FY2023 net income fell 16%, and Nike could not clear inventory without wholesale shelf space.

It re-entered DSW and Macy's in October 2023, rebuilt Foot Locker, and returned to Amazon in 2025, pairing the retreat with a $2B cost-savings plan. Its CEO conceded in 2024 that it had over-rotated away from wholesale. (Adidas ran a parallel reversal.)

The governance lesson: unilaterally rewriting the route-to-market map against your channel is a strategy decision with multi-year inventory and P&L consequences, not a conflict-resolution tactic. The partners you cut do not come back on the old terms.

## US antitrust - the RPM lineage (flag for counsel)

- _Dr. Miles v. John D. Park & Sons_ (1911): minimum resale-price maintenance per se illegal.
- _United States v. Colgate_ (1919): a manufacturer may unilaterally announce resale prices and refuse to deal with non-compliant resellers - the **Colgate doctrine** MAP policies rely on.
- _Monsanto v. Spray-Rite_ (1984): proving a vertical price conspiracy requires evidence excluding independent action.
- _State Oil v. Khan_ (1997): maximum RPM under the rule of reason.
- _Leegin v. PSKS_ (2007): overruled _Dr. Miles_. Minimum RPM now rule-of-reason federally.
- **State law diverges from _Leegin_, but the per se map is narrower than usually reported** - check the specific state, never a memorized list:
  - **Maryland** is the one clean express repealer. Com. Law §11-204(b) (SB 239, effective 1 October 2009) makes a minimum-resale-price agreement an unreasonable restraint per se. §11-209 caps the civil penalty at $10,000 per violation, each continuing day a separate violation.
  - **California** under the Cartwright Act is the defensible second case.
  - **New York is weaker than reported.** GBL §369-a makes minimum-price agreements _unenforceable_, not per se illegal. _People v. Tempur-Pedic Int'l_, 95 A.D.3d 539 (1st Dep't 2012), rejected the AG's per se theory.
  - **Kansas is no longer per se.** _O'Brien v. Leegin_, 294 Kan. 318 (2012), imposed per se treatment. The legislature overrode it the next session: K.S.A. 50-163(b) (L. 2013, ch. 102) directs the Kansas Restraint of Trade Act be construed in harmony with federal antitrust interpretations, rule of reason. §50-163(d) withholds that harmonization from attorney-general actions and indirect-purchaser claims under §50-161. Mark those `[LEGAL REVIEW]` rather than assuming either standard.
- This state-law tail is why national brands use unilateral MAP rather than RPM agreements. The FTC's 2000 music-distribution MAP orders (Docket C-3971) show MAP itself can cross the line when tied to co-op funds and coordinated across distributors.

## EU (flag for counsel)

- **VBER, Regulation (EU) 2022/720** (in force June 2022, to 2034): safe harbor below 30% market share, absent hardcore restrictions.
- **MAP is a hardcore restriction in the EU** - the Vertical Guidelines treat minimum advertised price as indirect RPM, the opposite of its US status. Keep MAP-style policies region-segmented. Never push a US MAP into EU/UK channels.
- **Dual distribution** (supplier selling direct in competition with its distributors - exactly the D2C-vs-retail scenario) has a narrowed safe harbor for information exchange: sharing future prices or identified end-customer data with distributors you compete with is constrained.
- **Active vs passive sales**: active selling into an exclusively allocated territory can be restricted (shared exclusivity up to five distributors). Passive sales generally cannot.
- **Commercial Agents Directive (86/653/EEC)**: self-employed agents get mandatory termination indemnity or compensation, cannot contract out, and a non-EU choice-of-law clause will not defeat it (_Ingmar_). A routine US channel action - terminate at will and reassign the territory - is a mandatory-indemnity trigger in the EU. Treat termination/reassignment and dual-distribution information exchange as legal-review triggers, not operational decisions.
