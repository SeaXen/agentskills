# Compensation Neutrality and Incentive Design

## What neutrality means

Compensation neutrality: the direct rep is paid, or has quota retired, at the same rate whether or not a partner is involved in the deal. By default most comp plans pay direct reps little or nothing on partner-transacted deals, which makes competing against partners the rep's rational move.

The conflict this creates is structural, not attitudinal: no rules-of-engagement document survives a comp plan that pays reps to break it. Programs that rely on rules of engagement alone report the policies routinely circumvented. Comp neutrality is the single most-cited real conflict control across B2B software, two-tier distribution, and consumer channels.

## The three design variants, ranked by efficiency

Behavior change bought per unit of comp spend and rollout effort. Cost and value run the same way here, which is exactly why the efficiency order inverts both: the most expensive variant is not the one to start with:

- efficiency: quota retirement > channel metric in the plan > full commission on channel deals
- value (behavior bought): full commission (reps chase partner deals) > channel metric (reps seek partners out) > quota retirement (reps stop competing with partners)
- effort: full commission (comp-plan amendment, finance sign-off, and the hardest thing on this page to withdraw later) > channel metric (a target to set, track and defend each cycle) > quota retirement (a line in the plan, then nothing)

1. **Quota retirement from channel revenue**: partner-involved revenue retires the rep's quota dollar-for-dollar, or at a haircut (e.g. $0.50 on the dollar). The rep still hits their number on a partner deal. The haircut variant costs less still but reintroduces a mild bias against partner deals: present the trade-off. This is the durable steady state, and the other two step down to it.
2. **A channel metric in the comp plan**: a dollar quota of partner-involved business, or a required share of deals including a partner (e.g. "40% of quota flows through partners"), paying as a secondary metric or accelerator. Actively steers behavior toward partners rather than merely removing the penalty.
3. **Full commission on channel deals as if direct** ("pay both" / double-comp): most expensive, fastest-acting, and the right opener only for an early-stage program that needs direct sales to embrace partners inside one comp cycle. Adopt it only with a stated review date for stepping down to variant 1 or 2: a comp lever is easy to add and politically expensive to take back, so the reversibility cost lands on whoever holds the plan next.

Default rung: quota retirement, full dollar-for-dollar. Move up one rung when the interview shows reps still route around partners under neutral comp, or when a hard date makes slow behavior change unacceptable.

Full commission is what this ratio starves: top of value, top of effort, bottom of efficiency. It is the only variant that makes a direct rep actively want the partner in the deal. Promote it when an early-stage program needs that switch flipped inside one comp cycle and someone senior will defend the plan change.

Where the interview says there is no political capital in the direct sales line, delete it from the menu and say it was deleted. A variant left at the bottom gets proposed again at the next comp cycle by someone who never heard the constraint.

Related mechanisms worth pairing, cheapest first:

- Bonuses for _supporting_ a partner deal rather than taking it over (near-zero, and it targets the exact behavior).
- Channel-sourced deal overrides protecting partner margin when a direct rep assists.
- Partner-attached selling requirements (certain deal types must include a partner even when direct originates: a standing enforcement job).

## The partner-price trap

If the rep is paid (or quota-retired) on the discounted price the partner pays rather than the price the customer pays, the rep earns less on every partner deal even under nominal "neutrality": expect persistent vertical conflict. This is the single most important line-item to check in the direct comp plan, and the neutrality decision must be written into **both** the partner agreement and the direct comp plan. A commitment that exists in only one document is not a commitment.

## Stackable margins

Paying partners at each of several stages: opportunity identification, technical win, transaction, rather than one registration uplift. Explicitly pitched as an antidote to registration abuse: a partner who only registered and did nothing earns only the identification slice, so squatting stops paying. (Trade-press sourced. Adopt as a candidate design, not a default.)

## The genuine dissent

Neutrality is not unanimous: at a partnering-executive roundtable, nearly half of participants opposed comp neutrality on cost grounds, paying full freight on revenue the channel would have delivered anyway.

The honest framing for the user: neutrality trades comp cost for conflict reduction. The dissent is about whether the trade is worth it at their margin structure, not about whether the mechanism works. Model the cost of each variant against gross margin before recommending (per-partner economics belong to the economics sibling skill).

## Provenance flags on the magnitude claims

The direction - neutral comp reduces conflict - is near-universal practitioner consensus. The magnitudes are not evidence: the widely circulated figures (a company doubling sales after adopting channel-neutral comp, deals 181% bigger, win rates 40% higher for collaborative selling) trace to a single self-interested practitioner source (Norma Watenpaugh, via vendor-adjacent content) and are illustrative at best.

Likewise the circulating claim that unresolved conflict cuts partner-sourced pipeline 30-40% is vendor-glossary material. Quote the direction with confidence, the numbers only with flags.
