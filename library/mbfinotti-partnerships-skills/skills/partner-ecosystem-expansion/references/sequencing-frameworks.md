# Sequencing Frameworks

What genuinely fits this decision, what partially fits, and what to reject. No peer-reviewed optimal sequence exists; the honest method assembles the pieces below.

Deliberately unranked: these five are not competing options for one goal but complements used together in a single pass.

- The trifurcation builds the pool.
- The value blueprint decides order.
- Capacity math caps it.
- The inverted-U says when to stop.
- Moore says which gap a segment demands.

Ranking complements by value per unit of effort would be false precision, since dropping the "low-efficiency" one leaves a question none of the others answers. The rankings that do apply are built at their own workflow steps, each against its own axes: candidate categories, facilitation formats, and leading indicators.

The force-fits in the last section are not options in that pool at all - they are rejects kept only so a user who arrives with one gets a straight answer.

## The anchor: Adner - MVE, staged expansion, value blueprint

- **Minimum viable ecosystem (MVE)**: "the smallest configuration of activities (and partners) that can create enough evidence of value creation to attract new partners" (Adner). Every expansion conversation starts here: is the current configuration an MVE with repeatable joint wins? If not, stage 1 of the roadmap is finishing it, not adding.
- **Staged expansion**: "the order in which an additional partner or activity should be brought on board." Additions are deliberate and sequential; each stage builds on the evidence the previous one created.
- **Value blueprint**: map every actor and link required for the value proposition to materialize, drawn from the end customer backward. It surfaces the two risks that decide category order: **co-innovation risk** (a category only creates value if someone else's piece also ships) and **adoption-chain risk** (an actor in the chain has no incentive to participate). A candidate category whose blueprint shows unresolved adoption-chain risk gets sequenced later or fixed first, however attractive its economics.
- **The ego-system trap**: presuming your own centrality - drawing the blueprint with yourself at the center and assuming partners will orbit. The check: redraw from the customer's position and ask, for each candidate category, what that partner type gains by joining _now_. A category with no answer will sign logos and never activate.
- Cross-adoption evidence: BCG's ecosystem-design work explicitly builds on Adner's MVE - the framework has left academia and entered consulting practice.

Sources: _The Wide Lens_ (2012); _Winning the Right Game_ (2021) - ACADEMIC/PRACTITIONER.

## The restraint evidence: the alliance-portfolio inverted-U

Alliance-portfolio-diversity research (Jiang, Tao & Santoro 2010, _Strategic Management Journal_; de Leeuw, Lokshin & Duysters 2014; Lee, Kirkpatrick-Husk & Madhavan 2017 meta-analysis) finds that partner-type diversity has an **inverted-U** relationship with firm performance: adding categories helps up to a point, then coordination and governance costs dominate. This is the field's only independent, non-self-interested evidence.

It justifies restraint and sequencing; it prescribes no order. Practical use: the standing argument against "add them all", and the roadmap-level signal - coordination cost rising while sourced revenue flattens - that says stop adding and deepen. ACADEMIC.

## The taxonomy: McBain / Canalys channel trifurcation

- **Influence**: referral, affiliate, advocacy, creators, analysts.
- **Transact**: resellers, VARs, distributors, marketplaces.
- **Retain-and-deliver**: MSPs, SIs, agencies delivering outcomes.

A classification for finding the empty bucket - which journey stages carry no partner coverage - never a sequence. Use it to build the candidate pool, then sequence with the other tools. ANALYST.

## The partial fit: Moore - whole product and bowling alley

- **Whole product**: pragmatist buyers purchase the complete solution, and partner categories exist to close the gap between what you ship and what the segment needs. Genuinely useful for deciding _which_ category a segment demands: a gap named in three or more lost deals pulls its filling category forward.
- **Bowling-alley adjacency**: expansion works pin by pin, each conquest making the next cheaper. The adjacency logic transfers to categories - the cheapest next category is the one that reuses the most of what the current base already built (an ISV motion makes a marketplace stage cheap; a referral motion makes an agency-referral track cheap). The segment-marketing machinery around it does not transfer; take the adjacency logic, not the whole book.

## The working method: coverage/capacity math and stage-gate pilots

Unbranded but real - this is what practitioners actually do:

- Coverage-gap analysis (segment × geography × journey stage) to justify a category.
- Partner-manager-to-partner ratios and enablement bandwidth as the hard cap.
- Pilot cohorts of one to three partner types, gated on partner-market fit (repeatable joint wins) before any scale investment.

Alexander Group's published SaaS guidance follows this shape - activate new categories one at a time, "prove the model with a handful of named accounts", and extend only once the flywheel shows momentum, typically within two to three quarters (CONSULTANT - the firm's own modeled engagement figures, not independent evidence).

## Named force-fits - reject these

- **Ansoff matrix applied to channels.** Built for product/market growth options; "new channel = new quadrant" supplies a label, no sequencing logic, and there is no evidence of systematic partner-category use.
- **McKinsey three horizons.** Innovation-portfolio timing vocabulary ("core/adjacent/transformational partner bets") borrowed loosely; nothing in it says which category or when.
- **BCG growth-share matrix.** Portfolio-balance language retrofitted to partner mix; intuitive, but no documented channel methodology behind it.
- **Iansiti & Levien (keystone/niche/dominator).** Describes your _role_ in an ecosystem, not which partner types to add next; academically influential, rarely used by channel practitioners for this decision.

When a user arrives with one of these, keep its vocabulary if it helps the room communicate - then rebuild the actual decision on MVE + gates + capacity.

## A genuine open tension - say it, don't resolve it silently

Whether partnering is a day-one motion or a later-stage accelerant is genuinely contested across sources, and the resolution is category-dependent. Influence-bucket categories (referral, affiliate, creators) are cheap enough to run early, while transact- and deliver-bucket categories (reseller, MSP, SI) presuppose a proven product motion and real enablement infrastructure.

That is a statement about which categories are _available_ early, not a sequence - the per-category efficiency order decides what opens first among the available ones. Tell users this is a tension in the evidence rather than picking a side for them.
