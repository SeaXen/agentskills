# Discovery Workshops: VNA, Value Blueprint, IPP, JVP

The profession has its own discovery formats. Run these instead of generic brainstorming, and keep the session discipline throughout: one question per message, 2-3 candidate options with trade-offs plus a recommendation, and every output validated section by section before it hardens.

## Value Network Analysis - the participative mapping workshop

Verna Allee's Value Network Analysis (developed 1993, adapted 1997; later adopted by ITIL and eTOM standards) is the most directly applicable _technique_ for drafting the map itself, and it is natively a **participative workshop**. That participative form is also the most expensive way to source an inventory: a week of calendars plus the political capital to convene them. Workflow step 2 ranks it last on efficiency and reaches for it only when the map has to be owned outside partnerships.

The technique below still applies when you draft solo. You just lose the ownership, and you have to hunt the intangible arrows yourself.

1. Place the focal organization at the centre of a shared canvas.
2. Arrange the roles around it - partner categories, customers, competitors, other participants.
3. Draw an arrow for every **tangible** deliverable exchanged (product, payment, leads) and every **intangible** one (trust, market insight, brand association).
4. Assign an owner to each relationship layer (sales owns customers; partnerships owns consultants, channel, alliances).
5. Follow up with interviews and analysis where the workshop exposed unknowns.

The intangible arrows are the point: they surface the influence band (advisors, communities, advocates) that a transaction-only view misses entirely.

## Adner's value blueprint - the dependency check

Ron Adner's _The Wide Lens_ (2012) supplies the strategic overlay: the **value blueprint**, "a map that makes your ecosystem and your dependencies explicit." Apply its three tests to the drafted map:

- **Co-innovation risk** - which mapped players must build or change something for your value proposition to work?
- **Adoption-chain risk** - which intermediaries must adopt before the end customer can? A partner category on the chain that has no reason to adopt is a hidden blocker, not coverage.
- **Minimum Viable Ecosystem** - the smallest set of mapped players that delivers the value proposition at all; anything beyond it is expansion, not requirement.

## IPP definition session - one per surviving category

An **Ideal Partner Profile** is a screen: it defines who is worth recruiting before anyone spends time on an individual partner. Crucially, it is _not_ the ICP copied across: it profiles the company best placed to _bring you_ those customers.

Sequencing, resolved for mapping work: outcomes scope the map first (what the company needs: markets, integration, distribution). Then **one IPP per partner category that survives prioritization** is an _output_ of the map, not an input to it.

Criteria families to work through in the session:

- Firmographics - size, market standing, financial stability, geography.
- **Customer-base overlap with your ICP** - the map's overlap data answers this directly.
- Delivery capability - can they actually sell, implement, or build with you.
- Economic motivation - does the partnership pay them enough to prioritize it.
- Solution fit and sales-motion alignment.

Review the drafted IPP with sales, product, and marketing before anyone targets recruitment from it.

## JVP workshop - the category kill test

A **Joint Value Proposition** workshop tests whether a category can articulate joint customer value. A category that cannot should not survive the map. Run it as a structured session, preceded by discovery interviews, and work the questions one at a time:

- What is the fundamental customer benefit of the joint solution?
- What unique, differentiated contribution does _each_ side make?
- How do the offerings combine - technical integration, service layer, or domain expertise?
- What outcome will the customer see - faster deployment, better ROI, lower risk - and can it be backed with a use case or measurable result?
- Why should a customer trust the pairing - certifications, recognition, shared clients?
- Who is the target - existing customers of either side, or a new market?
- **WIIFT - what's in it for them?** If the partner category's motivation is not obvious, its members will never prioritize the relationship.

Output: a core JVP framework adaptable across segments, commonly compressed into 25-, 50-, and 100-word narratives, tailored per partner type (co-innovating, integrating, implementing, reselling). Feed the JVP verdict straight back into the prioritization 2×2: a weak JVP with high overlap is the definition of the uphill-battle quadrant.
