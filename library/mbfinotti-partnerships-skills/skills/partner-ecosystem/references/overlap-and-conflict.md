# Account Overlap, Coverage Gaps, and Conflict Zones

Overlap data is what makes an ecosystem map real. Without it the map is a list of logos arranged aesthetically.

## Account mapping - the four overlap quadrants

Compare your account list with a partner's, cell by cell:

| Their side ↓ / your side → | Your customer                               | Your prospect                                      |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| **Their customer**         | Co-sell and expand together                 | **Partner-sourced intro - the highest-value cell** |
| **Their prospect**         | You can introduce them - builds reciprocity | Co-sell into a net-new logo                        |

Run this permissioned and filtered: pre-filter by geography, segment, and use case, and grade what each partner sees (totals only, account names, or more). Where no partner will share data yet, approximate overlap from customer interviews and tech-stack evidence, never from assumption.

Attribution note: this mechanic was popularized by **Ecosystem-Led Growth** (Bob Moore, Crossbeam, 2024), which also coined the **Ecosystem Qualified Lead (EQL)**: a lead identified through ecosystem activity, treated as a distinct, higher-converting category alongside MQL/SQL. State plainly that ELG is a category its author's company created to sell the associated tooling: useful vocabulary and a sound method, not independent research.

## Whitespace and coverage gaps

As overlap accumulates across partners, the inverse picture emerges: segments, geographies, and journey stages where no mapped partner reaches your ICP. Quantify each gap: how many target accounts, how much revenue potential, which journey stage is uncovered. This skill surfaces and sizes the gap; planning which partner category to add into it is the expansion sibling's job.

The strongest warning in the practitioner literature: teams that skip the map and jump to partner conversations pick partners based on who they know or who reached out. They sign agreements with partners who share no accounts, no ICP, and no path to joint pipeline. The map must come from customer interviews and data, not LinkedIn connections or inbound requests.

## The retroactive closed-won review

Sample recent closed-won deals and record every third party that influenced each: who advised the buyer, what integrations were required, who implemented, which marketplace transacted. This is the cheapest high-signal step in the exercise. It proves the ecosystem is already influencing revenue before any new partner is recruited, and it grounds the influence band of the map in deals rather than theory.

## Conflict zones - detect and quantify, never resolve here

Three overlap patterns to flag on any map, plus a fourth specific to consumer brands. For each, record the accounts, revenue, and territory at stake. Writing rules of engagement is `mbfinotti/partnerships-skills@partner-channel-conflict`'s job.

1. **Partner vs partner** - e.g. a high-volume discounter competing against a value-added partner that invests in pre-sales and needs higher margin. Root cause is usually structural over-recruitment: too many partners in one territory creates a zero-sum environment.
2. **Direct vs partner** - the direct rep's immediate win against the partner's need to build a sustainable business. Aggravated when partner deals and direct deals live in separate systems with no shared visibility.
3. **Multi-channel / ecosystem** - a customer finding a lower marketplace price than a reseller quoted; an affiliate driving a lead that closes through a VAR with no credit. Growing as vendors run resellers, marketplaces, direct, product-led, and referral motions simultaneously.
4. **D2C vs retail partner (B2C signature conflict)** - building direct-to-consumer weakens retail relationships; watering down direct promotions to please retail partners hurts direct sales. D2C is resource-intensive, and its payoff is first-party data, margin, and brand control.

   Levers the map should note as observed practice, not prescribe, and deliberately left unranked:

   - Exclusivity of certain ranges or editions to owned channels.
   - D2C-priority allocation of high-demand products.
   - Selective retention of key retail partners.

   Ordering these levers here would be false precision from this skill: it detects and sizes conflict without the mandate to fix it, and the ranking depends on contract terms it never reads. `mbfinotti/partnerships-skills@partner-channel-conflict` ranks them.

Cost of unmanaged conflict, with flags: research circulated by channel-management vendors puts pipeline lost to unresolved conflict at 30-40% and names conflict the top reason partners disengage (vendor-published, directional).

The capacity question, are we staffed to support this partner type at all, belongs in the prioritization score, not as an afterthought.

## Choosing the map artifact

Pick by the decision the format settles against what it costs to build: `matrix > heat map > tiered view > network diagram` on that ratio. A whiteboard or slides suffice for simple maps.

The full axis breakdown and the conditions that reorder it sit at workflow step 10, where the choice is actually made. Rows below are in that same order.

| Format                                                                    | Settles                                     | Build effort                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Matrix view - partners on a grid, e.g. market coverage × capability depth | Where do we invest?                         | Near-zero - a table, fed by the scorecard composites          |
| Heat map - colour by geography, vertical, or capability                   | Where is concentration; where are the gaps? | An hour, once one clean dimension is attached to every player |
| Tiered view - concentric circles or pyramid by strategic relevance        | Who is core vs periphery?                   | An hour, but it restates a ranking step 9 already produced    |
| Network diagram - vendor, partners, and connections between them          | Who connects to whom; where are the hubs?   | A day or more, plus layout skill; unreadable without it       |

Placement convention: your company at the centre, partners in concentric circles by strategic relevance. The circles are a map layout, not program tiers, and must not be presented as tier criteria.
