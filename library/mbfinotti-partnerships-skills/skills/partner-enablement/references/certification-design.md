# Certification Design

Whether to certify, what to certify, and what a credential must gate to be worth a partner's time. Design levers here are vendor and practitioner consensus, not a standard; the 2022-2026 structural trend is what the large vendors publish in their own program guides.

## First decision: certify at all?

Certification is not a default. It earns its cost only when at least one holds:

- Customers check partner credentials before buying (common in cloud/infrastructure ecosystems).
- The product is complex enough that an unvalidated partner damages customers and the brand.
- The program needs an objective, defensible gate for tiers, co-sell priority, or lead routing.

A young program with a handful of partners is usually better served by a capability check inside onboarding (a live pitch or demo pass) than by a formal certification apparatus.

## Individual accreditation vs organizational competency

Two different instruments - mature programs run both:

- **Individual accreditation**: a person passes an assessment. Portable (it leaves when the person does), cheap to issue, drives coverage.
- **Organizational competency/specialization**: the partner company holds a designation, typically requiring N certified individuals in named roles PLUS validated customer outcomes (references, delivered projects, sometimes third-party assessment). This is the instrument that gates tiers and co-sell eligibility.

Count organizational requirements in certified-headcount-by-role, not raw certificates. The headcount requirement is a real wall for small partners - at least one major cloud program built a separate small-business designation after partner feedback for exactly this reason.

## Assessment format: exams vs labs vs outcome validation

- **Knowledge exam**: cheapest to run, weakest signal. "A certification a partner clicks through certifies nothing" (practitioner formulation). Acceptable for entry-level product literacy only.
- **Hands-on lab / practical assessment**: validates capability - a live demo pass, a sandbox implementation, a role-played discovery call. The recommended minimum for anything that gates economics.
- **Outcome validation**: the credential (or its renewal) requires demonstrated real-world results - launched customer opportunities, delivered projects, customer references. This is where the major vendor programs converged between 2022 and 2026: away from pay-to-play and volume tiers, toward validated, outcome-gated designations. Strongest signal, highest burden; reserve for top-level designations.

## Completion economics: what gets finished vs refused

Completion follows economics, not content quality. Certifications get completed when they are:

1. Free or low-cost and short - a few hours per credential at entry level.
2. Tied to tangible economics: tier status, discount level, MDF eligibility, lead routing, co-sell priority, directory ranking. One major program's partner-matching engine explicitly favors accredited partners - a direct economic pull.
3. Linked to a real capability check, so the credential means something to customers.

They get refused when long, expensive, knowledge-only, or decoupled from margin - and when certified-headcount requirements exceed what a small partner can staff. Ask the burden question numerically in the roadmap: hours per person x people required x exam fees, against the economic payoff the partner sees.

## Recertification and credential decay

- Set an expiry per level: entry credentials commonly lapse around annually; organizational designations commonly re-validate every 2-3 years (observed vendor range).
- The decay argument (attributed to channel analyst commentary, not a study): customers check credentials and expect partners to hold the current level; a certification earned against last year's product may already be stale. Fast-moving products need faster recertification - but every cadence increase is a burden increase, so pair it with delta-training (what changed) rather than full re-examination.

## Linking certification to tiers and co-sell

Certification counts are a legitimate tier criterion - the major vendor programs all wire certified-headcount into tier attainment. If the roadmap uses certification this way, say so explicitly in the spec and hand the criteria design (thresholds, measurement windows, gameability checks) to `mbfinotti/partnerships-skills@partner-tiering`. This skill decides what the certification validates and costs; the tiering skill decides where the bar sits in the ladder.

## Negative example: the certification gauntlet

A vendor launches its program with a mandatory 90-day certification gauntlet: every partner must complete a multi-week curriculum and pass three exams before they may register a single deal. Documented outcome pattern: partners stall in the gauntlet, activation collapses, and the partners who do finish are the ones with idle bench capacity - not the ones with customers. The failure is sequencing, not rigor: certification was placed before the first deal instead of alongside or after it.

The corrected pattern deliberately avoids the gauntlet - minimum viable path to first deal registration in the first weeks, with certification as a post-first-deal milestone that unlocks economics (better margin, co-sell, leads), so the learning curve tracks the earning curve. (Pattern assembled from convergent practitioner accounts; no controlled study exists.)
