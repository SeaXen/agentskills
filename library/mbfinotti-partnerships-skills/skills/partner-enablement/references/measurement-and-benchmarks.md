# Measurement and Benchmarks

How to judge whether enablement worked. Reliability first: **no independent, non-vendor benchmark dataset exists for time-to-first-deal, activation rate, or certification completion.**

Every band below is a PRM-vendor or channel-consultancy figure with undisclosed methodology - directional rules of thumb, never measured constants. Say so whenever citing one.

## Kirkpatrick's Four Levels applied to a channel audience

Established training-evaluation methodology (Donald Kirkpatrick, 1959; "New World" update 2016). Not channel-native - this application is a practitioner adaptation, flag it as such:

| Level      | In a partner program                            | Data source               |
| ---------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| 1 Reaction | Did partners rate the training relevant         | Learning platform surveys |
| 2 Learning | Assessment pass rates, pre/post knowledge delta | Learning platform         |
| 3 Behavior | Do partner reps apply it in real deals          | Requires field evidence   |
| 4 Results  | Partner-sourced pipeline, revenue, win rate     | Requires CRM/deal data    |

- **A partner learning platform by default yields only Levels 1-2** (completion, quiz scores, engagement). Levels 3-4 need deal-registration and CRM data joined in - plan the data join before launch, not after.
- Plan backwards from Level 4: define the business result first, then the behaviors, then the training.
- Level 3 without direct observation (you cannot shadow another company's reps): self-rated application scale + verification by the partner's manager or the partner account manager + one open question naming the blocker. Start measuring within 2-4 weeks of training, not after 90 days. Self-report alone is weak evidence.
- The channel-specific caveat: partner reps sit inside another company's incentive structure the vendor does not control - a perfect curriculum still fails Level 3 if the partner's own comp plan points elsewhere. Check the economics before blaming the training.
- Phillips ROI adds a fifth, monetized level (enablement spend vs partner revenue return) - established extension, rarely worth the modeling effort below a mature program.
- 70-20-10 (experiential/social/formal learning) is a widely cited heuristic with a weak empirical base; its useful channel implication is that formal course completion measures only the smallest slice of how partner capability actually forms.

## The KPI set

Four domains; track a core of 6-10 KPIs total, not everything available:

1. **Capability**: training completion, certification coverage BY ROLE per partner, assessment pass rates, recertification currency.
2. **Engagement**: portal logins, content views/downloads, asset requests, deal-registration volume, MDF utilization.
3. **Commercial**: partner-sourced pipeline and revenue, time-to-first-deal, win rate on registered deals, active-vs-dormant partner ratio.
4. **Experience**: partner satisfaction with enablement specifically.

Rules:

- **Coverage, not counts.** Count certified individuals by role per partner - a raw certificate total hides a partner with ten sales certs and zero technical capability.
- **Maintain the certified-but-inactive list** - partners who completed training and produced no pipeline. It is a named failure mode and a coaching/outreach queue; completion above 60-70% is described as strong for partner programs, but completion proves nothing without activation.
- **The proof artifact is the trained-vs-untrained cohort comparison**: contrast enabled and un-enabled partners on partner-sourced opportunities, time-to-first-deal, and win rate. This is the one measurement that separates "enablement happened" from "enablement worked." Circulating cohort figures (certified partners earning multiples more; structured onboarding closing first deals 2-3x faster) trace to vendors' own partner surveys - self-selected and self-serving; run your own comparison instead of citing theirs.
- **Attribution breaks the moment the learning platform, portal, and CRM stop sharing partner identity.** Unify partner IDs across systems before launch; a composite partner score (weighted across capability/commercial/activity/experience) only works on top of that join.

## Benchmark bands - all PRM-vendor sourced, directional only

- Time-to-first-deal: 60-90 days for most B2B SaaS programs; under 45 described as well-optimized; trending above 90 is the commonly cited rebuild signal.
- Time to first deal _registration_: sources conflict (under 30 vs under 60 days) - define registration vs closed-won before benchmarking anything.
- Activation rate (partners with a registered deal): 30-50% cited as a target band; 90-day activation above 60% described as mature, below 30% pointing at onboarding/enablement rather than partner quality. Sources conflict; pick one definition and track the trend, not the absolute.
- Onboarding completion: 80%+ target.
- Single-vendor claims, unverified: partners registering a first deal within 30 days being 4-5x likelier to be active at 12 months; 40-60% of partners not completing onboarding within 90 days going permanently inactive. Use as motivation for sequencing, never as fact.
- Content-usage statistics in circulation (65% unused, 90% unused, etc.) all measure direct sales reps, not partners - never present one as a partner figure. Genuine partner-specific figures now exist: Gleanster research found only 52% of partner marketing development funds get claimed, meaning nearly half goes unused every year; and the "content abandonment rate" (share of partner-portal assets never downloaded, shared, or viewed by any partner) is documented at under 15% in a healthy, demand-driven program, versus past 70% where the content strategy has fractured.

## Interpreting engagement honestly

- Low portal logins usually mean access friction (passwords, slow UI, poor findability), not partner apathy - single-sign-on portals see measurably higher engagement. Fix friction before diagnosing motivation.
- Falling engagement is the earliest warning: fewer logins, slower responses, no co-selling - commercial decline follows. Watch it as a leading indicator.
- Low adoption of one specific asset is a signal about that asset: update, reposition, or retire it.
