# Needs Assessment and Collaborative Design

How channel professionals discover what partners actually need before building anything. Skipping this step is expensive: one published practitioner post-mortem documents ~18 months of enablement, recruitment, and tier structure built for a partner profile that turned out not to exist.

## The gap-analysis method

1. Interview 5-10 partners, deliberately split between active and inactive. Active partners tell you what works; inactive partners tell you why the program failed them - the more valuable half.
2. Map every enablement component to a current-state vs desired-state pair: onboarding path, product training, sales assets, demo capability, technical training, deal-registration mechanics, competitive positioning, co-marketing support.
3. Write the deliverable as a gap document: component, current state, desired state, evidence, impact, and fix effort in orders of magnitude (an hour, a week, a quarter, a standing job) - never a budget figure.
4. Rank by impact per unit of fix effort, not by impact alone, and say the ranking out loud; do not launch everything at once. Impact alone always promotes the quarter-long gaps and buries the hour-long ones that would have unblocked a deal this week. The most commonly reported gaps across practitioner sources are demo enablement (a few weeks to fix, high impact) and competitive positioning (a day or two, and it rides along with the sales play already being written). The second usually ships first even though the first ranks higher on impact. A gap whose fix exceeds this cycle's effort ceiling is deleted from the plan and named as deferred, not left in it at low priority.
5. Launch fixes with a pilot group and collect feedback before scaling.

Complement interviews with surveys (product knowledge, satisfaction, marketing support - scale cheaply, miss nuance) and partner self-assessment tools. Interviews reveal what surveys miss; use both when capacity allows.

## Partner interview questions

Adapt, don't recite. The intent behind each matters more than the wording:

- What is driving your business this year, and what role does our category play in it? (surfaces whether enablement has any economic hook at all)
- Walk me through your last deal with us - where did you get stuck, and what did you improvise? (improvised assets are your build list)
- What did you expect from onboarding that you never got?
- Which of our assets have you actually used with a customer in the last quarter? Which did you rebuild yourself, and why? (rebuilt assets signal a co-brandability or fit failure, not a distribution failure)
- Could your team demo the product today without us on the call? What breaks first?
- What would a certification need to give you - economically - for you to put two people through it?
- Who inside your company would need training that we've never addressed? (usually surfaces neglected technical and delivery roles)
- What do competitors' programs do for you that we don't?

## Listening tour and partner advisory council

- A listening tour is the interview round above run deliberately across segments (type, size, tier, geography) before any design work - discovery before design.
- A partner advisory council is a standing forum where the vendor's executives and its most strategic partners review direction. Use it to socialize the near-final roadmap before public launch, not to generate the first draft.
- The documented trap: collecting partner feedback and doing nothing with it signals that input doesn't matter - worse than never asking. Close the loop visibly: "you said X, we changed Y."
- If the user cannot run interviews, mine what already exists: joint business plans, QBR notes, the portal's asset-request log and search queries, support tickets, win/loss notes on partner deals.

## Joint business planning inputs

Joint business planning (JBP) is the field's primary structured collaboration mechanism - a genuine documented discipline (analyst-defined process stages exist), though most vendors run it inconsistently or not at all. For enablement purposes, pull these inputs from any existing JBP:

- The partner's stated business goals, resources required, obstacles, and view of their competitive environment - the listening inputs.
- Any training and certification plan already committed per partner - enablement milestones that belong in your roadmap, not a parallel one.
- Revenue targets and target accounts - they size how much enablement investment a partner justifies.

The analyst-documented first pitfall of joint planning is "no value proposition for the partner." The same rule governs enablement: the partner's business case precedes the training plan. If a JBP shows no economic reason for the partner to invest learning time, fix that with the program-design or tiering sibling skills before building curriculum.

## The internal audience

Partner enablement has two audiences, and the second is frequently missed: the vendor's own channel organization. Partner managers, channel sales, and solutions engineers must know the program, the content, and the tooling at least as well as partners do. If a partner asks for a competitive battlecard and the partner manager doesn't know it exists, that is an internal enablement gap - budget a track for it in the roadmap.

## Ownership before content

A recurring documented failure: product marketing creates content, channel ops manages the portal, and a separate team runs training - uncoordinated, producing a fragmented program. Centralize accountable ownership of the roadmap in one named person even when content creation stays distributed. In small programs (roughly under 20-30 active partners), practitioners nest enablement under partner ops rather than hiring a dedicated enablement manager - a staged-investment heuristic, not a rule.
