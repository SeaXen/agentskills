# Should you take the VP's 40%?

**Yes to the money, no to the terms as offered.** The offer has real downsides, but most can be negotiated away before you accept.

## Downsides

1. **The event's purpose shifts.** Your goal is adoption of the testing modules, so success means attendees activate them. Once sales funds 40%, the implied success metric becomes leads and meetings. Budget owners usually get their metric prioritized, even without saying so.
2. **Attendee mix skews.** "20 biggest accounts in the room" pushes invites toward buyers and executives. A hands-on day needs practitioners, because engineers are the ones who turn on a testing module. A room of VPs with no one at a keyboard fails your goal.
3. **Developer trust erodes.** A hands-on day works because engineers expect no sales pitch. If they find out their details went to sales, or an AE shows up mid-workshop, next year's registrations and candor drop.
4. **Consent and privacy risk.** These attendees are existing users who registered for a hands-on day. Handing the full list to sales is a different purpose from the one they signed up for.
   - Under GDPR or similar rules, this likely needs explicit opt-in.
   - Check your registration form's wording before promising anything. If it didn't mention sharing with sales, you can't give him the full list as-is.
5. **Meeting pressure on the day.** Intro meetings with 20 accounts mean pulling key attendees out of sessions, or promising them VIP treatment. That fragments the agenda and splits your team's attention.
6. **Agenda creep.** 40% of the cost buys informal say over content, speakers, and invitees. Expect requests for more demo time and a "customer success story" slot.
7. **Budget dependency and precedent.**
   - Next year you can't easily refuse him, and if he leaves or his budget shrinks, you lose 40% overnight.
   - Finance may read "sales covers 40%" as proof your budget can be cut further. Your 15% cut makes this a real risk.
8. **Channel conflict.** Many of the biggest accounts already have an AE or CSM. A cold "intro meeting" from the VP's team can clash with those relationships.

## Upsides

- Real money, and an executive sponsor with a stake in the event.
- Sales alignment. If the testing modules are paid add-ons, sales has a legitimate interest in who adopts them.
- Pressure to get the right accounts into the room.

## Recommended counter-offer

Ask for the funding, but redesign what he gets back:

1. **Opt-in lead list only.** Add a registration checkbox: "Share my details with the Quenlab account team." He gets everyone who ticks it. For accounts already in the CRM, the account team already has the contact, so no new sharing is needed.
2. **Give him better than a list.** Offer post-event engagement data, such as who activated or tried which testing module and where they got stuck. That is far more useful for a follow-up than a name.
3. **Replace the 20 intro meetings with an optional track.**
   - Hold a dinner or executive breakfast, outside the hands-on sessions.
   - Invite the big accounts, and let the account owner host.
   - Make meetings opt-in and book them for the day before or after.
4. **Put the terms in writing.**
   - No sales pitches in sessions.
   - No AEs in the workshop rooms.
   - You keep final say on agenda and speakers.
   - He can nominate invitees, but the invite list stays practitioner-led.
5. **Set a shared scorecard.** Primary metric: testing-module activation among attendees. Secondary: opt-in leads and meetings held. This protects your goal and gives him numbers to defend his spend.
6. **Ring-fence his money.** Have him fund specific line items, such as the executive dinner and travel for key accounts, instead of 40% of the whole. Then his money doesn't touch core programming.
7. **Cap the commitment at one year.** Don't let it become an assumed baseline. Tell your own leadership the 15% cut still stands as your base budget.

## Draft reply to the VP

> Thanks, I'd love your support on Quenlab Day. Here's how I think we both win. Attendees who opt in at registration are shared with your team, and I'll send you module-activation data after the event so your follow-ups are targeted. I'll also host an executive dinner for the largest accounts, outside the workshop sessions, with your team hosting. To keep the day trusted by engineers, no sales pitches in sessions and I keep agenda control. Could your contribution cover the dinner, travel for key accounts, and part of the venue cost? Happy to talk through it.

## Questions that would change my advice

- Are the testing modules a paid add-on? If yes, sales alignment matters more.
- What did your registration form promise about data use?
- Are many attendees in the EU or other strict-privacy regions?
- What is the dollar amount of the 40%, and does your plan survive without it next year?

If he refuses every condition and insists on the full list plus 20 meetings, I'd decline. The event's value comes from developer trust, and the money isn't worth losing it.
