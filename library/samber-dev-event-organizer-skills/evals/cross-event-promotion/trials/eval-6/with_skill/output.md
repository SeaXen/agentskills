# What to nail down before the KernelFest × ObservaCon swap goes ahead

I followed `cross-event-promotion` and its two reference files. Treat the DM as an intention, not a deal. Don't promise Metricly a booth until the first two items below are resolved.

## 1. Read the TraceHound exclusivity clause first

The "exclusive observability sponsor" clause matters. Metricly sells observability tooling, so a free Metricly booth at KernelFest is a direct category conflict with a paying gold sponsor. The skill names this failure mode: a comped booth is in-kind value that can violate a paying sponsor's exclusivity or occupy inventory they bought. Their $8,000 is at stake.

Check these points in the contract:

- **Scope of "exclusive."** It could cover booths only, or also branding, talks, mentions, and promo. Mentions and ticket-code swaps may count if the clause covers promotion.
- **Definition of the category.** Does "observability" cover Metricly's product line?
- **In-kind and comped presence.** Does it count even if Metricly pays nothing?
- **Remedies.** Look for refunds, termination, or make-good obligations.
- **Waiver.** Can TraceHound consent in writing?
- **Term.** Which editions does it cover? Does it include the next one?

Then pick one outcome:

- **Clause covers the booth:** don't give Metricly a booth. Offer a non-booth swap instead, after confirming those formats are also clear.
- **TraceHound waives in writing:** the booth can proceed.
- **Clause is ambiguous:** ask TraceHound before telling Metricly anything.

Your side of the swap (a KernelFest presence at ObservaCon) doesn't breach the clause. Still check that TraceHound doesn't expect you to avoid promoting alongside their competitor.

## 2. Turn the DM into a written agreement

"Sounds good, we'll sort the details later" is no agreement. Nothing is defined: which edition, what a booth includes, who pays.

Your event is community-run and Metricly's is company-run, which the skill calls a mixed pole. The community side moves fast and the company side can't. That means:

- Set the timeline by Metricly's approval chain, with brand, marketing, and probably legal sign-off.
- Use a signed one-pager, not a chat recap. Informal recaps don't survive a marketing-team handover.
- Confirm your contact has authority to commit Metricly. Ask who else must approve.

The agreement should cover these points:

1. **The swap:** one sentence per side on who delivers what.
2. **Deliverables and dates:** the edition, the unit (booth, flyer, stage mention), and ship dates.
3. **Minimum commitments:** the floor below which the deal wasn't honored.
4. **Attribution:** which codes or tagged links exist, who creates them, and that both sides share numbers afterward.
5. **Approvals:** logo and event-name use, and copy sign-off.
6. **Costs:** shipping, furniture, power, badges, and travel. Name every one so "free" doesn't silently accrete costs.
7. **Exit clause:** either side can withdraw if the other cancels, changes materially, or has a conduct incident.
8. **Recur review:** a named date after both editions to exchange numbers and decide recur, renegotiate, or drop.

## 3. Resolve the staffing gap

Your crew can't travel, so a booth at ObservaCon doesn't work as agreed. An unstaffed booth looks worse than a flyer stack. Left alone, the deal is also one-sided: Metricly staffs a booth at your event while you send nobody.

The skill's fix is to trade the booth down. Same deal shape, no travel:

- **Flyer or sticker exchange:** Metricly places your material on their tables.
- **Stage or keynote mention:** a short slot at ObservaCon, or a pre-recorded video.
- **Remote volunteer or local proxy:** someone near their venue, if you know one.

Then handle the asymmetry openly, as the skill's parity heuristic says. If Metricly gets a staffed booth and you get flyers, the larger side may resent it. Offer something in units to balance it, such as extra newsletter mentions, a richer placement, or a longer discount window.

Also confirm that your own crew can host a Metricly booth. A table, power, badges, and a point of contact are all volunteer hours. If the crew is stretched, even hosting may be too much.

## 4. Sort out the remaining items

- **Audience fit.** Linux kernel developers and observability buyers may overlap, but check. Confirm the two events are complements, not substitutes, before investing.
- **Size parity.** Get ObservaCon's real attendance. You're at 600 and volunteer-run. If they're much larger, name the imbalance in the proposal.
- **Brand and values.** Read Metricly's and ObservaCon's code of conduct, past incident handling, and sponsor roster. A volunteer community may react badly to vendor sales tactics at a booth, so agree booth rules such as no cold outreach to attendees.
- **Attendee data.** Ask whether the booth does badge scanning or lead capture. If so, decide what consent attendees give and who owns the data.
- **Engagement quality.** You can't verify it from outside, so ask Metricly for real newsletter open rates and past swap results.
- **Dates and timeline.** A booth swap needs lead time. Confirm both editions' dates and whether each falls within the other's planning window.
- **Attribution.** Set up per-partner discount codes, tagged links, and a source field on registration before launch. Otherwise you can't honestly evaluate the swap afterward.
- **Ticket-code swap.** This only works if both sides have discountable inventory, and a code deeper than your public early-bird discount undercuts your own pricing.

## 5. Recommended shape

Based on your answers so far (volunteer crew, no travel, an exclusivity clause), I'd restructure the deal like this:

1. **Cross-list both events on community calendars now.** It's free, needs no consent, and doesn't touch the TraceHound clause.
2. **Offer a mention or ticket-code swap** in place of the booth, but only after checking whether the clause covers promotion.
3. **Drop the booth from the deal** unless TraceHound waives in writing and you can staff it.
4. **Put it in writing** with Metricly's marketing lead, once her side confirms approval.

## 6. Measurement

No benchmark exists for event-to-event swaps, so every target you set is self-set. Report only what you measure and mark the rest "unmeasured". A successful first swap means attribution worked, both sides delivered what the agreement said, you have your own baseline numbers, and both sides want to repeat it.

## What to do first

1. Read the TraceHound clause.
2. If it covers the booth or promotion, decide whether to ask for a waiver or drop the booth.
3. Reply to the marketing lead: confirm interest, say the details need a short written agreement, and propose the traded-down format. Don't commit the booth until step 2 is settled.
4. Gather the facts you still need: ObservaCon's attendance, edition dates, and her approval chain.

## Questions I didn't ask

The skill runs a one-question-at-a-time interview, and I couldn't ask it. These answers would change the recommendation:

- **Deadline:** when must the promotion land? A tight deadline favors the mention swap.
- **Attribution:** can each side issue codes and read redemptions?
- **Recurrence:** is this a one-off or a relationship you want to repeat? A repeat deal makes speaker-pipeline sharing more attractive.
- **Existing assets:** do you already have a strong newsletter or social channel to trade?
