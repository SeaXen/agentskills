## Short answer

An internal transfer is **not an established route into tech-event roles**. The skill's research marks it as a hypothesis. It is strongly evidenced as the dominant path in DevRel, but nobody has separately sourced it for tech-event roles.

The best-documented route is volunteer-to-paid. That evidence comes from the general events industry, not tech specifically. You are already past that stage: you've done DevRel and community work at Ferrostack for three years, and the owner of FerroConf is leaving.

So the answer is not "transfer or something else." Make the internal ask now, because the vacancy is the window. Build a fallback in parallel so you don't depend on one answer.

## Where you stand

- **Operating context:** in-house at a tech company running its own conference. This is the best-sourced context for pay (tech premium). It is also the one where titles are least consistent and the role usually blends producer and manager work.
- **Role shape:** most likely a manager-level hybrid owning production, sponsors, content and stakeholders. If your DevRel work is mostly community, you may also fit the community-manager-shaped end.
- **Ladder:** no universal title ladder exists. Three sourced orderings disagree on whether Producer sits above or below Manager.
  - The closest anchor is a UK conference-recruiter ladder: Junior Producer, Producer, Senior Producer, Conference Manager, Director. It is a general B2B ladder, not tech-native.
  - The recruiter source says people can enter above junior level when they bring specialist topic knowledge. Three years of developer credibility counts.
  - If you lean community-shaped, CMX's ladder applies instead: Community Manager (1-6 yrs), then Director of Community.
- **Treat the title as negotiable.** Don't fight for "Producer" versus "Manager." Fight for scope: budget, program, sponsors, final say.

## Plan

**1. This week: ask before they backfill.** Go to the hiring manager and the exec sponsor of FerroConf.
- Say you want to own it and propose a transition overlap with the departing manager.
- Frame it as a hypothesis you're testing, not an entitlement.
- The risk is that the company posts an external job first.

**2. Get a handover from the outgoing owner.** Document the vendor contracts, venue timeline, sponsor pipeline and budget history. This shows readiness and also works as a trial run of the job.

**3. Build your evidence pack (1-2 weeks).** Interviewers in this field start with a portfolio review, then ask for metrics. Pull together:
- the FerroConf work you've already touched, with your named role and the scale (1,200 attendees);
- attendee satisfaction, on-budget delivery and repeat-attendee rate, if you can get them;
- any testimonials from speakers, sponsors or colleagues.

**4. Bank five stories**, each in a 2-minute and a 30-second version:
- an event you ran end to end, with scale and one metric;
- a live problem you fixed in real time (venue, vendor no-show, speaker drop-out);
- a difficult stakeholder or vendor;
- a budget trade-off you made;
- if community-shaped, a conflict between what the community wanted and what leadership wanted. Prepare a concrete example, not a diplomatic non-answer.

**5. Expect a work sample.** For a senior or community-shaped role, the documented version is a paid mini-audit of an existing community. For you, a one-page "FerroConf next edition" plan covering risks, budget and the first 90 days would serve the same purpose. Offering it unprompted helps.

**6. Run diligence on the role itself.**
- Does the title match the day-to-day?
- Does one person own sponsorship, content and production, or is the scope split?
- What are the team size and budget for the next edition?
- What does success look like in year one, and who decides?
- Which team does the role report into? Today it sits in marketing, and yours is DevRel.

**7. Fallback, in parallel.** If the answer is no or "we'll post externally":
- Apply for the posted role anyway as an internal candidate.
- Volunteer on the program or organizing team of an external community conference to build a record beyond Ferrostack.
- Look at agencies, where the ladder is the clearest documented one and freelance work is common, though this hasn't been tested for your situation.

## Compensation

No tech-conference-specific salary survey exists, so these are three separate figures. Do not read them as one number.

- **PCMA Convene 2025** (275 people, mostly North America, all industries): Coordinator $77,321, Manager $95,077, Director $133,147, Executive/VP $176,217.
- **US BLS** (all levels and industries): median $59,440-$101,310.
- **Glassdoor:** Information Technology is the highest-paying industry for Event Manager titles. Event Marketing Manager reaches a median total pay of $175,690 in IT.

Check how your current DevRel band compares before you negotiate. A move to an events title could lower or raise it.

## Certifications

**Skip CMP by default.** There's no evidence tech or developer conferences require or mention it. Tech-event hiring leans on a record of events you've actually run. CMP only makes sense if you later target the corporate-meetings side.

## What the evidence doesn't cover

- The internal-transfer route is untested for tech events, and I won't claim it is standard.
- Standing for-profit conference businesses are unsourced. If you ever move to one, treat any agency-like assumptions as unverified.
- The "27% better odds with volunteer experience" figure comes from a survey whose method couldn't be confirmed. Treat it as directional.

## Three questions that would sharpen this

1. **Deadline:** how long until the owner leaves, and has the company said whether it will backfill externally?
2. **One-off or durable:** do you want this one role, or a long-term career in events? Long-term argues for building the external track record sooner.
3. **Effort:** how many hours a week can you give to the handover and prep, and would you accept running FerroConf alongside your current duties for a while?
