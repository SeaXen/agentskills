## Part 1: Is it a good idea?

Four years of organizing, 300 to 2,600 attendees, and running Discord and social is real evidence. It's also an insider's advantage, because the board already trusts you. Three things work against you.

**1. A first paid hire changes the organization.**
- The first hire is a permanent cost in a $280k budget, which likely swings with sponsorship.
- Boards usually pick the role by the biggest bottleneck: sponsorship revenue, operations and logistics, or finance and admin. Community is rarely the first choice.
- If your Discord and social work is what they value, they may still see it as work volunteers can do. Your pitch has to show why paying for it changes outcomes.

**2. Volunteer to employee is a relationship risk.**
- Your peers stay unpaid while you get paid. Resentment is common and you need a plan for it.
- You'd have a boss (the board), an employment contract, and possibly a conflict of interest.
- Check whether you sit on the board or vote on this decision. If so, recuse yourself from the hiring discussion.

**3. "Community Manager" may be the wrong title.**
- Community work for a yearly conference is seasonal. Attention peaks around the event.
- A year-round role often needs to cover sponsor relations, speaker and CFP pipeline, volunteer coordination, and communications.
- A broader title like **Community & Programs Manager** or **Events & Community Lead** is easier to justify for 12 months a year, and it gives you more room to grow. You'd run the same Discord and socials inside a bigger remit.

### How to pitch it

1. **Ask for a 30-minute chat with the board chair or treasurer first.** Don't send a formal proposal cold. Ask what problem they hope a hire solves.
2. **Bring one page with evidence, not a title request.**
   - Numbers you can verify: Discord members and active share, social reach, volunteer retention, attendee growth.
   - Which of these you drove versus the team.
   - A link between community work and revenue: sponsor leads, ticket conversion, repeat attendees, volunteer pipeline.
3. **Propose the smallest version that tests the hire.** For example, a 6 to 12 month contract, or a 20 to 24 hour per week role, with a defined review. Boards say yes to reversible commitments.
4. **Name the outcomes you'd own for the year.** Examples: year-round engagement between events, sponsor-facing community reporting, a volunteer program, a stronger CFP pipeline. Put targets and a review date on each one.
5. **Say what stays volunteer.** Show you aren't turning the whole organization into paid labor.
6. **Offer a funding argument.** The role pays for itself if it raises sponsorship or ticket revenue. Estimate by how much, and say how you'd measure it.

### Questions to put to the board

- What's the single biggest bottleneck that a hire would fix?
- Is there a funding source for a salary beyond one year's budget?
- Who would I report to, and who evaluates me?
- Do I stay a volunteer in any other role, and is that allowed?
- Is it a contract or an employee role, and in which jurisdiction (this changes taxes, benefits and legal duties)?

## Part 2: Pay

**I can't give you a market number.** Salary-survey figures for nonprofit community roles vary a lot by country, city, and whether the role is contract or employee. Pull current figures yourself, and keep each number's source, date, geography and seniority level.

**Where to look (about 1 hour):**
1. Nonprofit salary or Form 990 data. In the US, find comparable organizations' filings. Look for similar-size nonprofit events and developer communities.
2. Peers. Ask other conference or community organizers what their first paid staff earned. Community-manager Slack and Discord groups often share this.
3. Crowdsourced levelling and salary sites. Treat them as wide directional ranges, because they mix in adjacent roles.
4. Local government or labor-board wage data for "community manager" and "event coordinator" in your city.

**What I can compute from your numbers:**
- Every $10k of salary is about 3.6% of a $280k budget.
- Loaded cost (payroll tax, insurance, benefits, software) typically adds a meaningful share on top of salary. Ask your accountant for the exact figure.
- If the board has a ceiling, it's likely a total cost, not a salary. Ask what that total is, then work backward.

**What to expect, qualitatively:**
- Nonprofit pay for a first hire usually sits below the same role at a for-profit company. Your counter is scope, title, flexibility and mission, not matching tech salaries.
- A part-time or contract start is common for a first hire.
- Boards often anchor on what they can afford, not on market rate. Show the market range, then offer a phased path: a lower starting figure with a dated review and a raise if you hit the agreed targets.

**Negotiate beyond salary:**
- Title and written scope
- Hours and flexibility, including a clear policy for overtime during the event
- Paid time off, especially after the event
- Conference and travel budget
- Right to publish and speak about your work
- Review date with a raise tied to agreed outcomes
