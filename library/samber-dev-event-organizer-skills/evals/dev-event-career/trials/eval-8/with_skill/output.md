## What to change in your portfolio

A PDF of photos plus a vendor list shows that events happened. Interviewers will look for evidence of style, scale and quality, client testimonials, and then hard metrics. Your current portfolio covers only the first.

1. **Turn each photo into a case study.** Use one page per event, with this structure:
   - Event name, format, attendee count, and number of days or tracks.
   - Your exact role and what you owned.
   - Budget band, if you can disclose it.
   - One problem that came up live and how you handled it.
   - One outcome metric.
2. **Add the metrics interviewers ask for.** Include attendee satisfaction scores, percentage of events delivered on budget, and repeat-attendee rate. If you don't have them, reconstruct what you can from post-event surveys, finance sign-offs and attendance records. Say plainly which numbers are estimates.
3. **Add testimonials and references.** Get one or two short quotes from internal stakeholders or senior vendors. Line up two or three people who will take a live reference call. Interviewers weight live calls above written recommendations.
4. **Replace the vendor list with a vendor narrative.** A bare list shows contacts, not judgment. For three or four vendors, state what you sourced, how you negotiated, and what you did when something failed.
5. **Make the bank background an asset.** Say what internal bank events demand: compliance, executive stakeholders, tight risk tolerance and zero-failure delivery. Then show the gap you're closing for tech conferences, such as speaker management, sponsor-facing delivery and developer audiences. If you've run anything with a tech audience, put it first.
6. **Show both halves of the senior profile.** The recruiter source describes a "duality." One half is research and detail: run-of-show, budgets and contingency plans. The other half is networking and strategy: senior stakeholders, sponsors and speakers. A portfolio that shows only one half reads as under-developed for senior rungs.
7. **Mind confidentiality.** Redact names, client details and internal figures that the bank wouldn't want shared. This is also a general rule for anything you publish.

## What the interviews will probably look like

This is assembled from general event-industry hiring guides, so use it as a rehearsal frame rather than a prediction.

1. **Portfolio and past-events walkthrough.** This is the first step in every source. Expect "walk me through your most complex event." Prepare two lengths for each story: a two-minute version and a thirty-second one.
2. **Metrics request.** Be ready for satisfaction scores, on-budget rate and repeat-attendee rate.
3. **Scenario questions.** Typical examples are a last-minute venue change, a vendor no-show and a weather contingency. These test real-time troubleshooting. Answer with a real story and your decision logic, not a memorized process.
4. **Budget management.** Prepare one budget you kept on track and one trade-off you made to stay within it.
5. **Client and vendor relations.** Expect a question about handling a difficult stakeholder. At an agency, "client" means an external client with their own agenda, which is different from an internal sponsor at a bank. Prepare a story that addresses that.
6. **Live reference calls.** Brief your referees.

**If Kestrel's role leans toward community or developer relations**, expect more:
- **Five competencies:** growth mindset, data-driven approach, empathy, leadership and communication. A pointed leadership question is "if the community wants one thing and your boss wants something else, what would you do?" Prepare a real conflict, not a diplomatic non-answer.
- **A work sample:** a paid mini-audit of an existing community for senior candidates, or a sample-thread-response exercise for junior ones. A sample task is a plausible variant for a producer role, but the skill found no source that documents one.

## Prep plan for the next two weeks

1. **Days 1–3:** Collect the metrics, testimonials and referee commitments. Choose the three to five events for case studies.
2. **Days 4–7:** Build the case-study PDF and the vendor narrative. Draft the five-story bank: an event run end to end, a live problem, a difficult stakeholder, a budget, and (if relevant) a conflict between community and leadership.
3. **Days 8–11:** Research Kestrel Live and rehearse out loud. Study their past conferences, clients and team structure.
4. **Days 12–14:** Run a mock interview, finalize the portfolio, and prepare your diligence questions.

**Questions to ask Kestrel**
- What operating context and team structure does this role sit in?
- What does one person own, and what is shared with account management?
- What does success look like in the first year, and who decides?
- What are the team size and budget for the next edition?
