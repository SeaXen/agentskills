## Why one number is hard

The two sites you saw are measuring different jobs:

- **$85k** is usually a national or all-industry figure, or an events coordinator or specialist. It often includes low-cost metros.
- **$175k** is usually a senior manager or director at a big-tech or SF company, or total comp that includes bonus and equity.

Neither describes a NYC fintech hire who owns a flagship conference.

## Where this role lands

| Level | NYC base range |
|---|---|
| Event Marketing Manager, generalist (trade shows, field events) | $100k–$135k |
| Senior Event Marketing Manager, owns a flagship or user conference | $130k–$165k |
| Director, Events | $170k–$220k |

Your role is the middle of the table pushed toward the second row:

- **It's a first-time conference.** One person builds the whole thing: venue, speakers, sponsors, agenda, registration, and the post-event pipeline.
- **1,200 attendees is a real production.** It's well beyond a booth program.
- **It's fintech in New York.** That adds roughly 5–10% over general tech.
- **It's one in-house owner.** If there's no team under them, pay for senior scope.

That puts the base at about $120k–$155k, with a midpoint of about $135k. Add a 10–15% target bonus tied to attendance, pipeline, and NPS. Offer total cash of roughly $150k–$175k, which may be where the $175k figure comes from.

## Two things to settle before Friday

1. **NYC pay transparency law.** NYC requires a good-faith salary range in the posting. HR can't use a single number anyway. Post $120k–$155k and use $135k internally as the target.
2. **Title and level.** If the hire is solo with only an agency for support, title it "Senior Event Marketing Manager" and pay toward $145k–$155k. If they'll have a coordinator or production partner, keep "Manager" and pay $120k–$140k. A Manager title with Senior scope loses candidates to better offers.

## If HR pushes back

Event marketers with real conference experience are scarce, and a first-year flagship is a high-visibility, high-risk job. Underpaying here usually costs more in agency fees and a failed event than the salary difference.
