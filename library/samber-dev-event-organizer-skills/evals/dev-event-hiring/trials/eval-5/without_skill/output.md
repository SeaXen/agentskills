# Beaconforge: Developer Community Manager final round

**Do this first:** drop the "audit our Discord and send a plan before the final round" exercise. Details are in section 1. Section 3 replaces it.

Assumptions (veto any of them):
- She's the first and only community hire.
- The role is funded by developer adoption.
- Product-led motion.
- She reports to someone in marketing, DevRel, or product.
- She doesn't code deeply but must be credible with engineers.

## 1. Why the audit-and-plan exercise fails

- ⚠️ **It's unpaid spec work.** She has a public track record, which is already evidence. A senior finalist with other offers is the most likely person to decline or withdraw. Those who comply are often the ones with the fewest options.
- ⚠️ **It tests the wrong thing.** An outside audit of a Discord and a meetup program she can't see inside is guesswork. She has no attendance data, retention, support load, budget, or political context. You'd be grading polish, and a deck can be written by someone else.
- ⚠️ **It's hard to score fairly.** With no fixed rubric, the plan gets judged by taste and by how much you like her ideas.
- ✅ **What you actually want to learn:** how she diagnoses with partial data, what she prioritizes as a team of one, how she handles a hard community moment, and how she reasons about metrics. A live session tests all four.

## 2. Final-round loop

About 4 hours of her time, one day, remote or on-site.

| # | Block | Length | Who | Tests |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Working session (section 3) | 90 min | Hiring manager + 1 engineer | Diagnosis, prioritization, program design |
| 2 | Community judgment and moderation | 45 min | Hiring manager + support/product lead | Judgment, health, conflict |
| 3 | Measurement and business case | 45 min | Hiring manager + founder/exec | Metrics, tradeoffs |
| 4 | Engineer conversation | 30 min | 2 engineers | Technical credibility, empathy for devs |
| 5 | Founder/exec round | 30 min | CEO or CTO | Opinion round, scope, mutual fit |

Rules:
- Every interviewer asks from the question bank in section 4 and covers the same competencies for every candidate.
- Everyone scores alone in writing before any debrief (section 5).

## 3. The exercise: paid working session

**Format**
- 90 minutes, live.
- She gets a 1-page brief 48 hours ahead and is told to spend no more than 30 minutes reading it.
- **Pay her.** Pay a flat fee for the session and say so in the invite. Calibrate to a half-day consulting rate (assumption: roughly $400–700; pick your own number). If you truly can't pay, cut it to 60 minutes with no pre-read, and tell her it's unpaid and short.

**The brief (you prepare it once and reuse it)**
- Discord: member count, weekly active members, messages per week, the last 8 weeks of channel activity by channel, the share of questions answered within 24h, who answers (staff or community), 3 anonymized real threads (one great, one unanswered, one heated).
- Meetups: last 6 events per city with RSVPs, attendance, venue and cost, speaker source, and the number of repeat attendees if you have it.
- Community day: last year's attendance, budget, and what went wrong.
- Constraint: one person, one budget figure, a named year-one goal (e.g. "adoption signal X").
- Anonymize everything. Strip customer and employee names. Don't share anything confidential.

**The session**

| Min | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0–10 | She asks you questions. Note which ones she asks. |
| 10–35 | She walks through her diagnosis from the data and states what's missing. |
| 35–65 | Prioritization: "You can do 3 things in your first 90 days. Which, and what do you refuse?" |
| 65–80 | Curveball: "The unanswered thread is from a paying customer. Your engineer says it's a docs problem. The heated thread has a long-time member publicly attacking a staff member. Handle both." |
| 80–90 | Her questions to you. |

**Scoring rubric for the session (1–4 each, feeds the scorecard)**
- Asked about goals and constraints before prescribing: 4 = asks about the funnel, owner, and success definition; 1 = jumps to a ready-made playbook.
- Diagnosis is evidence-based: 4 = ties each claim to a number in the brief and flags missing data; 1 = generic best practices.
- Prioritization: 4 = a clear top three with explicit "not now" items and reasons; 1 = a long list.
- Program design across three cities: 4 = proposes distinct hosts/champions, a repeatable format, and a cost model; 1 = assumes she'll run everything personally.
- Curveball: 4 = acts on the customer first, handles the member with a private-first approach and a documented rule, loops in engineering; 1 = public shaming, ignoring it, or deferring to someone else.

**Negative signals** (name them in the scorecard when seen):
- Reports follower counts or total members as proof of success.
- Proposes programs with no owner or no metric.
- Designs her plan around her personally attending every event.
- No view on moderation.
- Can't name a developer she helped.

## 4. Question bank

Ask the same questions of every finalist. Use follow-ups to dig for evidence ("what exactly did you do, what was the number, what would you change").

**A. Program design and operations (meetups in 3 cities, community day)**
1. Tell me about a meetup series you grew or rebuilt. What was attendance at the start and the end, and what drove the change?
2. You can't be in three cities every month. How do you run this? (Look for local hosts, a playbook, venue partnerships, and cost per attendee.)
3. A sponsor pulls out six weeks before the community day. Walk me through the next 72 hours.

**B. Community health and moderation (Discord)**
1. Describe the worst moderation incident you handled. What did you decide, what did you get wrong?
2. How do you decide when a question goes unanswered for 3 days? Who owns it?
3. How do you bring in volunteer moderators or champions, and how do you keep them from burning out?

**C. Measurement and business judgment**
1. What would you report to our CEO after 90 days, and what would you refuse to report?
2. Tell me about a metric you used that turned out to be misleading. What did you replace it with?
3. How would you connect meetup or Discord activity to product adoption without overclaiming?

**D. Developer credibility and technical fluency**
1. Pick a tool you've used recently and explain what you found frustrating. (Engineers listen for specifics.)
2. How do you take a recurring community question and get it fixed in docs or product? Give an example with a result.
3. Our product is a dev tool. What would you do in your first week to understand it as a developer would?

**E. Scope, self-direction, prioritization**
1. You're one person with three cities, a Discord, and an annual event. What do you stop doing in month two?
2. Tell me about a time you pushed back on leadership about the scope of a community project.

**F. Collaboration and communication**
1. How have you worked with engineering and support when community feedback conflicted with the roadmap?
2. Show me an update you wrote for non-community colleagues. What would you change?

**G. Opinion round (founder/exec)**
1. What's wrong with how most dev-tool companies do community?
2. When should a company not invest in community?
3. Where do you disagree with the conventional wisdom on community metrics?

**Reference-check questions (ask her former manager and one peer)**
- What would you hand her tomorrow, and what would you not?
- How did she handle a community crisis?
- What was her measurable impact? Can you point to it?
- Would you rehire her? What do you need to know to manage her well?

## 5. Scorecard

**Mission:** Grow developer adoption of Beaconforge by running a healthy Discord, a repeatable three-city meetup program, and an annual community day.

**Year-one outcomes** (calibrate each to your baseline before posting; the numbers below are placeholders):
1. Discord: median time-to-first-answer under X hours and Y% of questions answered within 24h.
2. Discord: weekly active members up Z% with at least N non-staff members regularly answering.
3. Meetups: a repeatable format running monthly in all three cities, each with a local host or champion, and cost per attendee under $X.
4. Meetups: repeat-attendee rate above X%.
5. Community day: delivered on budget, attendance of N, and a post-event survey above X.
6. Feedback loop: at least N community-sourced docs or product fixes shipped, each traced to a thread.
7. Reporting: a quarterly community report tied to adoption signals, no vanity metrics.

**Competencies and weights**

| Competency | Weight | Main source |
|---|---|---|
| Program design and ops | 25% | Section 3, blocks 1, 5, bank A |
| Community health and moderation | 20% | Block 2, bank B |
| Measurement and business judgment | 20% | Block 3, bank C |
| Developer credibility | 15% | Block 4, bank D |
| Scope and self-direction | 10% | Blocks 1, 5, bank E |
| Collaboration and communication | 10% | All blocks, bank F |

**Anchors (1–4)**
- **1 – No evidence or red flag.** Generic answers, no results, or a negative signal from section 3.
- **2 – Partial.** Understands the concept, thin real experience, results vague.
- **3 – Strong.** Specific examples, numbers, clear reasoning, owns mistakes.
- **4 – Exceptional.** Has done it at your scale or harder, reasons from first principles, and teaches you something.

**Mechanics**
1. Each interviewer writes scores plus one evidence line per competency before the debrief. No discussion first.
2. Compute the weighted total mechanically: sum of (score × weight) ÷ 4.
3. Suggested bar (my working assumption): 75% or above to hire, and no 1 on moderation or measurement.
4. Debrief only on disagreements of 2 or more points. Do not re-vote on the total.
5. Reference checks can lower a score only with a concrete, documented reason.

## 6. If she falls through: where to find more

Use two profiles. Decide which you want, because the sourcing differs:
- **A. Professional community manager** from B2B SaaS or dev-tools, strong on ops, metrics, moderation.
- **B. Engineer-turned-community person** who ran a great meetup or OSS community, strong on credibility, weaker on ops and reporting.

Your role (three cities plus Discord plus event) favors A with some B credibility, or B with a strong ops partner.

**Where to look (highest yield first)**
1. **Organizers of the best dev meetups in your three cities.** Search Meetup.com and Luma for the busiest tech groups. They're proven, local, and already do the job unpaid.
2. **Your own community.** Who answers questions in your Discord, or in competitors' Discords? Top volunteer moderators and answerers often want this job.
3. **Referrals from adjacent dev-tool companies.** Ask their heads of community or DevRel who they'd hire or who they lost. Community managers know each other.
4. **Laid-off or between-role community people** from dev-tool companies. Check LinkedIn and the people-moves posts in community Slack groups.
5. **Hackathon and conference organizers** (student hackathon networks, local conference teams, volunteer leads).
6. **Community-professional networks and job boards.** Examples to check (I haven't verified current activity or posting rules): CMX, Community Club, DevRel Collective, Developer Marketing Alliance, and the DevRelCon and local conference communities.
7. **Maintainers of mid-size OSS projects** with healthy contributor communities, for profile B.

**How to approach**
- Lead with the role's outcomes and your budget and reporting line, not "exciting opportunity".
- State the compensation range up front. Check your hiring jurisdiction's pay-transparency rules before posting.
- Post the scorecard outcomes. Senior candidates screen out postings that list tactics with no goals or ask one person to cover everything.
