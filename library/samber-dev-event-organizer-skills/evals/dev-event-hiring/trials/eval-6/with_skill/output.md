## 2. Interview loop

The skill states plainly that no documented tech-conference-specific loop exists. This is the general event-planner sequence, adjusted for your context.

1. **Portfolio and past-events review.** Look for events of comparable scale and quality. Ask for the metrics they had.
2. **Metrics request.** Ask for attendee satisfaction, on-budget delivery rate, repeat-attendee rate, sponsor renewal and margin. Score whether they volunteer these unprompted. In a business where the event is the product, this stage weighs most.
3. **Scenario questions, scored live.** Use a venue change eight weeks out, a headline speaker or major sponsor pulling out, and ticket sales running 30% behind pacing at T-10 weeks. Score real-time troubleshooting, not memorized process.
4. **Budget and P&L questions.** Ask how they cut costs without harming the attendee experience, and how they have handled attrition and force-majeure clauses.
5. **Sponsor and vendor relations.** Ask how they handled a difficult sponsor or vendor. Ask what they would do to keep a sponsor who is unhappy with leads.
6. **Live reference calls, not written recommendations.** Include a former sponsor or vendor, not just a boss.

**Work sample.** Run a scenario walkthrough, scored live. Don't assign a mock full-event plan, because that is unpaid client-style work and the skill bans it. If you want a take-home, make it a bounded, paid review of one of your own past editions.

**Scoring.** Each interviewer writes an evidence note independently before the debrief. Then combine the scores mechanically against the weights above.

## How your bar should differ from a normal in-house events hire

The skill's comparison runs between agency, in-house and non-profit roles, with your context unsourced. These are the differences the structure of your business suggests. They are hypotheses.

| Dimension | In-house at a tech company | Orbit Row Media |
|---|---|---|
| What the conference is | A marketing or community expense. Value is measured in pipeline, brand and goodwill. | The product. A bad edition hits revenue directly. |
| Primary accountability | Cross-functional alignment with sales, marketing and product | Conference P&L, ticket sales and sponsor renewal |
| Where the experience should come from | A tech brand, cross-functional collaboration, internal stakeholders | Running paid events: pricing, sponsorship packages, attendance forecasting |
| Reporting line | Pulled into sales, marketing and product alignment | Closer to a general manager of a business unit |
| Cost of failure | Reputational | Direct loss of revenue and sponsor trust |
| Pay premium | Tech-industry premium is well sourced | Not sourced. Don't assume a tech premium. |

Practical consequences for your bar:
- **Weight commercial ownership above cross-functional fluency.** An in-house hire is screened for working across departments. You need someone who has owned a budget and a revenue line.
- **Screen for sponsor renewal, not only closing.** Renewal drives margin across three recurring editions.
- **Require past comparable events with numbers.** An in-house candidate can get by with attendee-satisfaction stories. For you, ask for margin and repeat-attendee data.
- **Don't over-index on a tech-company events pedigree.** Corporate-conference experience does not show the candidate can sell tickets or sponsorships.
- **Test developer-audience credibility separately.** Developer attendees punish weak programs, so give it its own weight (10% above).
