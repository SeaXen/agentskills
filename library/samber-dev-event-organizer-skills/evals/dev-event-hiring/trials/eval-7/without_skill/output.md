## Prep plan (Thursday is 3 days out)

**1. Today, about 90 minutes: build the portfolio.**
- Pick 3 events with different shapes. For example: one large conference, one small developer dinner or workshop, one that went wrong and was recovered.
- For each, write one page: goal, your exact role, budget, team size, result in numbers, what you would change.
- Lead with outcome, not activity. "Ran 40 events" is activity. "Cut cost per qualified attendee 30% by moving to regional meetups" is outcome.
- Give every claim a link where one exists: recap post, deck, photos, talk video. A published artifact beats a claim.

**2. Tomorrow, about 2 hours: rehearse the scenario exercise.**
Expect something like "Plan a 500-person developer conference in 12 weeks with $X" or "A keynote speaker cancels 48 hours out." Use one fixed structure every time:
1. Ask clarifying questions first: goal, audience, budget, success metric, who decides.
2. State assumptions out loud.
3. Give the critical path: venue, speakers and CFP, sponsors, registration, comms, run-of-show.
4. Name the top 3 risks and a mitigation for each.
5. Define how you measure success and who owns each number.

Do 2 timed dry runs, 30 minutes each, out loud. Add one crisis drill: speaker no-show, AV failure, sponsor pulls out, venue overbooked. Stay calm, triage, communicate, then recover.

**3. Day 3, about 1 hour: research the company.**
- Read their past events and their developer community: what they run, what attendees said, what looked weak.
- Prepare 2 concrete observations and 1 improvement idea. Developer audiences punish corporate-feeling events, so show you understand that.
- Know the audience: developers distrust sales pitches. Know how you would balance content quality, sponsor needs and community trust.

**4. Day 3 evening, about 30 minutes: prepare questions to ask them.**
- What does success look like at 12 months, and who measures it?
- Who does this role report to: marketing, DevRel or community?
- What is the event budget, and what is the headcount plan?
- Which events will I own end to end?
- What level does "Senior" map to on your ladder?

The reporting line changes the job more than the salary does. Ask it outright.

**5. Thursday morning.**
Review your 3 one-pagers. Have your stories ready in short and long versions, using STAR (situation, task, action, result).

## Portfolio review: what they will check

- You can show outcomes, not just activities.
- Your role in each event is clear: owner or contributor.
- You handled failure and show what you learned.
- Developer-audience fit is visible.
- You show cross-team work with DevRel, product, sales and legal.

**Do not** build new unpaid spec work for them (a full plan for their next event, for example). Offer a live working session instead. Your public portfolio already answers the question.

## Salary

You are in the final round, so an offer is likely. Do this before Thursday:

1. **Get the range.** Ask the recruiter now, before the interview: "What is the budgeted range for this role?" In many regions the posting must already include it. Check there first.
2. **Pull current data.** Check Levels.fyi, Glassdoor, Blind, and peers in event and DevRel communities for "Senior Event Producer" or "Senior Events Manager" at similar developer-tooling companies. Record source, date, location and level for each number. A blended average mixes different levels and is not a useful anchor.
3. **Set three numbers.** Target, walk-away floor, and an anchor about 10–15% above target. Companies of this size usually have a band, and your anchor should sit near the top of it.
4. **Negotiate the package, not only base.** Base, bonus, equity, event and travel budget, headcount, title level, remote or hybrid terms, and publishing or speaking rights.
5. **Do not name a number first** if you can avoid it. If pressed: "Based on the scope and market data I've seen, I'm looking at X to Y. What's the budgeted range?"
6. **Counter in writing** after the verbal offer. Ask for 24–48 hours to review.

Large developer-tooling companies often level roles on an engineering-style ladder. Ask what level this role maps to. Level changes pay more than haggling within a level does.
