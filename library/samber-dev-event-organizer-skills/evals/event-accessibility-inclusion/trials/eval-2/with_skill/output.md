**Short answer: no, captioning isn't covered.** Your AV setup is excellent, but a capable crew isn't a captioning provision until someone states it, budgets it and publishes what it covers. Capture and livestream don't help a deaf or hard-of-hearing attendee in Room 3 on Day 1. The skill treats "production capability = coverage" as a failure mode, not a rung.

Your plan, cleaning up auto-captions after the event, is a real rung: **post-event corrected captions**. It's the right floor for the recordings. It helps nobody in the room and is never an answer to a live need.

## Recommended set (cheapest respectable, no budget line)

| # | Provision | Rung | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Publish a scoped statement** on one accessibility page. Example: "Talks are not live-captioned by default. Live captioning or interpretation is available on request. Tell us by `<date>`; we need `<N>` weeks and will confirm within `<M>` days either way." | Declared scope | An afternoon |
| 2 | **On-request live captioning**, booked when someone asks, with a named owner and a stated lead time | On-request live provision (the efficiency default) | Near zero unless it fires, then a vendor fee |
| 3 | **Corrected captions on the recordings**, with the transcript published | Post-event corrected captions | Organizer hours |
| 4 | **Hand delivery to `event-production`** to own the signal path | Routing | Your crew already has the path |

Point 2 is the weak spot. It only fires if someone discloses, and most people don't. It also commits you to act, because refusing after receiving a disclosure is the exposure. A promise you can't fund is worse than no promise. So hold a small contingency, or line up a sponsor to cover it, before you publish it. I have no sourced price for live captioning, so get two quotes. With 5 rooms and 2 days, one on-request room-day is a bounded cost.

## Why not "every room"?

Live captioning in every room is the top of the range (the foundation template does it). It also costs the most and needs a budget line. You have none, so it's out unless a sponsor funds it. With no line, you can't commit to it, so don't publish it.

## Gaps you should close

- **Publish early.** Attendees book travel months ahead. A page published after that reached nobody it was for.
- **Say what is and isn't covered.** Don't let a deaf attendee find the scope out in the room. Say outright that rooms are uncaptioned by default.
- **Check the speaker release.** Does the recording release cover a transcript and any translations? Answer this once, before publishing.
- **Handle disclosures properly.** Decide retention, access, turnaround, where the data lives and who decides a decline before you open the channel. Delete the requests at the debrief. If the registration form asks, the ticketing platform holds that data by default.
- **Counsel question.** Which statutory duties apply to a public event in your jurisdiction? That's out of scope here and needs counsel.
- **Digital layer.** Put captions on published media and meet contrast minimums on the schedule and forms. Auto-captions on the livestream player are a reasonable extra (my suggestion, not from the skill), but they don't replace anything in the room.
- **Unmentioned so far.** Step-free routes, reserved seating and a quiet space also matter at 800 attendees. I've only covered captioning because you asked about it.

## Questions that would change the ranking

1. Does any sponsor or foundation fund an accessibility line? If yes, every-room captioning becomes promotable.
2. Did last year's edition get captioning or interpretation requests? Known need moves captioning from on-request to scheduled.
3. Is this recurring? If so, a volunteer subtitle pipeline starts to pay off across editions.
4. Who owns accessibility from now through the debrief? It needs a named person, not an inbox.

Answer #1 first. It decides whether you stay at the cheap set or can fund live captioning.
