## Why the colleague's two ideas fail

- **Silence.** He asked for a reasonable accommodation, and your site invited the request. Ignoring it is the worst outcome on every front: ethical, legal (in many places, including the US under the ADA, "we didn't answer" is indefensible), and reputational. One screenshot of an ignored email would do more damage than an honest "we're still working on it."
- **Auto-captions.** Automatic captions are not an equivalent substitute for a sign-language interpreter. Written English may not be Tomas's first language, and auto-captions are error-prone on technical vocabulary and speaker changes. You can offer them as an addition. Don't offer them as the answer.
- **"We technically can't."** Your vendor said they can't book a certified interpreter in your city. That is not the same as "no interpreter exists." Remote interpreters, interpreters travelling in from nearby cities, and human captioners (CART) are all bookable on short notice. Nobody has tried them yet.

## The 19-day plan

**Days 0–2: widen the search**

- Ask the AV vendor for the names of the agencies they tried, and ask whether they tried remote and out-of-region interpreters.
- Contact two or three agencies directly. Ask about:
  - in-person interpreters travelling in
  - remote video interpreting (VRI) with a live feed to the venue or the stream
  - a team of two per day (interpreters are normally booked in pairs for sessions longer than about an hour)
  - rush fees, and the earliest date they can confirm
- Contact your national association of sign-language interpreters or your national registry. Many keep job boards or referral lists.
- Get a quote for a remote **human captioner (CART)** as the fallback. Remote captioners are often available at short notice.

**Day 3–4: decide, then tell Tomas**

- **Full interpreters for both days:** confirm in writing.
- **Partial coverage:** cover his priority sessions first, and add CART for the rest.
- **Only CART is possible:** say so honestly and let him choose (attend, refund, or transfer).
- **Nothing is possible:** tell him early, with the refund or transfer offer.

**Days 5–15: prepare so it works on the day**

- Send the interpreters the agenda, speaker names, slides, and a glossary of technical terms at least a week ahead. Interpreting a developer conference cold is hard.
- Brief speakers. They should speak at a measured pace, repeat audience questions, and avoid walking out of the interpreter's sight line.
- Reserve front-and-center seating with a clear view of the interpreter. Light the interpreter's stage position.
- Show the interpreter on the stream as picture-in-picture, if Tomas is remote.
- Add a named staff contact for Tomas on site.

**Day 19 and after:** ask Tomas for feedback and log what worked. Don't skip this; it becomes next year's process.

## Avoiding this next year

1. **Replace "accommodations available upon request."** Publish a real accessibility page that lists:
   - what you provide by default (live captions on every session, step-free access, quiet room, and so on)
   - what you can add on request (sign-language interpreting, CART, large print, guide support)
   - a request deadline, which should be at least 6 weeks before the event
   - a named contact and a promised response time (for example, 2 business days)
2. **Ask at registration.** Add an optional accessibility field to the ticket form, so you hear about needs on day one, not day −19.
3. **Budget for it.** Create a standing line item. As a rule of thumb, reserve a small percentage of the budget (often 1–3%) for accommodations, and make clear it is not charged to attendees.
4. **Build the supply before you need it.**
   - Pick an interpreter agency and a CART provider in spring, with a standing agreement and an agreed rush process.
   - Add accessibility requirements to the AV vendor's contract, including who finds interpreters and what the lead time is.
5. **Own it.** Name one accessibility lead and write a one-page runbook:
   - request comes in
   - reply within 24 hours
   - owner assigned
   - vendors contacted within 48 hours
   - confirmation sent to the attendee
6. **Cover the basics for everyone.** Consider hiring interpreters for the keynote and main track by default. It costs less than a crisis, and it turns accessibility into a selling point.
7. **Review it.** After the event, hold a retro and add the lessons to the runbook.

Check your local accessibility and anti-discrimination obligations with counsel. I can't verify them for your jurisdiction.
