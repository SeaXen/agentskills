# StratosDev Days: quiet room sizing and explanation

## 1. Is there a standard ratio?

No. I don't know of any published standard, such as seats per 100 attendees, from a standards body, a venue association, or a conference-accessibility guideline. Treat any figure you see quoted as an organizer heuristic. The numbers below are my own sizing logic, not a standard.

**Starting spec for 900 attendees**

| Item | Suggested spec | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Peak simultaneous use | About 1.5–3% of attendees, so roughly 15–25 people | Use is spiky. It peaks at lunch and between sessions, and it is near zero during talks. |
| Seats | 16–20, with some overflow tolerance | Sizing for the peak keeps people from being turned away at the door. |
| Floor area | About 3 m² per seat, so 50–60 m² | Quiet rooms need spacing between people. Theater-style density defeats the purpose. |
| Seating mix | Armchairs and low sofas, a few floor cushions, and 2–3 tables with chairs for people who need to sit upright | People use the room differently, for example to decompress or to work quietly. |
| Lighting | Dimmable or low and warm, with no flicker | Bright or flickering light is a common trigger. |
| Sound | A real door, a location away from the expo floor and catering, and no PA feed | A room beside the main hall with thin partitions fails. |
| Location | Same floor as the main sessions, step-free, and signposted from the main route | A room that's hard to find won't get used. |
| Hours | Open for the full conference day, including breaks | |
| Staffing | A volunteer or staff check-in once or twice an hour, not a gatekeeper | |

Because the figures are heuristics, put them in the venue spec as a minimum with a target. For example: "minimum 40 m², target 55 m²." Then **review day-one occupancy** and ask the venue whether an adjacent room can be opened if it runs hot. Record actual peak occupancy this year and use it to size next year's room.

Consider keeping these as separate rooms from the quiet room: prayer or reflection, lactation, and medication or rest. They have different privacy and noise needs.

## On "accessibility matters less here"

I'd push back on the premise before you finalize the spec.

- Needs don't track ticket price. Migraine, sensory sensitivity, anxiety, ADHD, autism, chronic illness, and post-illness fatigue all affect well-paid professionals. Commonly cited estimates put neurodivergence at about 15–20% of the population, which at 900 attendees is on the order of 100+ people. Take that estimate as indicative, not exact.
- Who pays the ticket doesn't change what a three-day, 900-person event does to a person's sensory load.
- A quiet room is cheap relative to a 1,400 euro ticket. The cost is one room and some furniture.
- Legal duties depend on your jurisdiction and venue. They can include equality or disability law and the venue's own accessibility obligations. Have the venue contract and your counsel confirm what applies, because I can't say from here.
- Enterprise buyers sometimes check accessibility provisions when they approve attendance or sponsorship. A missing basic provision is easier to avoid than to explain.

None of this requires "heavy" provisions. A modest set done properly is enough: step-free access, captions or a transcript for main-stage sessions, clear signage, and the quiet room.

## 2. "Quiet rooms are standard everywhere, so we shouldn't need to explain ours"

The claim is partly true. Quiet rooms are now common at larger and well-run conferences, but they aren't universal, and attendee awareness of them is uneven. Even where they are standard, the board member's conclusion doesn't follow. An unexplained quiet room tends to fail in specific ways:

- **People don't use it.** Many attendees don't know it exists, or don't know whether they're "allowed" in.
- **It gets repurposed.** Without stated rules it becomes a phone-call booth, a meeting room, or a place to eat lunch, which defeats its purpose.
- **Staff can't answer questions consistently.** Without a one-line description, each person improvises.
- **It's unclear who it's for.** If it isn't explained, attendees who need it often won't ask.

Explaining it takes four things and very little space:

1. **Location:** on the map and in the app, with signs along the route.
2. **Purpose:** one sentence, for example "A low-light, low-noise space to take a break from the event."
3. **Rules:** no calls, no meetings, no talking above a whisper, and silent devices.
4. **Access:** open to everyone, with no need to explain why you're using it.

**Draft signage and listing copy**

> **Quiet Room.** Level 1, Room 104. A calm space to rest, recharge, or work in silence. Open all day. Everyone is welcome. Please keep calls, meetings, and conversation outside.

If the board wants a short answer: "Even where quiet rooms are common, people use them correctly only when the purpose and rules are stated. That takes three lines in the program."

1. Put the spec table into the venue request as minimum and target.
2. Add the signage copy to the program and the event app.
3. Ask the venue for a candidate room and its sound isolation, then confirm it before you sign.
