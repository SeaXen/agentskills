# BitSpark Hackathon: review of the three plans

**Verdict:** all three have a problem, and the sticker plan is the worst. I checked each against the `event-accessibility-inclusion` skill and its references. Labels: **[sourced]** means it comes from published organizer practice in the skill's catalog. **[self-set]** means it is my judgment, not a standard. This is not legal advice. Allergen labeling and disability-data duties vary by jurisdiction, so those questions go to counsel and your caterer.

## 1. Stickers: the colour meaning is wrong (fix before printing) 🔴

**The problem.** The sourced convention for these stickers is an interaction and recording boundary, not personality **[sourced]**:

- **Green:** open to talk.
- **Yellow:** only if you know me.
- **Red:** not interested in talking, or in being photographed or filmed.

Your community manager's "green = extrovert, red = introvert" collides with that meaning in three ways:

- **Introverts become unapproachable.** An introvert who wears red because they are introverted will be avoided at a 36-hour team-forming event. That is the opposite of a fun label.
- **Extroverts lose their boundary.** An extrovert who wants no photos has no way to say so.
- **Red becomes a photo opt-out by accident.** Photographers and attendees will read red as "don't photograph me" whatever your legend says. If red means "introvert", you will shoot people who opted out, and they will have no recourse.

**Fix.**
1. Keep the stickers, but print the sourced meanings on the sticker sheet and on a sign. Use "open to talk / only if I know you / no talking, no photos" and drop the personality framing entirely.
2. Make them self-selected, picked up at registration **[sourced]**.
3. Brief photographers and volunteers: no recording of anyone wearing red.
4. Make sure your code of conduct lists recording someone who wears red as prohibited, and routes it through its incident process. The skill hands enforcement to `event-code-of-conduct`; it does not draft it.
5. If you do want a fun personality sticker, make it a separate sticker, not the same colour system.

**Question for you:** do you have a code of conduct and an incident process for 36 hours straight, including nights? Who handles incidents at 3am?

## 2. Food: "allergies? ask a volunteer" is not a plan 🟠

**The problem.** Three things are wrong:

- **It filters out the people who most need help.** Anything that requires asking a human screens out people who won't ask. It is also a poor fit for a safety issue.
- **It is unowned.** The skill lists "no accessibility owner" as a failure mode. A volunteer at a table at 2am does not know the ingredients of a contracted menu.
- **It is too late.** A restriction found at the table cannot be fixed by the caterer.

**Fix** (practice from the skill's dietary catalog, **[sourced]** from the hackathon organizer guide and the IACC dietary guide):

1. **Ask everyone who will be fed before the event.** That means all ~340 people: participants, mentors and volunteers.
2. **Use grouped questions, not one free-text box.** Three groups:
   - Common diets.
   - Health-related diets: gluten-free, celiac, diabetes.
   - Religious or cultural diets.
3. **Include the common allergens:** peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, fish, milk, eggs, soy and wheat, plus sesame as an emerging ninth.
4. **Send the results to the caterer now.** The contract is signed, but ask for a one-off adjustment to the order. That is a sourced fallback.
5. **Have a second fallback.** If the caterer cannot cover a restriction, give that person a delivery credit with advance notice, so they can plan their own meal.
6. **Hold restricted meals at a separate table.** Check names off a printed list as meals are handed out, so nobody else takes them.
7. **Label dishes at the food table.** Keep the sign, but have it point to labels and a named person.
8. **Name one owner** for dietary needs for the whole 36 hours, with a shift handover.

Ask counsel and the caterer what allergen information your jurisdiction requires you to display.

**Questions for you:**
- Does the catering contract allow menu adjustments?
- Is there a delivery-credit budget?

## 3. Skipping housekeeping in the opening: reverse this 🟠

**The problem.** The skill says a provision nobody announces is used only by the people who already knew about it. "People can read the website" has the same flaw. At a 36-hour event, many people arrive tired, and the people who most need the information are the least likely to have read it.

**Fix.** Spend about sixty seconds **[sourced]** on this, not a full housekeeping segment:
- Where the quiet space is.
- Where the all-gender restrooms are.
- What the stickers mean (see section 1).
- Where the dietary table is, and who to find.
- Where reserved seating is, if you offer it.
- Who owns each provision, and when they are on shift.

Then back it up with physical signs and a briefing for the registration desk **[sourced]**. Brief volunteers on every provision, including where each thing is. Add a short written accessibility page on the website for people who want to read ahead.

## 4. Gaps in the plan 🟠

These are not in your plan, and the 36-hour length makes them bigger:

- **No accessibility owner** [self-set to name one]. Name one person until the debrief, not an inbox.
- **No quiet space.** At 36 hours straight, the need for a low-stimulation place grows. The skill gives no sizing rule. The one sourced observation is roughly 5% of attendees in the room at once, from an unconference series, and it is not a standard. So the figure for your room is **[self-set]**: for 340 people, a room for about 17 at a time. Say it is self-set. Siting guidance **[sourced]**: away from the main hub, dim lighting, step-free, separate from any nursing room.
- **No request channel.** Only booked items need one, such as interpretation. If you open one, publish the owner, reply time and retention before it opens.
- **Captioning scope.** Decide whether the opening ceremony, demos and judging are captioned, and say which parts are covered **[sourced]**.
- **Website accessibility.** If the website is where people read everything, it needs WCAG 2.2 AA on registration and the schedule. Check the registration flow by keyboard before you claim anything. This covers the website only, not the venue.
- **Venue.** If the venue is not yet fixed, write the access criteria first. If it is fixed, publish the real step-free facts and what it cannot do.

## 5. Priority order

1. Fix the sticker legend before the print run. It is cheap to fix now and expensive afterwards.
2. Send the dietary survey to all ~340 people and brief the caterer.
3. Name the accessibility owner and the dietary owner, with shifts.
4. Add the sixty-second ceremony script and the signs.
5. Publish a short accessibility page, honest about what is and is not provided.

**Not checked:** your jurisdiction, venue, catering contract terms, and whether a code of conduct exists. These change the answer.
