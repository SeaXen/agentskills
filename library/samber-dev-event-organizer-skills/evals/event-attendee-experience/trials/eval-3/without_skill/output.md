**Short answer: no, it doesn't work.** Don't merge the two spaces, and don't hide the room. Make two separate rooms and announce both.

## Why the plan fails

1. **A curtain isn't private.** A curtain stops sight lines but not sound. Next to the networking lounge, the loudest zone of a 500-person event, anyone reporting an incident can be overheard. Anyone who needs quiet gets noise instead.
2. **The two uses clash.** A quiet room needs dim light, silence and anonymity. A reporting room needs confidentiality and a trained person. Someone in sensory overload would end up beside a conversation about harassment, and a reporter would have an audience. Each use makes the other worse.
3. **The sign exposes people.** "Wellness & Incident Support Room" in the busiest spot tells everyone that whoever walks in is reporting something. Many people won't use it at all.
4. **Telling people only if they ask defeats both goals.**
   - The people who most need a quiet space (anxious, autistic or otherwise neurodivergent attendees) are the least likely to ask.
   - Your survey respondents asked for it, so they would expect to be told about it.
   - A CoC reporting route must be known before anything happens. A route nobody knows about doesn't count as one.
5. **The nap fear has a design fix.** Nobody naps in a room with no beds, upright chairs, "no phones, no talking" rules and a 30-minute guideline.
6. **There are practical risks.** An enclosed corner of an open atrium may block fire egress or sight lines. Venue and fire rules may forbid it, so check with the venue. A responder alone behind a curtain with an attendee is also a safeguarding problem, both for the attendee and for the responder.

## What to build instead

### 1. Quiet room
- **Where:** a real room with a door on a session floor, away from the atrium, session AV and the lounge. Choose one near step-free access, a lift and accessible toilets.
- **Size:** 8–12 seats for 500 people.
- **Setup:**
  - Dim or warm light, with no music.
  - Chairs and a few floor cushions. No beds or sofas that invite sleeping.
  - Water, free earplugs, and ear defenders to borrow.
  - A card on the door and inside with the rules: no talking, no calls, no laptops.
- **Staffing:** none is needed. A volunteer checks in every 30–60 minutes.
- **Hours:** open all day, from doors-open to close.
- **Door sign:**
  > **Quiet Room**
  > Rest your ears and eyes. No talking, no calls. Everyone welcome, no need to ask.
  > Please keep stays to about 30 minutes if others are waiting.

### 2. Code of Conduct and support room
- **Where:** a small meeting room with a real door, on a different floor or at the opposite end from the quiet room. It should be in a low-traffic spot that isn't isolated and has two ways out. Not near the lounge.
- **Cheap option:** reuse the organizers' office or speaker green room. It costs nothing, and the neutral sign protects privacy.
- **Staffing:** two trained responders on rotation, so nobody is ever alone with a reporter. Put a white-noise machine outside the door.
- **Door sign (neutral on purpose):**
  > **Organizers' Room, 2.04**
- **Announce its purpose everywhere else:**
  - Code of Conduct page, attendee guide and badge back: "To report a concern or get support, go to the Organizers' Room (floor 2, room 2.04) or contact any organizer in a [colour] lanyard. You can also call or text [number], staffed all day."
  - Opening remarks, plus the venue map with both rooms marked.
- **Phone line:** give a number so people can report without walking into the room in view of others.
- **Medical needs:** keep first aid separate, at registration or the info desk.

## Plan B if you really can't get two rooms
- Keep the CoC function in the organizers' office, since a neutral sign makes it private at no cost.
- Make the quiet room the best spare breakout room you can find.
- Use the atrium only for the quiet space, and only as a last resort. Put it at the opposite end from the lounge, with a proper partition (a screen or acoustic panels, not a curtain), and still announce it.

## Checklist
1. Get the venue floor plans and pick the two rooms.
2. Confirm hours, keys and any fire-code limits with the venue.
3. Recruit and brief the two responders.
4. Add both rooms to the attendee guide, badge, website and opening slides.
5. Print the door signs and the wayfinding signs.
