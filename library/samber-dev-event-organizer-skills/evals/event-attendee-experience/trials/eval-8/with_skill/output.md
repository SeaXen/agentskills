# DataMesh Live: audit sign-off and pre-print checklist

**Don't sign off, and don't send signage or badges to the printer tomorrow.** The table isn't an audit yet, and several print-critical decisions are still open. The cost of waiting is small. A sign with the wrong room number can't be fixed at 09:00 on the day.

## 1. Why the table fails

- **It restates the touchpoints.** In every row, Action repeats the touchpoint name and Opportunity restates the problem. "Improve the check-in experience" is the question, not an answer.
- **No row names a place, an hour or a cause.**
  - "Queue could be long" gives no hour and no cause. At 700 people it could mean 80 arrivals in the first 15 minutes at one table.
  - "Rooms might fill up" names no room and no capacity.
  - "Lines" for lunch gives no number of serving tables, no restricted-diet table and no staggering.
- **No row can be executed this week.** A usable row names a location, a cause and a fix you could do before doors open. For example: *"Nothing is signed between the desk and the corridor junction eight metres away. One A3 sign at the junction names both tracks by destination."*
- **Three of the ten stages are covered.** The audit should also cover:
  - arrival
  - the first wayfinding decision after the desk
  - the hallway track
  - the quiet room
  - swag
  - the help moment
  - departure
  - the restricted-diet line
- **It came from the floor plan only.** The valuable rows are the ones that look fine on paper and fail when someone walks the building. That walk hasn't happened.

Your ops lead's work isn't wasted. It's a first-pass skeleton, and the rows need to be rewritten with specifics.

## 2. Decisions that block printing

These change what goes on the printed objects, so settle them before the printer. Questions to answer first:

1. **Does any sponsor contract already promise badge scanning or lead capture?**
   - **If no (the default):** print name-only badges with no machine-readable code. Scannable badges are actively discouraged, and attendee contact lists are never given or sold to sponsors.
   - **If yes:** the badge needs all four mitigations together:
     - consent at registration before printing
     - a physical opt-out sticker
     - written sponsor terms
     - the code on the **back** with two-point lanyard attachment
   - Never encode attendee data directly in the QR.
   - This is the highest-risk item. If the badge ships wrong, you find out when a sponsor asks for the export.
2. **Which ticket categories exist, and do any need their own lane?**
   - Pre-sort badges by last name, then first name.
   - Build the sponsor/regular split to tolerate attendees who don't know which ticket they hold.
   - Use a separate walk-in lane with the full registration form, not a shortened one.
3. **What register did the culture work set, and are pronouns printed on badges?** Pronoun indicators are a culture decision, but printing them is yours.
4. **What dietary categories and accessible-route bar were set?** Ask for them. Don't invent the list.
5. **What is the signed floor plan?** Every room name and number on a sign must come from it.
6. **What is the last-change date for each artifact?** Badge print, signage print and catering headcount may have different deadlines. "Tomorrow" may be your ops lead's pace rather than the printer's hard deadline.

## 3. Rebuild the audit before printing

Do this in order:

1. **Redo the paper audit** across all ten stages, with one row per touchpoint, using the corrected format below.
2. **Count the decision points on the floor plan.** Count the places where a person must choose a direction, not the number of rooms. Every decision point gets a sign or a person.
3. **Walk the building**, or have the venue send a walkthrough, with the same rows. Rows that look fine on paper and fail on the walk are the ones that matter.
4. **Review three artifacts before anything is printed:**
   - the floor-plan markup
   - the badge design
   - the signage list

## 4. Gaps to design before print

| Area | What to decide | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Signage** | A list keyed to decision points. Sign destinations, not geography. Show the accessible route on the signs. Sign toilets, water, the quiet room, food and the way back to the main hall. | A sign naming only the staircase makes the accessible route invisible. |
| **Check-in model** | Default: pre-sorted pickup with a walk-in lane. Promote to a multi-lane desk if you have a real ticket-category split or a door-open rush above one queue. Keep swag at a separate table if check-in is the constraint. | At 700 people, one table doing check-in, swag, walk-ins and questions fails at the busiest ten minutes. |
| **Quiet room** | A real room with a door. Findable without crossing the main thoroughfare. Signed plainly as "Quiet Room", with no incident label. | It must serve decompression and confidential intake at once. A curtained hallway corner serves neither. |
| **Help path** | A named, visibly identifiable help point. Lost property needs a named owner and a stated disposal date. If the code-of-conduct pipeline names an on-duty responder, add an on-call organizer. | Questions arrive anyway, at whoever looks least busy. |
| **Dietary** | Choose Model A (collect in advance, serve from a separate table with a printed checklist) or Model B (live per-meal request). Your catering contract decides which. Ask in advance about fallbacks and tell any attendee the caterer can't accommodate. | The collection is the easy half. The separate table and checklist is the half that fails. |
| **Dietary data** | Collect only the categories you will act on. Destroy the printed list after the meal service. | Dietary answers are health data in several jurisdictions. |
| **First-timers** | A line on the badge back, or an offered (not assigned) lanyard colour. Print the three unasked questions on a card: where do I sit, can I leave a talk partway, what is the hallway track. Name a place with a person in it for the first twenty minutes. | Don't build a separate track. It turns awkwardness into a label they wear all day. |
| **Hallway track** | Space on the path between session rooms and food, with high tables, power and coffee between breaks. Keep it acoustically separate from the quiet room. | Both are "not a session room", so they get placed next to each other constantly. |
| **Caring responsibilities** | Ask whether a meaningful share of the audience has them. If yes, consider a nursing/wellness suite near registration. | The accessibility bar may already require it. |

The first-timer line on the badge back, the quiet-room sign and the caregiver provision all depend on the badge and signage design. That is another reason to settle them before the print order.

## 5. Plan for the next three weeks

If the printer deadline is firm, print in two stages:

- **Now:** items that don't depend on the open decisions, such as lanyards. Choose two-point lanyards only after confirming the scanning posture.
- **After the walkthrough:** signage and badges.

Doors are in three weeks and printing is tomorrow, so a two-to-three-day delay to run the walkthrough is almost certainly absorbable. Confirm the print lead time with the printer.

## 6. Record before doors open

Pick two measures, write down what each would change next edition, and record both before the event:

- **Dietary fulfilment** (gate): every attendee who declared a restriction received the meal ordered for them. The only number that matters is zero misses.
- **Consent-record completeness** (gate): applies only if any badge carries a machine-readable code.
- **Quiet-room dual-purpose check** (gate): the room has a door, is findable without crossing the main thoroughfare, and is signed so ordinary use is unremarkable.
- **Longest check-in queue, and the hour it happened** (self-set).
- **Signs added by hand on the day** (self-set): each one is a decision point the design missed.
- **Peak moments** (self-set): ask attendees whether anything stood out. Organizers systematically overestimate this (Freeman 2025 reports 78% of organizers believe they deliver peak moments, against 40% of attendees reporting one). Treat the gap as the finding, not a target.
