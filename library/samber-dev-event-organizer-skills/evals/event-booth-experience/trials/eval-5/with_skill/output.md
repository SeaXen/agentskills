# MergeConf edition 2: corner-spot answer and back-wall fix

## 1. What to tell the biggest sponsor

**Don't quote a number.** No usable data links booth position to foot traffic or leads at developer conferences. The figures that circulate come from booth-display vendors selling booths. If you quote one, the sponsor will hold you to it.

Say something like this:

> Nobody has reliably measured what a corner spot is worth at an event like ours, and I won't invent a figure. Here is what the upgrade physically gives you. Here is what we will count this year and share with you afterwards. And here is what we learned last year about the back wall, which affects how we place everyone.

**Describe the position, not its value:**
- Say it sits on the entrance path, or the food and hangout path, and whether it is off a spur or on the main flow. This is the only placement principle that is actually published: put tables where foot traffic already passes.
- Put the upgrade in the tier spec as physical rows. The sponsor can then judge the price against the spec. The eight rows are:
  - footprint (dimensions)
  - furniture
  - position class
  - power
  - network
  - vertical limit
  - signage rule
  - staffing
- Write the corner as a "premium zone" tier. If the sponsor's package promises a larger footprint, a power drop or a taller banner, state the exact dimensions. Check the venue before promising any of these.

**Offer what you can measure.** These numbers are self-set, so label them that way.
- Count entrance and corner-area passes with a volunteer tally or a door counter. This is anonymous footfall, not attendee tracking.
- Let the sponsor use their own scanner or lead count. The consent flow is theirs and belongs to `event-sponsor-fulfillment`.
- Promise a post-event report with the counts, and no performance guarantee.
- If they want a per-lead or per-visit guarantee, route that to `event-sponsor-pricing`. This skill won't invent that price.

**Tell the sponsor about the trade-off.** If the back-wall fix works, position matters less across the floor. That is good for attendees and the other booths, and it narrows what the premium spot buys. A premium that only works while the rest of the floor stays dead is a weak thing to sell, so say so. The corner's remaining value is first sight at the entrance, plus the physical spec you wrote down.

## 2. Fixing the back wall

**Treat last year's claim as a symptom to verify.** "Basically nobody came by" is the sponsors' report. That is the right source for dead-booth count, because you ask sponsors and don't infer it. Do these first:
1. Ask each of the four back-wall sponsors for their rough visitor count. This is your dead-booth baseline.
2. Pull last year's floor plan. Check where the food, coffee, hangout space and doors were relative to the back wall.
3. Check whether those booths were staffed for the whole open time. An empty booth is a dead spot the traffic pattern can't fix.

**Re-site on the path attendees already take.**
- Default rung: circulation-path siting. Put tables on the food and hangout route. It has near-zero effort and is the only layout rung with published practice behind it.
- If the floor is a dedicated hall, or the booth count is more than one path holds, move to a **racetrack perimeter loop**. One circulating path, no booth off a spur. Position then matters little, which is the easiest layout to defend to a sponsor who paid for a good spot.
- Don't use grid rows if the back wall is the problem, because aisle interiors starve and ends do well.
- Don't leave the floor unmapped for sponsors to pick spots. Last year's dead corner is how that ends.
- Zone by tier only if the ladder really sold position, and you have the political capital to hold the line. If the corner is a paid zone, the zone map is where the corner sponsor's upgrade lives.

**Pull attendees to the booths layout can't reach: a passport or stamp card.**
- One card, one stamp per booth, a prize for a completed card.
- Give the stamp for arriving. Never require a conversation or a signature, which turns it into a forced pitch.
- Don't scan attendees at booths. That becomes lead capture, with a consent flow, and moves to `event-sponsor-fulfillment`.
- Upgrade to a live-leaderboard scavenger hunt only if you already have a screen and someone to run it.
- The prize and one print run are the cost. The print date comes from the floor-plan sign-off date, so work back from that.

**Measure it the way the skill says.**
- Dead-booth count this year, compared with the four from last year (self-set).
- Passport completions by attendees who were not already touring booths (self-set). This is the only thing the mechanic exists to buy.
- Tables moved by hand on the day (self-set). Keep the count.

## 3. Prerequisites before you commit to the floor plan

I couldn't run the full interview, so the following are open. Each one can change the picks above.
1. Is the floor a dedicated hall, a room or a corridor? This decides which layout rungs exist.
2. Where do the entrance, food and hangout space sit on the signed floor plan, and where do power and network reach?
3. What is the print and sign-off date for the floor plan? Get the fire-lane and egress review before printing, not before doors. Any rigging, such as hanging signs, needs written venue approval.
4. How many booths and tiers are there, and what did each tier's package promise on floor presence? Route a missing ladder to `event-sponsor-pricing`.
5. What is your effort ceiling, and do you have the political capital to tell a top-tier sponsor where they sit?
6. Is a screen and someone to run it available? Only then is the hunt a real option.

I stated no aisle widths, amperages or egress distances, because those come from the venue and the jurisdiction. I also didn't invent power or WiFi per-booth figures. Ask the venue what a booth drop is, what it costs and by when to request it, then publish that deadline as a row in the floor brief.
