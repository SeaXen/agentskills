# Compiler Days: cash-flow check

**Verdict:** ⚠️ Not fine yet. The event is profitable on paper, but the cash timing is a problem. You pay three deposits before any money comes in. Decline the vendor's offer.

## 1. Profit versus cash

- Margin is 14k on 82k revenue (17%). Break-even revenue is 68k, so a 17% revenue miss turns a profit into a loss. Most of your costs (venue, AV, insurance) are committed early and can't shrink if revenue does.
- Cash out comes first:
  - Mar 15: venue deposit (40%)
  - Mar 20: insurance
  - Apr 1: AV deposit
- Cash in comes later:
  - Sponsors: mid-May at the earliest
  - Tickets: nothing before May 1, and early sales are usually a trickle
- That leaves about 8 weeks with money going out and nothing coming in. The size of the hole is the sum of those three payments plus any other spend before mid-May (marketing, speaker travel, other vendor deposits). You didn't give me those amounts, so I can't say whether you can cover them.

## 2. Weak points in the sponsor timeline

- **Your dates don't add up.** Net-60 from a February invoice falls due between early and late April. You say mid-May, so you are either already assuming late payment (sensible) or the date is loose. Either way, all three deposits still land before the earliest payment.
- **Real payment is often later than net-60.** Corporate payables commonly pay at 75–90 days. A February invoice could then pay in mid-May to June. Plan for that case, not the best case.
- **The February invoice date is a forecast.** Contracts slipping to March pushes cash to June.
- **Concentration risk.** If sponsors are most of the 82k, one or two that don't sign or pay late is the whole margin. I need the sponsor versus ticket split to size this.

## 3. The vendor offer

Decline it as stated.

- Paying early with no discount costs you cash you don't have in March–May. It buys you nothing.
- It removes your leverage. Once they're fully paid, you have no way to hold back payment if delivery slips.
- It adds counterparty risk. If the vendor fails or the event is cancelled, you become an unsecured creditor for the full amount.
- "To get it off your plate" benefits their cash flow, not yours. Find out why they want it. A vendor short on cash is itself a risk to your event.
- If you want to engage, make it a trade: a real discount (say 3–5%) in return for early payment, and only once sponsor money is in.

## 4. What to do next

1. **Build a week-by-week cash table** from today to event day. Include every commitment and each balance due date, not just the deposits. I'm missing the amounts, the event date, and the remaining-balance dates. Send those and I'll build it with you.
2. **Run a stress case**: sponsors 25% short and paying at 90 days, tickets 30% under plan. Check the lowest cash point and whether you can fund it.
3. **Decide who covers the gap.** Options are a founder or org reserve, a credit line, or a bridge from the organizing non-profit. Settle this before March 15.
4. **Pull cash forward**:
   - Ask sponsors for 50% at signing, or net-15 or net-30 for early signers. A 5% early-pay discount is the same trade you should refuse from the vendor, in the direction that favors you.
   - Open ticket sales earlier with a discounted early-bird in Feb–March.
5. **Push cash out**:
   - Ask the venue to move the deposit date or split it into two payments.
   - Ask the insurer for installments.
   - Ask AV to start its deposit after May 1.
6. **Set a go/no-go gate.** Before paying the venue deposit, require a minimum number of signed sponsor contracts (say, enough to cover costs) or a committed funding line.
7. **Read the refund terms** in the venue and AV contracts. Confirm the insurance covers cancellation and sponsor refunds, and that it binds before your first deposit goes out.
