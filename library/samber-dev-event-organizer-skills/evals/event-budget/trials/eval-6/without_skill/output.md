**The three numbers can't settle the question, because none of them measures what you'd need.** Each one has a different denominator, and none comes from a business like yours. Set the target from your own cost base and ticket economics instead.

## Why each claim fails

| Director | Claim | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| A | An open-source conference is 90% sponsor-funded, so match it. | It's one event, probably picked because the figure is striking. Those events usually have cheap or free individual tickets, so sponsors fill the gap by design. That's a pricing choice, not a law. Your tickets are priced as employer training spend. |
| B | A standards body's statements show cash sponsorship at about 3%, so tickets should carry us. | The denominator is the whole organization: dues, certification, publications. Sponsorship is 3% of that, not of any conference's budget. It also counts cash only, so in-kind support is excluded. This tells you almost nothing about an event P&L. |
| C | The biggest community conference earns about two-thirds of its parent foundation's revenue, so 65% sponsor share is the norm. | This is a non sequitur. "Conference is 65% of the foundation's revenue" measures how dependent the foundation is on the conference. It says nothing about how much of the conference's own revenue comes from sponsors. A fully ticket-funded event could produce the same figure. It's also the biggest event in the field, which is the least comparable to 350 people. |

Two more problems apply to all three:
- Sponsor share is an output of ticket price, attendee count, and cost structure, not an independent norm to match.
- Sponsor demand depends on audience size and buyer quality. At 350 people, booth and lead-gen value is small compared with large events.

## What your situation implies

- **Your tickets are the strong revenue line.** Employer-paid training budgets are less price-sensitive than individual or community buyers. That favors a ticket-led model, which is closer to B's conclusion, but for your own reasons.
- **Sponsors have a ceiling at 350 attendees.** Per-sponsor value is limited, so you'd need many sponsors to reach a high share. That crowds the program and can erode what training buyers pay for.
- **Sponsors and ticket buyers overlap.** If the same employers buy tickets and sponsor, a high sponsor share mostly recycles the same budgets. Check this against your records.

## Recommended rule

Set the sponsorship target from costs, not from a percentage.

1. **Tickets cover the fixed base.** Fixed cost (venue, AV, staff, insurance) ≤ 350 × ticket price × a conservative sell-through (use 80%, or your worst past year).
2. **Sponsors fund the margin and the extras.** That means scholarships, a lower entry price for community attendees, speaker travel, and a reserve.
3. **Treat the resulting share as an output.** My judgment, not data: for an event like yours it will probably land well below 90% and probably below 50%. A planning range of roughly 20–40% is a sensible starting point, to be confirmed or replaced by step 4.
4. **Check it with your own numbers.** Compute the share from your last 2–3 P&Ls. Also pull 3–5 comparable events (200–600 attendees, employer-bought tickets) whose organizers publish a P&L. A P&L has revenue by line and event cost. A standards body's or foundation's statements don't.

## What to bring back to the board

- Your own P&L split: revenue by line and cost by line, for the last 2–3 editions.
- The share of tickets bought by employers, and the overlap with sponsors.
- A ticket-price sensitivity check: did past price changes move sales?
- The comparable events in step 4.

With those, the board can pick a target by agreeing on what sponsor money should fund, and that's a clearer decision than arguing over three unlike percentages.
