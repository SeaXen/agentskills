## 1. Fee budget: verify before you trust it

- **Check the 5% card / 8% bank split.** It is unusual. Hosts normally charge one flat host fee on all income, and the payment processor's fee comes on top. Bank transfers are usually cheaper for the processor, not dearer.
- **Re-read your signed terms.** If the memory is wrong by a few points, the budget is wrong on every sponsor line.
- **Budget the processor fees separately.** These are card fees, FX conversion and payout fees. The USD and JPY sponsors add FX cost.
- **Ask whether the fee applies to gross including VAT.** If it does, VAT you collect for the tax office gets fee-charged. Budget on net revenue.
- **Assume sponsors pay by bank transfer.** Large B2B invoices mostly do, so use your higher rate for sponsor lines unless the contract says otherwise.
- **Confirm the host allows ticket sales and invoiced sponsorships.** Confirm the host can issue custom invoices with a VAT line. If it cannot, you have a bigger problem (see section 2).

## 2. Seller of record decides everything

Under a fiscal host, the host's legal entity is usually the legal seller. The host, not PortScan Summit, owes the VAT.

- **Check the host's country and VAT registration.** For a US-based host selling tickets to an event in the EU, VAT is owed in the event country. Non-established sellers have no registration threshold. The host must register there.
- **If the host will not register or collect, you cannot lawfully sell taxable tickets through it.** Options are:
  - a different host with an EU entity,
  - your own entity,
  - a ticketing platform acting as merchant of record.
- **Get the host's answer in writing.** Ask: "Will you register and account for VAT in [event country] on ticket and booth sales? How do you invoice sponsors?"

## 3. Do you charge VAT on tickets?

This assumes the event is physically in the EU. Tell me the country and I can tighten this.

- **In-person tickets: yes, almost certainly.** Admission to a conference is taxed where the event takes place. This applies whether the buyer is a company or a private person, and whether they live in Germany, Japan or the US. A reverse charge is not the default for admission. Some countries apply a local reverse charge to foreign sellers, so ask the host or an adviser.
- **Rate:** the event country's rate on the net ticket price. Show prices gross for private buyers.
- **Virtual tickets:** different rule, usually the buyer's location for private buyers. Keep them as a separate product if you sell any.
- **Small-business exemption:** some countries exempt small suppliers. It applies to the host's entity, not to the event, and it is lost for non-established sellers in most cases. Do not rely on it.
- **If the event is in the US or Japan:** the rules are different (US state sales/admission tax, Japan consumption tax). Say so and I will redo this section.

## 4. Do you charge VAT on sponsorships?

Sponsorship is an advertising and promotion service to a business. The default rule is place of supply = where the customer is established, so it depends on the sponsor.

| Sponsor | Treatment (EU event, EU-based seller) |
|---|---|
| German company | If the seller is in another country than Germany: no VAT charged, reverse charge, invoice shows the buyer's German VAT ID and a reverse-charge note. If the seller is German: German VAT. Validate the VAT ID in VIES before invoicing and keep the proof. |
| Japanese company | Outside the scope of EU VAT. No VAT charged. Invoice notes "not subject to VAT, place of supply outside the EU". Keep evidence the buyer is a business. Japanese reverse-charge rules are their concern. |
| US company | Same as Japan: outside scope, no EU VAT. US sales tax is not triggered by an EU seller, but a US host with nexus needs its own check. |

- **If a sponsor has no VAT ID:** treat it as a private-person sale and charge local VAT. A German sponsor with no valid VAT ID gets charged VAT.
- **If the host is US-based,** the host will also treat the money as a sponsorship payment with benefits, not a donation. The benefits are not tax-deductible for the sponsor.

## 5. The bundled package: one price, two tax treatments

A sponsorship plus an expo booth is likely two supplies, not one:

- sponsorship = advertising service, taxed by customer location (section 4);
- booth space = taxed where the event takes place (exhibition or property-related rule), whatever the sponsor's country.

A single price does not merge them. So for a Japanese or US sponsor, the booth part may carry local VAT while the sponsorship part carries none.

1. **Keep one commercial price in the contract.** That satisfies the sponsor's wish for one line item.
2. **Split it on the invoice.** Allocate the price between sponsorship and booth, based on your standalone price list, with the bundle discount spread proportionally. Show VAT per component.
3. **Document the allocation method** in a short internal note. This is what an auditor will ask for.
4. **Do not bury VAT inside "bundle".** One taxed lump sum, if challenged, means VAT on the whole amount.
5. **Check the host can issue a split invoice.** If its platform only allows one amount per tier, you need to invoice outside the platform.
