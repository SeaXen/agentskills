## 1. Late-payment interest

**No, not automatically.** Interest only exists if your sponsor agreement says so. Without an express clause you have no contractual right to it. (Source: the event-budget reference on sponsorship terms.)

- **If you want it, put a clause in the agreement.** Published sponsorship contracts that state it use 12-18% per annum. That figure comes from a handful of contracts, not a market standard. Drafting belongs to the sponsor-agreement skill, not the budget. Have counsel check whether your jurisdiction caps or adds statutory interest.
- **Interest won't fix the real problem.** A late payer moves your inflow date, and that's what breaks the cash model. Plan the schedule with a late-payment case, not just the contract dates.

**Your "standard structure" isn't standard.**

- **Published terms are heavier up front.** Real sponsor terms skew toward 50-100% at signing or application. Examples: 50% minimum with the application, 80/20, 100% at signing. "Small deposit, balance 30 days out" is a fallback pattern that nobody has verified, so don't cite it as what everyone does.
- **Your structure leaves money out late.** Venue deposits are typically 25-50% at signing, with the balance due 14-30 days out (generic industry-guide figures). A small sponsor deposit means your outflows can leave before sponsor money lands. That's the "profitable but still fails" shape, and the lowest cash point is the number to read, not the bottom line.
- **Before you fix the schedule, answer two questions.** By what date must sponsor money be in the account, not just invoiced? What is the lowest cash point once you lay venue deposits against your sponsor dates? I can build that schedule if you give me the dated venue terms.

## 2. The 60-room block: yes, something is wrong

"No charge to hold it" doesn't mean no risk.

- **Attrition is the catch.** A hotel giving a courtesy block to an event that isn't also buying its meeting rooms and catering may require that roughly 80% of the blocked room-nights get used. The shortfall is owed on what you committed, not on who turned up. Your block is 60 rooms × 3 nights = 180 room-nights, so about 144 would need to be used (the 80% is illustrative from one guide; check your contract).
- **The night after closing day is the weakest night.** Most attendees leave that evening or the next morning. The source guide's advice is to block only the night before day one and the nights between days, and to exclude the night after the final day.
- **Start small and grow.** The same guide starts with as few as 5 rooms. Hotels generally allow additions as the block fills. Adding capacity late is cheap, and unwinding a committed block is expensive or impossible. Locking all 60 now commits the full exposure on a headcount you haven't proven yet.

**What to do:**

1. Get the attrition clause in writing: the percentage, whether it counts per night or across the whole block, and the cutoff date. Ask whether reductions are free until a set date.
2. Confirm whether the block is truly "courtesy". Check whether the hotel is also your venue, and whether the terms change if you buy meeting rooms or catering.
3. Size the first block from the expected out-of-town show-up number. Don't use the registration count, because registrations overstate who needs a room.
4. Block two nights, not three. Start with a small number of rooms and add as attendees book.
5. Put the group rate code's expiry on your calendar. It typically lapses about a month before the event.
6. Someone must watch pickup through the sale window. Staging without a tracker is just a smaller order.

**Open question for you:** is the hotel also your event venue? That changes the attrition terms.
