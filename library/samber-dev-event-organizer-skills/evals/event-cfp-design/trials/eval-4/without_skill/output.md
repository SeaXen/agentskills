**Verdict: not as stated. Turning the toggle on is fine, but announcing "fully blind" is not.**

**Why the plan fails**

1. **Opt-in blinding isn't blind.** The people most likely to skip the toggle are the well-known names, so reviewers will still see them. The criticism was about those people, so the committee would be blind to everyone except the people at issue.
2. **The content leaks identity.** Abstracts, "at my company we…" stories, repo links, prior-talk references, and co-speaker fields all point to the author. In a small infrastructure community, three volunteers will recognize many authors even when the name is hidden.
3. **"Fully blind" is a claim you can't verify.** You can't see how the platform leaks. Common leaks are notification emails, file metadata, co-speaker fields, and what admins see. If one well-known speaker is identified and accepted, the public criticism gets worse, because you promised something you didn't deliver.
4. **Blind review addresses only one cause.** Lineup concentration also comes from invited slots, keynotes, sponsor-linked talks, and who hears about the CFP. A blind committee doesn't touch any of those.

**What I'd do instead**

1. **Test the platform first.** Submit two dummy proposals, one with the toggle on and one off. Log in as a reviewer and check every screen, email, and downloaded file. About 1 hour.
2. **Make anonymity mandatory, not optional.** Check whether the platform has a conference-level setting. If it doesn't, require every submission to be anonymized and have one non-reviewer (an organizer outside the committee) screen for identifying content. They either redact it or send it back for a fix.
3. **Give reviewers a rubric.** Use 4–5 criteria, such as novelty, clarity, audience fit, and depth. Score before any reveal, and lock the scores.
4. **Reveal speaker names only after scoring.** Use the reveal for the final lineup balance (topic mix, duplicate speakers, new vs. returning). Say so openly.
5. **Add conflict-of-interest recusal.** A reviewer who knows the author, or works with them, skips that proposal.
6. **Disclose reserved slots.** If you keep invited keynotes or sponsor slots, say how many and why.
7. **Widen the pool.** Targeted outreach to first-time and under-represented speakers, plus a few mentoring offers, does more for repeat names than blinding alone.
8. **Publish the numbers after the lineup is out.** Report submissions, acceptance rate, the share of first-time speakers, and the returning-speaker share.

**Safer wording for the call**

> "Submissions are anonymized for first-round scoring. Reviewers score on a published rubric before speaker identities are revealed. Identities are used only afterward, to balance the program. A small number of invited slots are listed separately."

That text promises what you can actually enforce.

**One question that changes the advice:** do you expect more than about 150 submissions? With three volunteers, each proposal gets only two or three reads. Past that point, consider adding 2–3 more reviewers before the process improvements.
