# OpsCurrent CFP plan, year 2

**Bottom line:** Keep the goal (fewer pitches, better talks). Change two of the three tactics. Narrowing the topics hard fixes the wrong problem. A secret rubric makes disputes worse. A guaranteed slot is fine if it sits outside the CFP and is labeled as sponsored.

## 1. Narrowing the topic list

**Don't do it as a way to cut volume.**

- 310 submissions for 38 slots is about 8:1, with 12% accepted. For a year-one community conference, that is a strong result. The real cost is review load, roughly 155 hours at 3 reviews of 10 minutes each.
- Vendor pitches don't come from a broad topic list. They come from the call not saying pitches are unwelcome, and nothing screening for them.
- A hard cut also removes the first-time practitioners a community conference exists to find. They tend to submit at the edges of the topic list.

**Do this instead:**

1. **Narrow to 4–6 themes.** Make them specific enough to steer the program ("incident response under partial failure", "SLOs in practice"). Don't use narrowing to hit a submission count.
2. **Write a no-pitch rule into the call.** Say what a pitch is: product demos, roadmap talks, "how we use X to solve Y" where X is the speaker's employer's product. Say what is welcome: vendor employees talking about general practice, or a customer co-presenting.
3. **Require three things in the abstract:**
   - A real production situation.
   - What went wrong or was learned.
   - What an attendee does differently afterward.
4. **Add a disclosure field:** "Does this talk feature a product your employer sells?" Treat a "yes" as a flag, not a disqualifier.
5. **Review blind** for the first pass (names and employers stripped). Unblind only to check for pitches and to balance the program.
6. **Offer vendors a legitimate route** (see section 3). Pitches drop when there is somewhere else to put them.

## 2. Keeping the rubric internal

**Don't do it. Publish the criteria and weights.**

- Secrecy doesn't stop rejected speakers from arguing. It gives them "arbitrary" to argue with. A published rubric lets you answer: "scored 2/5 on 'concrete lessons'."
- Published criteria also improve the submissions you get. Speakers write to the rubric, so pitchy abstracts score lower and the volume of weak submissions drops.
- A secret rubric combined with a guaranteed sponsor slot (below) looks like a rigged process. For a community-run event, that reputation risk is larger than the nuisance of some emails.

**Middle path that protects your time:**

- Publish the criteria, their weights, and the no-pitch rule.
- Keep individual reviewer scores and comments private.
- Send each rejected speaker one reason code, such as "overlaps accepted talk", "too introductory", "reads as product-focused" or "scope too broad". Add one sentence.
- State up front: "decisions are final, no appeals." This stops the arguing, not the rubric.

Example weights (adjust to your values):

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Real production experience | 30% |
| Actionable takeaways | 25% |
| Clarity and structure of abstract | 15% |
| Fit with themes | 15% |
| Speaker's ability to deliver (past talks, video, or outline) | 15% |
| Product-pitch penalty | cap the total at "reject" |

## 3. The platinum sponsor's guaranteed slot

**Guarantee a slot, but not a CFP slot.** A guarantee inside the CFP means some submissions were decided before review. That undermines the process in sections 1 and 2 and is hard to defend if anyone finds out. Anyone can find out, because speakers talk.

**Offer instead:**

1. **A clearly labeled "Sponsored session" slot** in the schedule, outside the 38 CFP slots. Mark it in the program, on the website, and at the start of the session.
2. **Written content guidelines** for sponsored sessions: no hard pitch, a practitioner or customer speaker preferred, and organizer review of the abstract. Put these in the sponsorship contract.
3. **Its own format**, separate from CFP talks: a 20-minute lightning slot, a workshop, or a demo room. It shouldn't take a main-stage slot that a CFP speaker could have had.
4. **The same terms offered to other tiers** in a scaled-down form (for example, gold gets a demo-room slot). This makes the platinum slot a tier benefit, not a special favor.
5. **An open invitation for their engineers to submit to the normal CFP**, with no promise of acceptance. The usual outcome is that their best practitioners get accepted on merit and the sponsor gets real credibility.

If the sponsor insists the slot must be a regular CFP talk, that is the point to decline. Losing one renewal costs less than a public "pay to speak" accusation, which costs you every future volunteer reviewer and speaker.

## 4. How to know whether this year's CFP went better

Fewer submissions is not a success metric. It can mean you scared off good speakers. Fix the definition of "better" now, before submissions open, and measure the same things in both years.

**Baseline from last year (do this now, while you still have the data):**

- Randomly sample about 60 of the 310 submissions.
- Have two people label each one as pitch, borderline, or practitioner, using the definition from your new call.
- Compute the pitch share, with the same labeling process, for this year's submissions.

**Metrics:**

| Question | Metric | Target direction |
|---|---|---|
| Fewer pitches? | Share of submissions labeled pitch (2 labelers, same definition) | Down |
| Better talks accepted? | Attendee rating per talk (one-question survey, 1–5) plus "did this feel like a product pitch?" yes/no | Rating up, pitch-feel down |
| Are strong submissions still arriving? | Submissions scoring above your acceptance bar per available slot | Flat or up (not down) |
| Healthy speaker pool? | Share of first-time speakers, company types, company sizes, regions | Flat or broader |
| Fair process? | Reviewer agreement (how often 2+ reviewers' scores land within 1 point), rejected-speaker survey, complaint count | Agreement up, complaints down |
| Sustainable? | Reviewer hours per accepted talk, speaker dropout rate | Hours down, dropouts low |

**Do not use:**

- Raw submission count.
- Acceptance rate alone. It moves with volume and says nothing about quality.

**Caveats to state when you report results:**

- This is a comparison of two data points, so it shows a direction, not a proof. The year-one reputation, new themes, a bigger event, and the sponsored slot all change at once.
- Attendee ratings are only comparable if you ask the same question in the same way both years. Last year's baseline may not exist, so this year becomes the baseline for year 3.
- Judge the program on a two- to three-year trend, not one run.

**Practical steps:**

1. Write the targets down before the CFP opens (for example, "pitch share under half of last year's").
2. Collect the data in a shared sheet as you go: labels, scores, reason codes.
3. Run the attendee survey at the end of each session, not afterward by email.
4. After the event, compare the table above and write a one-page retro for the committee.

## Suggested order of work

1. Label the 60-submission baseline sample (about 2 hours for two people).
2. Draft the call: themes, no-pitch rule, abstract requirements, disclosure field, published rubric.
3. Draft the sponsored-session terms and send them to the platinum sponsor before renewal talks go further.
4. Set up the review sheet, reason codes, and the attendee survey.
5. Open the CFP.
