# Sanity check: no, don't ship this plan as written

Three parts of the plan have problems. The first two are the serious ones.

## 1. "Zero reports" doesn't show the community is healthy

Zero reports across 450 or so attendee-slots over three years is more likely a sign that people don't trust or can't use the channel. A page that has been up the whole time with no reports is what an unusable reporting path looks like.

Possible reasons for zero reports:
- **No known recipient.** The page may not say who reads reports or what happens next.
- **Reporting is awkward.** If the only route is the founder's inbox, it asks people to accuse someone in a small, tight community.
- **Fear of consequences.** People expect retaliation, being the one who "made it awkward", or nothing happening at all.
- **No visible enforcement.** Nobody has ever seen the team act, so people assume it won't.
- **Silent exit.** People who had a bad experience leave and don't come back. Nobody sees that as a report.

Test it instead of assuming it:
- Run a short anonymous survey of past attendees: "Have you seen or experienced something that made you uncomfortable at a MosaicJS event? Did you know where to report it?"
- Look at repeat attendance and who stopped coming, broken down by who they are where you can.
- Ask a few trusted regulars, in person, whether they would have used the page.

Zero reports with a good survey result is reassuring. Zero reports with "I wouldn't know who to tell" is a finding.

## 2. You as the only contact is the biggest problem

You are the lead organizer and the founder. A conduct policy works only if it applies to the most powerful people in the community, and that includes you.

- **Reports about you or your friends have nowhere to go.** A harmed person would have to report to the person they are reporting. They won't, so you would never see those reports.
- **Reports about your close circle are awkward too.** Speakers, co-organizers and sponsors you know personally would be judged by you alone.
- **One inbox is a single point of failure.** If you are away, ill or burned out, nobody reads it, and the published response time means nothing.
- **A single contact discourages reporting.** In a community where everyone knows the founder, many people will hold back.

Fixes, in order of importance:
1. **Add a second recipient.** At least two people, ideally one who isn't a co-founder, and ideally with different employers or backgrounds. Put both in the footer.
2. **Add a path for reports about the organizers.** Give it to someone outside the organizing team: a trusted community member, a neighbouring meetup's organizer, or a fiscal host if you have one. Publish this path next to the primary one.
3. **Name an escalation body above you.** Say who decides when the organizers are conflicted or a decision is contested.
4. **Publish the process.** Put a short "what happens when you report" in the program:
   - acknowledgment within a set time (for example 2 business days; pick one you can hold)
   - who sees the report
   - what outcomes are possible
   - a no-retaliation promise
5. **Offer an anonymous option** if you can, such as a form that nobody can trace.
6. **Make sure two people can do everything the policy promises,** so it still works when one of you is unavailable.

For the PDF footer, a workable wording is: "Report a concern to [name A] or [name B] at [address]. If your concern involves an organizer, contact [outside person] at [address]." A single founder email with nothing else fails the basic test.

## 3. Don't commit to zero reports as a shared KPI

This one is the most important to decline. The sponsor's KPI rewards the wrong thing.

- **It pays for silence.** If reports must stay at zero, every report becomes a cost to the organizers and a risk to the sponsor relationship. That pressure works against the people reporting.
- **It makes you the judge in your own case.** You would be the sole contact, and you would also be the one whose targets get missed if a report arrives.
- **It conflicts with real harm.** If something serious happens, the honest response is to act, not to protect a number.
- **It can't be met honestly in the long run.** A larger and more trusted community will produce reports. A good reporting process raises report counts at first. That is a sign of trust.
- **It makes the sponsor look bad later.** A "zero incidents" KPI reads badly if anything surfaces afterwards.

Offer the sponsor KPIs that measure the process, not the count:
- every report acknowledged within the published window (target 100%, or 95% as a starting baseline)
- every report has a recorded outcome
- median time from report to resolution
- an annual anonymized summary of reports received and actions taken
- a post-event attendee survey question: "I felt safe and welcome" and "I knew where to report"

Say it to them plainly: "We'll commit to responding well to every report. We can't commit to receiving none, and we don't want to."

## Summary of what to change

| Item | Verdict | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "Zero reports = healthy" | Weak evidence | Survey attendees; treat silence as unverified |
| You as sole contact | Fails | Two recipients, plus an outside path for reports about organizers |
| Email in PDF footer | Fine as a start | Add second contact and "what happens next" |
| Zero-reports KPI | Decline | Replace with process KPIs |
| No escalation body | Gap | Name who sits above the organizers |
