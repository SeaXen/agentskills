# Conduct incident plan: what I'd change

**Bottom line:** keep the small team and the case-by-case judgment. Change four things: the response time, the conflict-of-interest rule, a one-page frame around your judgment, and a 30-minute tabletop in place of the dry run. The conflict rule matters most.

## 1. Conflict of interest: full recusal, not "quiet but votes" (❌ change)

Staying silent while voting leaves his influence intact and takes away the discussion that could check it.

- **A silent vote still decides the outcome.** In a two-person team, one vote is half the decision.
- **It will look bad if it comes out.** The reporter would learn the subject's spouse co-decided the case. That ends trust in your process, and not only for that case.
- **It is too narrow.** The conflict is not only the wife. It covers any close tie, such as family, close friends, business partners, a sponsor, or an employer. It applies to you too.

Replace it with this rule:

> An organizer with a personal or financial tie to anyone involved in a report (reporter, subject, or witness) recuses fully. They do not vote, discuss, or see the case file, and they are told only that a case exists and who handles it.

Recusal leaves one decider, which creates a new gap:

1. **Name a third party now.** Pick someone outside the two of you, such as a former organizer, a trusted community peer, or a lawyer. They review any case where one of you is conflicted and any case about one of you.
2. **Decide the tiebreak.** If you are both unconflicted and disagree 1–1, the more cautious interim measure applies until the third party weighs in.
3. **Tell the reporter.** If a conflict exists, they learn that someone recused, though not the details.

## 2. "10 business days" (⚠️ change)

Ten business days is about 14 calendar days, and your event lasts 3. A report made on Friday gets no answer until after everyone has gone home. Split the commitment in two:

| Moment | Acknowledge | Act on safety |
|---|---|---|
| During the event | Within 15 minutes | Same hour, same day |
| Before or after the event | Within 2 business days | Within 10 business days for a final outcome (this part of your draft is fine) |

Three requirements make the on-site row real:

- **A staffed contact.** A phone number or chat that someone answers for the full 3 days. You can't be the only staffed contact if you're also running the event.
- **Briefed volunteers.** Every volunteer and staff member knows how to take a report. That means listening, writing it down, and passing it on, with no investigating and no promises.
- **Two or more channels.** Offer an email address, a phone number, and in-person staff. Don't offer only one.

Also name a person for each channel, so "we will respond" has a subject.

## 3. "Good judgment case by case" (🟠 keep it, add a thin frame)

Judgment decides outcomes well. It is a weak process, because nobody can see how you decide. Write one page, not a handbook, covering:

1. **What is unacceptable.** A short list: harassment, discrimination, threats, unwanted contact, and recording or photographing people who refuse it.
2. **Available responses.** Warning, a talk with the person, removal from a session, ejection, ban, notifying employer or sponsor, and contacting police for crimes. Keep it a menu, not a mapping from offense to penalty.
3. **Confidentiality.** Who sees what, and that reporters are not retaliated against.
4. **Fairness.** The person reported gets to hear the allegation and respond, except where safety requires immediate removal.
5. **Appeal.** To the third party from section 1.
6. **A private log.** Keep date, parties, decision, and rationale. Access is limited to unconflicted organizers.

With 900 people over 3 days, "may never happen" is optimistic. Plan for a reportable incident, even if it's minor.

## 4. Skipping the dry run (🟠 replace with a tabletop)

You're right that a full drill is overkill. Neither of you has handled an incident, so the first one will be improvised under stress. A tabletop costs 30 minutes and no preparation:

1. Read one scenario aloud: *"At 21:00 on day 2 an attendee reports a speaker made repeated unwanted advances at the afterparty. The speaker goes on stage at 10:00 tomorrow."*
2. Walk through who gets the report, who decides, what happens to the keynote slot, and what you tell the reporter and the speaker.
3. Run two more:
   - A report about a sponsor's booth staff.
   - A report that names one of you or a person you're close to.
4. Write down every point where you said "uh, I guess we'd…". Those gaps become the one-pager.

The third scenario tests the recusal rule from section 1 before you need it.
