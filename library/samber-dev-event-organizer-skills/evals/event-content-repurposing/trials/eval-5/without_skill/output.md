# Verdict: publish B after two checks. Hold A and C until they're fixed. 🟠

I'm not a lawyer. This is a risk read, not legal advice.

| Clip | Call | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A: Spotify track, 40 s | ❌ Hold | Fair use is weak here. |
| B: competitor dashboard | 🟢 Likely OK after checks | Low copyright risk. |
| C: analyst chart | ❌ Hold | Paid-report licenses forbid reuse. |

## Two points that apply to all three

1. **The editor's fair-use claim doesn't carry the weight it's being given.**
   - Fair use is a US defense. A court weighs four factors after a dispute starts, so it never pre-clears anything.
   - The conference being nonprofit doesn't matter. The question is how *you* use the clip. If it promotes your brand or channel, it counts as commercial.
   - "Short" only helps if the excerpt is not the heart of the work. A and C fail that test.
2. **"Worst case YouTube mutes it" is not the worst case.**
   - Content ID can also block the video, in whole or in some countries, or put a claim on it that takes the revenue.
   - A rightsholder can send a manual takedown, which gives your channel a copyright strike. Three strikes terminate the channel.
   - LinkedIn, X and other platforms don't auto-mute. They just take the video down.

## Clip A: Spotify track playing for 40 s ❌

- A licensed recording and its underlying composition are both protected. Forty seconds of unaltered, audible music is a large, non-transformative use.
- The track plays *through the best moment*. That is the strongest argument *against* "incidental capture", because the music is part of what the viewer watches.
- Muting the audio would also kill the moment you chose the clip for.

Fix, in order of effort:
1. Re-cut around the music, or use only the stretch before or after it.
2. Replace the audio under the demo with a licensed or royalty-free track, and keep the speaker's voice if you can separate it. This is hard when the music is baked into the room mic.
3. Ask the speaker whether they can re-record the demo without music.
4. License the track. This is rarely worth it for one clip.

## Clip B: competitor dashboard on screen 🟢 (conditional)

A competitor's UI shown during someone else's demo is normally low risk. Show it incidentally, don't present it misleadingly, and keep the competitor's trademark use factual.

Check before publishing:
1. **Customer or personal data on the dashboard.** Names, emails, revenue figures and API keys are the real risk. If any are readable, blur them or drop the clip.
2. **Misleading framing.** Don't title or caption the clip in a way that implies a claim about the competitor, such as "X is broken" or "X can't do this". That risks disparagement or false-advertising complaints, and a screenshot of their UI can't back it up.
3. **Speaker framing.** If the speaker trashes the competitor on camera, decide whether you want your brand to republish it.

## Clip C: chart from a paid analyst report ❌

- Analyst firms license reports under strict terms and actively enforce reprint rules. Reproducing a chart usually needs written permission and often a fee.
- The speaker may have had permission to show the slide to a live room. That doesn't extend to you republishing it online.
- The fair-use factors lean against you. You'd be copying a whole chart, which is the paid product's core value, and a free clip can substitute for buying the report.

Fix:
1. Blur or crop the chart, or cut to the speaker only while the chart is up.
2. Ask the analyst firm's permissions or reprint team for written approval and a price. Slides usually carry attribution, which tells you who to ask.
3. Ask the speaker whether their own data supports a replacement chart.

## Also check, for all three

- **Recording rights.** Confirm that ForgeData Summit's recording terms or speaker releases let you republish the footage. If the conference or the speaker owns it, you need their OK before any of the three go out. This is the cheapest check and the easiest to skip.
- **Speaker consent.** Get a quick written "yes" from each featured speaker.

## Next steps

1. **Today (about 15 min):** email the conference organizers and the speakers of A and B to confirm republication rights.
2. **Today:** scan B's frames for customer data. Blur if needed, then publish B.
3. **This week:** send the editor a re-cut brief for A (music-free or re-sourced audio) and C (chart blurred or cut). This is about an hour of editing, not a lost day.
4. **Only if you want C unmodified:** request analyst-firm permission and wait for a written reply.

If A or C is high-stakes, such as a paid campaign or a large channel you can't risk, spend 30 minutes with a copyright lawyer before publishing.
