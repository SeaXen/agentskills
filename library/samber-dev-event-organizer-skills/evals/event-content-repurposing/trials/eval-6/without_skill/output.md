# Which photos to pick for the KernelBloom Fest carousel

I can't see the 900 photos, so I can't name specific frames. I can give you a selection rule that avoids the opt-out problem, and I'd change one assumption in your plan first.

## ⚠️ "We're covered" doesn't hold for close-ups

- **The sticker was a promise.** You told attendees that a red dot meant "don't photograph me." Publishing a face you can't verify against that promise breaks your own commitment, whatever the law says.
- **Silence isn't consent.** Nobody complained, but that only tells you nobody saw a problem yet. People who opted out may not know the photos exist, and the carousel is the first time they'd find out.
- **A code of conduct isn't a lawful basis.** If attendees are in the EU or UK (I'm assuming France, going by where you're based), a sharp, recognizable close-up used for marketing needs consent or a documented legitimate-interest assessment. The assessment is hard to win here because you ran an opt-out system and can't prove it was honored. Photography clauses usually cover taking pictures, not promotional publication.
- **Instagram makes it hard to undo.** The post can be screenshotted and reshared, so removal after a complaint doesn't fully fix it.

I'm not a lawyer. If you want this confirmed, ask your DPO or counsel one question: "Can we publish identifiable close-ups when our opt-out log is incomplete?"

## ✅ Safe-first selection (no clearance needed)

Pick these first. They carry the energy marketing wants and don't need per-person checks:

1. **Backs, hands and over-the-shoulder shots** of workshop tables: laptops, whiteboards, gesturing hands.
2. **Wide crowd shots** where nobody is identifiable.
3. **Speakers and staff on stage** or in official roles, if they signed a release or agreed in writing.
4. **Blurred or silhouetted** hallway moments, such as motion blur or distant figures.
5. **Details**: swag, the venue, the badges' design (with names unreadable), food, signage.

## 🟠 Close-ups you actually want: clear them one by one

Use this process for the laughing-at-the-table and hallway-conversation shots.

1. **Cull to a shortlist of 15 to 20 photos**, at most 3 people each. A short list makes clearance feasible.
2. **Zoom in on every badge** in each shortlisted photo.
   - A red dot means the photo is out.
   - A badge that is turned away, hidden or illegible means you can't verify, so the photo is out unless you get consent.
3. **Identify each person** from your registration or attendee list, or ask the photographer who they remember.
4. **Get written consent** from each person you can reach. A short email works: "We'd like to use this photo on Instagram. Reply yes to approve. No reply means we won't use it."
5. **Keep a log** of photo ID, person, consent date and channel. This is the per-photo record you're missing today.
6. **Publish only the photos where every identifiable person said yes.**

Consent is opt-in, so a non-reply means no. People who laughed at the workshop tables are usually happy to say yes if you ask, and a tagged post often gets more reach.

| Slides | Type | Clearance |
|---|---|---|

| 2–4 | Hands, laptops and whiteboards at the workshops | None |

| 9–10 | Venue detail plus a "see you next year" slide | None |

## ⏳ Next steps

1. Ask the photographer for the full-resolution set. Badge dots are rarely legible at web size.
2. Send the shortlist and the consent email, and wait about a week for replies.
3. Put a short line in your next attendee email or event page: "Want a photo removed? Reply to us and we'll take it down within 48 hours."
4. For next year's event, give opt-outs a wristband or lanyard color that's legible from 3 meters, and have the photographer log every portrait. That removes this problem.
