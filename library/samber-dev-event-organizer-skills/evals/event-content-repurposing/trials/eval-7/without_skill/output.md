# Verdict: ❌ Not as written. Keep the goal, change the plan.

**Next action:** don't publish anything under CC BY yet. First fix the three problems below. A CC licence can't be taken back for copies people have already downloaded.

## 1. The TED premise is wrong

- ⚠️ TED uses **CC BY-NC-ND 4.0**: non-commercial and no derivatives. It does not use CC BY.
- So TED isn't evidence that CC BY is the standard. Conference practice is mixed: some use CC BY, some NC-ND, some "all rights reserved, free to watch".
- CC BY lets anyone resell, remix, excerpt and re-edit the talks, including for ads or paid courses. Decide whether you want that. Your speakers may not.

## 2. Last year's speakers: you can't license what you don't hold

- ❌ A release that covers "publication" doesn't grant a CC BY licence. That licence allows adaptation, commercial reuse and sublicensing.
- Speakers keep the copyright in their talks and slides.
- For that third of the speakers, pick one:
  1. Ask them for a new release that explicitly allows CC BY. Make it opt-in and keep a written record.
  2. Publish their talks under the old terms only, marked "© speaker, published with permission".
  3. Leave them out of the open-licensed set.
- Fix the release template now. For future years, include the CC grant, a warranty about third-party content, and a note that it is irrevocable.

## 3. Vendor screenshots: you can't license them either

- ❌ Product UI, logos and screenshots belong to the vendors. Putting CC BY on the file implies you hold rights you don't.
- Options per talk:
  1. Get written permission from the vendor.
  2. Blur or cut the screenshots in the open-licensed cut.
  3. Keep the licence but add an explicit exclusion: "Third-party material shown in this video is not covered."
- Option 3 only works if that notice sits on the file or page, which leads to the next point.

## 4. Don't keep the licence only in the spreadsheet and press kit

- ❌ A licence nobody can see isn't a working licence. A reuser who finds a video or photo has no way to learn the terms.
- CC BY needs reusers to give attribution and keep notices. They can only do that if the notice is attached to the file or its page.
- The exceptions (speaker-restricted talks, vendor material) are the items that most need a per-asset label. The spreadsheet can't carry them to the public.
- You don't need to stamp watermarks on files. Use light, machine-readable marking:
  - **Pages:** a licence footer plus `license` metadata (schema.org) on each transcript page.
  - **Videos:** a licence line in the description or credits, and the file metadata.
  - **Photos:** IPTC/XMP rights fields with the licence URL, plus a licence line on the gallery page.
  - **Exceptions:** a per-asset "not covered" line where it applies.
- Keep the spreadsheet as your internal source of truth. Add columns for licence, speaker consent date, third-party content flag and attendee-photo status.

## 5. Photos: a missed issue

- ⚠️ CC doesn't clear privacy or image rights for people shown. If you're in the EU, GDPR applies. Attendees recognisable in a CC BY photo set can be reused by anyone.
- Check these before publishing:
  - The photographer's contract grants you the right to license under CC BY.
  - Your attendee notice or consent covers open-licensed publication, not just "event photos".
  - Sponsor logos and badges are not an issue, or are excluded.
- Safer: license only crowd-free or speaker-consented photos as CC BY. Keep the rest under "all rights reserved, press use".

## Suggested licence split

| Asset | Suggestion |
|---|---|
| Talks, speakers who opt in | CC BY 4.0 (or BY-NC-ND if you want TED-style protection) |
| Talks, last year's speakers, no new consent | Not CC. Publish under existing terms |
| Transcripts | Same licence as the matching talk |
| Photos | CC BY only for cleared images, with IPTC rights fields |
| Vendor screenshots | Cut, or explicitly excluded |

## Questions for you

1. Do you want to allow commercial reuse and remixing (BY), or follow TED (BY-NC-ND)?
2. Can you re-contact that third of speakers before publication?
3. Do you know what your attendee photo notice said?

This is a licensing-practice review, not legal advice. Have a lawyer check the new speaker release and the photo consent wording before you publish.
