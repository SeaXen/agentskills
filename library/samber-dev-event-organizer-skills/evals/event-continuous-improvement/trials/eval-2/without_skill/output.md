# DevHarbor Conf: operational changes to lift retention

## What the numbers say

| | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attendees | 610 | 580 | 495 |
| Returning (derived) | n/a | ~275 (45% of 610) | ~174 (30% of 580) |
| New (derived) | 610 | ~305 | ~321 |

- **Acquisition held.** New attendees stayed at about 305–320 a year.
- **Retention is the problem.** Returning attendees fell about 37% (275 to 174), and that drop explains the whole attendance decline.
- **The sponsor loss probably follows from it.** Fewer returning attendees means a less engaged audience and weaker sponsor ROI. I'm inferring this, since I have no sponsor feedback.
- **Check-in did not cause the drop.** Check-in took about 90 minutes in all three editions, while retention moved from 45% to 30%. A constant can't explain a change. It is still a real, unfixed problem and a poor first impression.
- **We don't know why people left.** Nothing you gave me covers satisfaction, lapsed-attendee reasons, or sponsor exit reasons. The first step below fixes that.

## Changes, in order

### 1. Find out why people left (do first, ~2 weeks)
1. Survey the ~406 year-two attendees who didn't return. Ask 4 questions: main reason, what would bring you back, rating of check-in, rating of content.
2. Call both sponsors that declined. Ask what they measured and what would change their answer. Aim for 20 minutes each.
3. Pull per-session ratings and attendance from year three, if you have them. Look for sessions or tracks that emptied out.
4. Decide on the rest of this list after the results come in. Items 2–4 below are the safe bets either way.

### 2. Fix check-in (cheap, certain, long overdue)
1. Set a target of 15 minutes or less from arrival to badge, and a maximum queue of 10 minutes at peak.
2. Pre-print badges and sort them by last-name range.
3. Send each attendee a QR code by email in advance, and scan it at entry.
4. Open check-in the evening before, plus early morning, to spread arrivals.
5. Staff lanes to the arrival curve: staff for the peak 30 minutes, not an even spread.
6. Time it live and record it in the retro. Your notes show 90 minutes in every edition, which suggests nobody owns this fix.
7. Assign one named owner and review the result at the next retro.

### 3. Build a year-round connection, so attendance isn't a once-a-year event
1. Start a community channel (Discord or Slack) for past attendees, and open it right after the event.
2. Run 2–3 low-cost off-season touchpoints, such as a meetup, talk recordings, or a monthly digest.
3. Release session recordings and slides on a schedule, and use them to prompt "come back" messages.

### 4. Make returning worth it
1. Offer past attendees early-bird access before general sale opens.
2. Give a loyalty price or a bring-a-colleague discount.
3. Announce dates and a first speaker line-up while the event is still fresh, ideally at closing.
4. Tell returning attendees what changed because of their feedback.

### 5. Sponsor retention, driven by the calls in step 1
1. Give each sponsor a post-event report: leads, booth traffic, session attendance.
2. Offer a pre-renewal check-in 90 days after the event, before budgets lock.
3. Improve the sponsor-to-attendee match once step 1 tells us which sponsors felt out of place.

## Metrics for next year
- **Retention:** returning share of prior-year attendees. Target is back to 45% or better. The 30% figure is the baseline.
- **Check-in:** time from arrival to badge, measured live. Target is 15 minutes or less.
- **Survey response:** at least 30% of lapsed attendees answering.
- **Sponsor renewals:** count renewed, with a named reason for each decline.
- **Net promoter or satisfaction score:** collected at the event, so there's a baseline for next year.

- The returning and new counts are derived from the percentages. Check them against your registration data.
- Items 2–4 are reasonable defaults, not proven causes. Step 1 should confirm or overturn them.
