# ForgeFest editions 2–5: trend read and improvement plan

**Limits first:** I haven't seen your debrief doc, and I skipped `skill-wip/` as you asked. ForgeFest has one edition, so there is no year-over-year trend yet. What exists is a pattern across your events: sound, late speakers and sponsor logos fail every time. I treat that as the finding. Every number below is a target to confirm against your debrief, not a measurement.

## 1. What the pattern says

Three failures that recur across a meetup, a hackathon and a 200-person festival are not bad luck. They are missing systems.

| Recurring failure | Likely root cause | Why it repeats |
|---|---|---|
| Sound | Nobody owns it. No venue tech-check, no named audio person, no fallback mic or laptop audio. | Each event is a fresh venue, and the fix lives in someone's memory. |
| Late speakers | No arrival contract. Speakers get logistics too late and nobody holds a buffer. | Lateness is only noticed on the day, when it can't be fixed. |
| Sponsor logo mishaps | No single asset pipeline. Logos arrive in mixed formats via email and get placed by hand in several places (site, slides, signage). | Every placement is a new chance for an error, and there is no sign-off. |

Common thread: the problems appear at the handoff between people. You have three organizers and three recurring handoff failures. That suggests ownership is shared, which in practice means nobody has it.

**Scale risk:** 200 people was manageable by improvisation. A bigger edition 2 will turn "went okay" into visible failures, because improvisation doesn't scale.

## 2. Prioritized plan

Ranking is by recurrence times visibility times cheapness of the fix.

### Priority 1: before edition 2 planning starts

1. **Assign one named owner per recurring failure.** Sound, speaker logistics and sponsor assets each get one of you. They own the checklist, not just the task.
2. **Sound system:**
   - Hold an on-site tech check at least one day before, with every speaker's laptop and adapter.
   - Name one audio person present for the whole event.
   - Bring spare mic, batteries and a DI/audio cable. Keep a fallback for a dead mic.
   - Put audio requirements (PA, mics, monitors) in the venue contract.
3. **Speaker lateness:**
   - Set the speaker arrival time at least 60 minutes before the slot, in writing.
   - Send a logistics pack 7 days before and a reminder 24 hours before.
   - Take a mobile number for the day itself.
   - Keep a flex slot or a lightning-talk standby in the schedule.
   - Decide in advance who gets cut or swapped when a speaker is more than 15 minutes late.
4. **Sponsor logos:**
   - Collect one SVG or high-resolution PNG per sponsor by a hard deadline.
   - Store them in one shared folder, the only source for site, slides and signage.
   - Send each sponsor a proof of their placement for sign-off.
   - Use the sponsor's own brand guidelines, and keep a list of every place each logo appears.

### Priority 2: edition 2 delivery

5. Write a run-of-show with a time, an owner and a fallback per line.
6. Run a 30-minute dry run with all three organizers the day before.
7. Hold a 15-minute hot-wash right after the event, while memory is fresh. Add anything new to the tracker.
8. Recruit volunteers into defined roles (stage, doors, speaker wrangler), with a briefing.

### Priority 3: editions 3–5

| Edition | Focus | Gate to proceed |
|---|---|---|
| **2** | Close the three recurring failures. Keep scale roughly equal to edition 1 so you test the systems, not growth. | Zero repeats of the three failures, or each repeat has a logged cause. |
| **3** | Grow only if edition 2 passed the gate. Add a paid AV vendor if sound still failed. Formalize sponsor tiers with deliverables and deadlines. | Attendee and sponsor feedback, plus organizer workload still sustainable. |
| **4** | Reduce dependence on the three of you: volunteer leads, a written playbook, venue relationships reused. | Someone other than the three of you runs one stream end to end. |
| **5** | Decide the long-term shape: size, format, funding, and whether it stays volunteer-run. | A review of four editions of data, which gives you the first real trend analysis. |

**Real multi-year analysis becomes possible after edition 3.** Until then, use the tracker to build the dataset.

## 3. Airtable: a good idea with one fix

A tracker works only if someone is accountable for reading it. "The team owns reviewing it" tends to mean nobody does, and that's how your current failures persist. Fix: give review a named owner and a fixed time.

**Suggested tables**
- **Issues:** date, edition, category (sound, speaker, sponsor, other), severity, cause, fix, owner, status, and "recurred?" (yes/no).
- **Checklists:** per edition and per category, linked to the owner and due date.
- **Speakers:** confirmed date, logistics pack sent, arrival time, mobile, status.
- **Sponsors:** tier, assets received, proof sent, sign-off date, every placement.
- **Metrics:** registrations, attendance, start-time delay in minutes, feedback score.

**Review rules**
1. Hold a monthly 30-minute review, with a weekly one in the last 6 weeks before an event.
2. Each category owner presents their own open items.
3. Any issue marked "recurred" gets a root-cause line before it can be closed.
4. Run a full retrospective after each edition.

**Cautions:** speaker and attendee data are personal data under GDPR. Keep contact details minimal, restrict access, and set a deletion date. Also, start with the Issues table and the checklists. A simpler start gets used more.
