## 1. Close out the three changes

**You can't separate them.** Badge, catering and later start all changed in the same edition, so one set of reviews can't say which one helped. Anyone who picks a winner is arguing, not measuring. Don't let the team pick one.

**Do this in the close-out:**

1. Split the reviews by question. Ask which survey questions touch which change.
   - Badge: networking, finding people, check-in.
   - Catering: food rating, lunch queue.
   - Later start: first-session attendance, arrival stress, energy.
2. Read each change only against its own question, and against editions 2 and 3 on the same question. A catering change shouldn't move the networking score. If networking rose, look at the badge, not the catering.
3. Check the confounds, which are on every change at once: speaker lineup, audience mix, response rate, weather, venue. List them and don't explain them away.
4. Record one verdict per change:
   - **Keep** = the matching question moved and nothing harmful appeared.
   - **Revert** = the matching question dropped, or cost or complaints rose.
   - **Unknown, keep by default** = no clear signal. Say "effect unproven" in writing.
5. Write one log entry per change: hypothesis, expected metric, observed, verdict, cost, date. Keep the log in the planning folder so edition 5 inherits it.

**Expect "unknown" for at least one change.** The data may support it. Better to record "unknown" than a story that gets cited for years.

**Edition 5 rule: one change at a time.** If you must test several, run them side by side inside the event so each has a comparison group:
- Two badge designs by row or day.
- Two catering lines.
- A different start time per track, if the venue allows.

Decide the expected metric for each change **before** the event, in writing.

## 2. The risk scoring sheet

Past page 1 nobody filled it in. That is evidence the sheet is too long and tied to no decision. A reminder won't fix that.

1. Check which risks on pages 2 and later actually happened in editions 3 and 4. Anything that did bites for real. Keep those.
2. Cut the sheet to **one page**: the top ~10 risks, with an owner, a trigger and a mitigation for each. Drop the scoring maths unless someone uses the score to decide something.
3. Run a **30-min pre-mortem** at kickoff ("edition 5 failed, why?") to seed the list. Risks people name aloud are more likely to be owned than risks in a template.
4. Review it at set points: kickoff, T-8 weeks, T-3 weeks, T-1 week. Don't ask for continuous updates.
5. If the whole sheet turns out unused, delete it. Don't revive a dead template out of habit.

## 3. The consultant's advice

The consultant is **half right**. Adopt the cadence, but not OKRs with improvement targets on outcomes.

**Why the targets fail here:**
- You get one data point per year. A quarterly review has nothing new to read.
- Review scores are noisy at event volumes and confounded, as edition 4 just showed. A "+0.3 on satisfaction" target will be hit or missed by chance.
- Targets on a score invite gaming: survey timing, who gets asked, soft questions. Strathern/Goodhart: a measure that becomes a target stops being a good measure.
- An improvement target also pushes the team to change many things at once to hit it. That is exactly how edition 4 got confounded.

**What to adopt instead:**

1. **Quarterly checkpoints tied to the edition timeline, not to outcomes.** Examples: Q1 close-out and decisions, Q2 venue and speakers, Q3 logistics and risk review, Q4 event and survey. Each checkpoint reviews progress and decisions.
2. **OKRs on what the team controls.** Examples: survey redesigned and approved by date X; the single edition 5 change defined with its metric by date Y; the one-page risk sheet owned by T-8 weeks; response rate above a baseline-derived floor.
3. **Outcome metrics as tracked ranges, not targets,** for the first two cycles. Take 5–8 at most (satisfaction, per-change question scores, attendance, first-session fill, response rate). Label each threshold "baseline" (from your own history), not an invented industry number. Pair each with a counter-metric. For example, pair satisfaction with response rate so a score can't rise just because unhappy people stopped answering.
4. **Kill rule:** drop any metric that changed no decision in two cycles.

Ask the consultant what a quarterly review would decide when the data arrives annually. Their answer shows whether they planned for an annual event.
