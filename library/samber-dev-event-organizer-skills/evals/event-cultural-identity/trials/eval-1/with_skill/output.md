# ForgeConf Vibe Guide (draft v0.1)

Two things changed from your brief, because they would have undone the goal.

- **One personality, four volume settings.** Giving each channel its own personality is how you got here: playful site, formal email, solemn MC. That is three small identities. This guide fixes one register for ForgeConf, and each channel section below says how loud that register gets in that place.
- **"Welcoming but professional, fun but serious" is not a register.** Every axis sits at the midpoint, so it can't settle a disagreement between the website writer and the MC. I picked a lean for each axis below. It's the part you most need to check.

**Assumptions** (I couldn't ask, so please correct them):

- ForgeConf sits on the **community pole**: volunteer-run, sponsors pay to support the event, and the attendee is a peer, not a lead.
- Nothing here comes from your past editions, so the examples are illustrative. Swap in real ones from your own history where I've marked them.

## 1. Purpose sentence (provisional)

> ForgeConf is where 400 people who keep production running trade the stories behind the dashboards, in a room volunteers built, so the person next to you is a peer, not a prospect.

Test: could this describe any other DevOps conference? The "peer, not a prospect" clause is what makes it yours. Edit it until the board would defend every word.

## 2. The register: where you lean

| Axis | Lean |
|---|---|
| Irreverent ↔ formal | **Irreverent**, with one exception: safety and incidents |
| Insider ↔ newcomer-welcoming | **Newcomer-welcoming**. A first-timer must get every joke. |
| Spontaneous ↔ choreographed | **Spontaneous in the middle, scripted at the opening, the closing and any incident** |
| Austere ↔ lavish | **Austere**. Volunteer-run, and the budget goes to content and care. |

"Professional" means competent and reliable: the schedule is right, the sign is accurate, the reply arrives on time. It does not mean stiff. Tone is no longer a way to look professional, which settles the email question below.

### Four attributes

**1. Warm, but never cliquey.**
- Do: address people as peers. Explain in-jokes in a few words. Say "you" more than "we".
- Don't: use inside references without a gloss. Say "as everyone knows". Gate-keep on seniority.
- Example: "First time here? Welcome. The hallway is the real track." *(illustrative)*
- Anti-example: a "legacy YAML survivors" in-joke on the lanyard that only veterans get. *(replace with a real one from your history)*

**2. Dry-witted, but never at anyone's expense.**
- Do: aim humor at situations (pagers at 3 a.m., "it worked on my machine", the Friday deploy). Make it self-deprecating. Make it survive being read aloud.
- Don't: mock a person, a stack or an employer. Joke about outages that really hurt someone. Use a meme a newcomer can't decode in three seconds.
- Example: "Wi-Fi is provided. Prayer is optional." *(illustrative)*
- Anti-example: a meme that mocks a specific vendor's product. It's unsafe with a sponsor in the room and it excludes people who use that tool.

**3. Plainspoken, but never sloppy.**
- Do: short sentences. Dates, times and rooms exact. Typos fixed. Dutch and English both correct.
- Don't: corporate-speak ("leverage", "synergy", speakers as "content"). Stack exclamation marks. Pad.
- Example: "Doors 08:30. Keynote 09:30. Coffee is on the ground floor." *(illustrative)*
- Anti-example: "We are delighted to inform you that registration shall commence…"

**4. Serious about craft, never about ourselves.**
- Do: care visibly about speaker prep, accessibility, accurate schedules and the CoC. Take the work seriously.
- Don't: pose, flatter the organizers, or turn the stage solemn to seem important.
- Example: a speaker brief that says exactly what A/V they get and when.
- Anti-example: a stiff MC reading sponsor boilerplate to a room that came for a hallway conversation.

"Fun but serious" was always two things. Fun is the tone (attributes 1 and 2). Serious is the standard (attributes 3 and 4).

### Banned register

- Corporate-speak ("leverage", "synergy", "world-class", "thought leadership").
- Memes or in-jokes that a first-timer can't read in three seconds.
- Humor in any incident, safety, accessibility or CoC message.
- "Valued sponsor" press-release phrasing.
- Exclamation-mark whimsy in logistics.
- Joking at the expense of a person, tool, vendor or job title.

## 3. Channel sections

The **volume dial** is the only thing that changes between channels. The register stays the same.

| Channel | Humor | Formality of structure | Primary job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Website | High | Low | Make a stranger feel invited |
| Email | Low-medium | Medium | Get the right thing done, with warmth |
| MC | Medium (high at the opening, zero in an incident) | Scripted at the edges | Set the room's tone |
| Signage | Low | High | Be understood in two seconds |

### Website: dial high

**Personality:** the host at the door. Humor lives in headlines, microcopy and 404 pages.

- Lead with the purpose sentence, not the sponsor logos.
- Memes pass the **three-second rule**: a first-timer gets it, it targets a situation and not a person, and it needs no platform context.
- Keep the practical parts plain: dates, venue, price, CoC link, accessibility info, schedule. Never put a joke where a fact should be.
- Sponsor pages use your voice. Describe what the sponsor does and why they're supporting a volunteer-run event.
- Never joke on registration, refund, CoC or accessibility pages.

Before: "Welcome to the premier DevOps experience." After: "400 people who keep production running, one room in Rotterdam, no keynote sold to a sponsor." *(Only claim "no keynote sold" if it's true.)*

### Email: dial medium-low

**Personality:** the competent friend. Warm, short and exact.

**The sponsor cc is the actual problem, so fix the cause.** Formal tone is a symptom. Three options:

1. **Recommended:** stop cc'ing sponsors on attendee, speaker and volunteer emails. Send sponsors their own thread, in the same voice but with sponsor-specific facts (booth logistics, deadlines, attendance numbers).
2. If a cc is unavoidable, write the email you'd send to a peer. A sponsor reading it learns what the event is like. Formal prose misrepresents that.
3. Use formal wording only for contracts and invoices, where precision is the point.

Rules:
- Subject lines say the content, then optionally one light touch.
- Open with the thing the reader must do or know. Don't start with "I hope this email finds you well".
- One dry line per email at most. None in logistics-critical, billing or incident emails.
- Sign with a human name, never "The ForgeConf Team".
- Post-event thank-yous: specific and short. Say what the person did, not "your participation was valued".

### MC: dial medium, scripted at the edges

**Personality:** the host who's also a peer. This is where your brief most needs reversing. A "serious and professional" MC contradicts attribute 1 in the room where people judge you hardest.

**Hosting model (recommendation):** use **one or two organizer-hosts, not a professional MC.** They already embody the culture, and the cost is low. Move to *distributed hosting* (a different organizer introduces each block) only if "everyone's conference" is part of the identity you want to claim. A hired host would arrive knowing nothing about you, and the culture transfer costs more than it saves.

- **Open cold.** The first minutes are a story, a demo or a real moment. No wifi, no fire exits, no sponsor reel. Logistics get their own later slot.
- **Pop-up rules** (2–4, announced in your own words). Candidates, to choose from or replace:
  - No feigning surprise.
  - No well-actuallys.
  - No blame: describe failures by what the system allowed, not who did it.
  - Ask the question you think is dumb.
- **Sponsors:** thank them in your voice, in one or two plain sentences. No reading of copy.
- **Closing:** design a real final moment, such as thanking the volunteers by name or a last short story. A conference that just stops reads as running out of time.
- **Incident script (write it before the day):** the humor drops entirely. Plain, direct, factual: what happened, what to do now, when the next update comes. "Never unsafe" outranks "dry-witted". Improvisation gets this wrong under pressure.

**One-page host brief** (hand it over and rehearse once):
1. Purpose sentence.
2. The four attributes and the banned list.
3. Pop-up rules and the exact words for announcing them.
4. Opening beat sheet.
5. Closing ritual and who owns it.
6. Pronunciation list (speakers, Dutch place names, sponsor and tool names).
7. Incident script.
8. Run-of-show handoff.

### Signage: dial low, clarity first

**Personality:** a helpful local with good timing. A sign is read in two seconds from five meters away, so clarity beats jokes.

- Primary line: plain and exact. Optional second line: one dry touch.
- Never joke on: exits, safety, accessibility routes, quiet rooms, restrooms, CoC and reporting contacts.
- Sponsor signage: their name and logo in your typography and wording.
- Everything proofed in Dutch and English if you use both.
- Quiet room: warm and unambiguous, not jokey.

Example:
- Primary: "Registration → Hall 2"
- Second line, permitted here: "Badge first, coffee second." *(illustrative)*
- Zero joke on: "Quiet room, 1st floor. No talking, no calls."
