# Verdict

**Partly right.** Hallway track: yes. Opening: change it. "Whānau": drop it. A community feel is honest for a vendor event only if you give up some control.

## 1. Drop "whānau"

- **It's a kinship concept, not a synonym for "extended family."** Whānau is tied to whakapapa (genealogy), mutual obligation, and mana. Using it as a label for a vendor's customers turns a living Māori concept into a brand asset.
- **Māori cultural appropriation is a live issue.** Brands that use te reo Māori commercially without Māori involvement regularly get criticized, in Aotearoa and elsewhere. The agency's pitch ("warmer than users") shows the word is being used as a mood, not a meaning.
- **"Family" is a poor frame for a vendor.** Family implies unconditional loyalty. Your attendees are customers who will want to complain about outages and pricing. The frame invites cynicism, and it works against the candor you want from a community event.
- **Fix:**
  - Ask the agency whether any Māori people or organizations were consulted. If not, drop it. If you still want a Māori-language element, that needs a paid Māori partner and a real relationship. Don't treat it as a naming exercise.
  - Skip the invented in-group name. Call people "attendees" or "builders" for year one. Let a name emerge from the community. A name your users already use for themselves is worth more than a new one.
  - Cancel the themed lanyards and gifts. Instead:
    - Use a lanyard that helps people talk to each other, such as "Ask me about: sharding / migrations / Postgres compatibility" stickers.
    - Make the gift something people use: a good notebook, a reference card, or a donation to an open-source project in each attendee's name.

## 2. Hallway track: yes, but it's a scheduling decision, not a feature

A hallway track only exists if the schedule leaves room for it. Production crews tend to fill every minute.

1. **Schedule slack.** Leave 30–45 minutes between sessions, plus one 90-minute unstructured block per day.
2. **Provide spaces.**
   - Open-space or unconference board, with slots people claim on the day.
   - Rooms for birds-of-a-feather topics.
   - Engineer office hours: "bring your schema, bring your slow query."
   - A quiet room.
3. **Keep sales out of it.** No badge scanning or booth-style lead capture in hallway areas. Make any scanning opt-in, at fixed locations only. Staff on the floor should wear a different badge color than customers, so people know who they're talking to.
4. **Seed it.** At 1,200 people at a first event, hallway tracks fall flat because nobody knows anyone. Before March, introduce 30–50 people to each other: a pre-event channel, topic threads, and a "who's coming from where" list.

## 3. The opening: replace it

A two-minute sponsor logo reel into a hype video is what a sales kickoff opens with. It sets the room's expectation in the first 120 seconds, and everything after reads as either confirming or contradicting it.

- **Cut the logo reel.** Thank sponsors on screens between sessions and in the program, with one spoken line at most.
- **Cut the hype video to 60 seconds or drop it.**
- **Open with a person.** Options, best first:
  1. A customer or community member telling a real story, including something that went wrong and what they did about it.
  2. An engineer or maintainer walking through a hard problem the team solved this year.
  3. A short, honest "state of the community" from you: what we got wrong, what we changed because of your feedback.
- **Run the keynote as a conversation if you can.** A moderated talk with a customer beats a 30-minute product reveal.

## 4. Is a community feel honest for a vendor event?

**Yes, if you say who is hosting and prove it by behavior.** The risk isn't that a vendor runs it. The risk is dressing a marketing event as something else. Trust comes from what you let happen.

Honesty tests. Pass most of them or don't claim the feel:

- **Say it plainly.** "Cloudhaven hosts this event" appears in the first sentence of the site and the opening remarks. Don't hide the sponsor.
- **Open the program.**
  - Use a public CFP with at least a few reviewers from outside Cloudhaven.
  - Aim for at least half the talks from non-employees.
  - Pay speaker travel.
- **Allow candid content.**
  - Include at least one talk on migrating off Cloudhaven, or a "when not to use us" session.
  - Don't script customer talks, and let them mention competitors and failures.
  - Share roadmap trade-offs, including what you decided not to build.
- **Give people a voice with consequences.** A public feedback session where the team answers hard questions, and a follow-up post within 30 days saying what changed.
- **Have a code of conduct** with named responders, published in advance.
- **Keep sponsors out of editorial.** Sponsors get a booth and a logo. They don't get keynote slots or pitch talks.

## 5. The tension to settle with leadership now

The event is marketing-funded, so someone will ask for pipeline numbers. Lead-scan targets will fight everything above. Agree on success metrics before you finalize design:

- Sentiment and trust (post-event survey, with questions about whether it felt like a sales event).
- Retention and expansion among attendee accounts versus a matched group that didn't attend.
- Community outcomes: new contributors, meetup sign-ups, speakers who return.
- Pipeline as a secondary, labeled metric, not the headline.

If leadership insists on pipeline as the main number, call it a customer conference. That's a respectable thing to run. Just don't label it a community event.
