# ByteBazaar culture health check

**Verdict: ⚠️ The culture you advertise is not the culture you run.** The site promises "by the community, for the community, everyone's conference." The on-site signals describe a sponsor-and-speaker-centred conference with one real community moment, the Thursday party.

This is an impression from the six facts you gave me. I have no attendee survey, volunteer feedback, or CoC incident history.

## Signal by signal

| Signal | Reads as | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| 4-minute sponsor reel, hired pro MC | Opening ritual belongs to sponsors, and the host is a stranger to the community | 🔴 |
| Platinum sponsor gets the Friday keynote | The most-attended slot is sold, not earned | 🔴 |
| Sponsor-logo polos, unisex S-XL, guessed quantities | Swag is advertising, and the sizes exclude people. Guessing wastes money and runs out | 🟠 |
| Speakers get a green room and dinner, volunteers get standing sandwiches | The class system is visible. The people who make it "by the community" are treated as the lowest tier | 🔴 |
| Two paragraphs of legal boilerplate for a code of conduct | There is no reporting path, response team, or enforcement. Attendees at risk can't tell whether anyone will act | 🔴 |
| The Thursday party is the biggest budget line, and attendees rave | You spend the most on what the community values most | 🟢 |

## What's working

- The party is strong evidence that you know how to build belonging. Attendees rave about it unprompted, and you fund it first.
- Five editions is a track record. The conference has real demand and a base to build on.

## What's not

1. **Volunteers are the sharpest gap.** "By the community" is mostly literal here: volunteers are the community doing the work. Feeding them sandwiches while speakers get a dinner tells them where they rank. Volunteer burnout and churn usually show up first, and they hit your capacity to run the event.
2. **The code of conduct is the highest-risk item.** Boilerplate with no named contact, no reporting channel, and no stated consequences protects the organisation on paper and no one on the floor. It's the one gap where a single bad incident can damage the whole brand.
3. **The brand claim is now a liability.** "Hacker spirit" invites attendees to compare it against what they see. A sponsor reel, a bought keynote, and logo polos are what that audience tends to notice and mock.
4. **Unisex S-XL swag** quietly excludes anyone outside that range. That's a small signal, but it adds up with the CoC.

## Is the sponsor money the problem?

No. Sponsors fund the party and the conference, and that's legitimate. The problem is that sponsors get **editorial and ritual space** (the opening and the keynote), not just visibility. A sponsor can have a booth, a logo, and a clearly labelled talk without owning the moments that set the tone.

## Priorities

1. 🔐 **Rewrite the code of conduct.** Add a named contact, a reporting channel, response steps, and consequences. This is cheap and the highest risk reduction.
2. **Give volunteers parity with speakers.** At minimum, a seated meal and a thank-you. This costs little compared with the party.
3. **Move the keynote to a community-chosen speaker.** Give the platinum sponsor a clearly labelled slot elsewhere.
4. **Cut the sponsor reel to under a minute, or replace it** with a community opening. Consider a volunteer, organiser, or attendee host.
5. **Fix swag.** Offer a cut that fits more bodies, order by pre-registration size, and put the sponsor logo small.

## Bottom line

The culture works in one place, the party, and fails in the places that matter most for trust: who is welcomed, who is protected, and who is fed. Either close the gap by fixing items 1 to 3, or soften the website copy to match what you run. Fixing the practices is the better option, because attendees' enthusiasm for the party shows they want the community version.
