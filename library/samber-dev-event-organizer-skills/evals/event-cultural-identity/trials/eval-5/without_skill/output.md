# Kernelhaus host: recommendation and prep plan

## Recommendation

**Your co-organizer holds the mic.** Don't pay the €3,000 MC. And don't let her wing it either.

Last year's failure was a host with no incident protocol, not a host who wasn't funny. Hiring a pro doesn't fix that. A professional corporate-launch MC is arguably worse here, for three reasons:

- **He is unlikely to have the authority or context.** A corporate MC's job is hype and smooth transitions. A CoC incident mid-talk needs someone who speaks as the organizer and knows the community's rules and norms.
- **He doesn't know the room.** 300 systems people at a single-track, volunteer-run event want a host who is one of them. Polished stage patter can read as off-register.
- **The money is better spent elsewhere.** €3,000 across 300 at-cost tickets is about €10 per ticket. You could spend part of it on a stage manager, a backup-demo setup or a CoC response team.

Her being "great off the cuff" is a strength for filling gaps and handling a quiet room. It is a liability in an incident, where improvisation is exactly what failed last year. So the prep below removes improvisation from the one moment where it hurts.

## The structural fix: separate the roles

1. **Host.** Runs the day, introduces speakers, keeps time.
2. **CoC incident lead.** A different person who handles any report or incident. Ideally add a second responder.
3. **Stage manager or floor lead.** Handles the practical side: kills the projector, calls a break, escorts people.

The host should never be the sole incident handler. If your co-organizer is also your CoC lead, name someone else as the primary responder for conference day.

## Incident protocol (the host's only job in an incident)

**The rule: no jokes during an incident.** Humor resumes only after the incident is closed, and only at the host's own expense, or not at all.

1. **Pause.** The host says one flat, pre-written line, for example: *"Let's pause here for a moment. Thank you."* There is no commentary and no reading the room aloud.
2. **Hand off.** The host signals the CoC lead, who takes it away from the stage, to the hallway or a side room.
3. **Offer a break.** *"We'll take a ten-minute break and restart on time."* Slides and the demo wait.
4. **Resume.** The host returns with one neutral sentence, for example: *"Thanks for your patience. We take our code of conduct seriously, and it's being handled. Back to the program."* Then they move on and don't riff.

Agree these exact lines in writing beforehand. The host reads them aloud in rehearsal until they come out flat and calm.

## Humor guardrails

- **Safe targets:** herself, the schedule, the venue, the coffee, tooling absurdity in general.
- **Off limits:** any person in the room, any speaker's work, anything about an incident, and any topic the CoC covers.
- **Test for a joke:** if she wouldn't say it with the CoC lead standing next to her, cut it.
- **Script the intros.** Keep each speaker intro to two sentences, written and approved by the speaker. Leave spontaneity for the gaps between talks.

## Speaker-side prevention

Last year's trigger was a live demo, so close that gap:

- **Brief every speaker** on the CoC, including what demo content (data, examples, prompts, jokes) is out of bounds.
- **Ask for a recorded fallback** of any live demo, ready to switch to.
- **Ask for a dry run** of demos that pull in live or user-generated content.
- **Give them a contact.** Tell each speaker who to find if something goes wrong on stage.

## Prep timeline

| When | Action |
|---|---|
| 6 weeks out | Name host, CoC lead, backup responder and stage manager. Write the incident lines. |
| 4 weeks out | Send speaker briefing. Collect demo fallbacks. |
| 2 weeks out | Finalize run-of-show and intro scripts. |
| 1 week out | **Run a 30-minute drill.** Simulate a mid-talk incident: the host says the pause line, the CoC lead takes over, and the stage manager calls the break. Repeat once with the roles swapped. |
| Day before | Walk the stage. Agree a silent signal between host, CoC lead and stage manager. |
| Day of | Brief the volunteers in 5 minutes: who to find, what to say, what not to say. |
| After | Debrief within 48 hours and update the protocol. |

## Before you decide: three checks

1. **Does she want the job?** Confirm she's comfortable with the drill and the no-joke rule. If she balks at scripted lines, that's a signal.
2. **Is she also your CoC lead?** If so, appoint someone else as primary responder.
3. **Is the room too big for a volunteer host to feel safe?** At 300 people single-track, it isn't.

## When to reconsider the pro

Change course only if she declines the drill, or if nobody else can fill the CoC lead role. A cheaper middle path is to ask the AV company whether their MC would do a half-day at a reduced rate. He would have to follow your incident protocol and script, and he gets no authority over incidents.

## Last year's incident, in one line

The joke failed because it treated a conduct issue as a mood problem. This year the host's job in that moment is to pause, hand off and say nothing clever.
