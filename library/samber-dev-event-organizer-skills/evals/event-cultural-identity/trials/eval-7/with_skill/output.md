## Verdict

❌ **Don't remove the wall.** ❌ **Don't book the mariachi band.**

You have it backwards. The wall is the signature tradition you're trying to buy. The mariachi band is the copy-paste the skill warns about.

## Why the wall is your tradition

- **Attendees invented it.** It appeared two years running, unprompted, near the coffee station. The skill's mechanic is to institutionalize what attendees already invented. PyCon did this when it adopted the attendee-coined "hallway track" into its official language. You have the same raw material.
- **It fits your event.** A conference called ShipItCon, where people stick up their worst production failures. That is a purpose claim: *we ship, we break things, we tell the truth about it.* The skill's test is whether the event's purpose could describe a competitor's event. A failure wall passes. A mariachi band fails.
- **It's the right ladder rung.** You run it yearly and plan to for a decade, and one edition is enough to have something to institutionalize. That makes it a compounding annual asset, so "one protected signature tradition" is the promotion target. Self-built ritual worlds like CCC took four decades and a standing volunteer corps. Don't aim there.
- **It's free and it works.** No band fee, no rehearsal, and attendees already do it. The skill's measure is tradition pull: unprompted mentions, and returners naming it as a reason to come back. Check your feedback forms and posts for how often the wall comes up. That is your evidence, and I haven't seen it.

## Why the mariachi plan fails

1. **You'd copy the surface and skip the reason.** The DevOpsDays guide reserves its "Best" budget tier for "the fun things that make your event unique." Its example is a mariachi band at lunch in Austin. The reasoning is *spend on one moment that is locally flavored and unique to your event*. The band was Austin's answer to that, not a template. I can only source that the band happened and why the guide budgets for such moments. "It was a hit" is your reading and I couldn't verify it. If ShipItCon isn't in a place where mariachi is local, it's a transplant. The skill names this exact case as a failure mode: "a copied tradition without its reason... a mariachi band."
2. **It risks spectacle outshining purpose.** The skill's retro check is whether attendees remember the party but not the point. A lunch band that people photograph does exactly that.
3. **Budget is not a reason.** HashiConf's rule is that extras are subordinated to purpose. Having the money doesn't make this the right purchase.

## The real problem: "professional"

The press photos embarrass you, and the fix you chose is to remove the wall. I think "look more professional" is a hedge. It is the skill's midpoint trap ("welcoming but professional, fun but serious"). Removing the wall would also make the event more generic, since a generic event is the one thing attendees can't tell apart from the next.

The actual problem is that the wall looks chaotic in photos. That is a presentation problem, and you can solve it without deleting the tradition.

## What to do instead

**Protect and design the wall:**

1. **Name it and give it a home.** Pick a name that fits the event, such as "The Post-Mortem Wall" (my placeholder, rename it). Use a dedicated, designed panel, not whatever surface is near the coffee. Give it a header, a clear prompt, and a good marker and sticky-note supply.
2. **Set pop-up rules, 2-4 of them.** For example:
   - No blame, and no naming individuals.
   - No customer names, no secrets, nothing under NDA.
   - Say what you learned, not only what broke.
   - Add one if you want, then retire it if it isn't used.
3. **Add a deliberate closing moment.** The skill says gatherings "simply stop" unless you design the end. Read a few notes aloud in the closing, or have the MC pick three and say what each taught. That turns the wall from a corner joke into the event's emotional close.
4. **Handle the photo problem separately.**
   - Put a photo and press policy in the code of conduct's culture layer, such as a line on whether note text may be photographed.
   - Brief your photographer to frame the wall and pick shots where individual notes aren't legible. The wall as a mass reads as culture, and the individual notes don't have to be readable.
   - Curate a few notes for press, with the writer's consent.
5. **Check the confidentiality and legal exposure.** Notes describing real production failures may contain sensitive information. Rule 2 above covers this. For legal, ask your counsel or sponsors' comms teams whether sticky notes need any policy. I can't answer that from the skill.
6. **Cover who wrote the wall's content.** Sponsors and speakers may be on it. Decide in advance whether any content is off-limits, and write that down.

**If you still want an extra moment:** the skill allows reserving budget for one locally flavored moment. Derive it from your own city or community, not Austin's. Run it only if it passes the purpose test above, and treat it as a separate decision from the wall.

## Measure it next year

Set these thresholds before the event, not after:

- **Tradition pull:** unprompted mentions of the wall, and returners naming it. If nobody mentions it two editions running, replace it.
- **Identity echo:** the share of posts describing the event in your register's words, not "great talks, nice venue."
- **Retro:** do attendees remember the point, or only the spectacle?
