# Antenna Conf 3: culture plan

Two things to fix before the plan itself. Both change what you do in the next few weeks.

## 1. Two corrections

**The purpose statement describes a format, not a culture.** "Two days of talks, workshops, and networking" is true of every conference. It doesn't say how people should treat each other, or who should feel at home. Section 2 derives the culture from your statement and from what is specific to you: wireless and RF developers, community-run, third edition.

**Don't decide what good looks like after you see the numbers.** If you wait, any result can be read as success: "ok, 4.1 is fine for a community event." Three steps keep you honest:

1. Write each metric's formula and threshold down before ticket sales open. Commit it to a doc with a date.
2. Take baselines from editions 1 and 2. If you have no survey data from them, mark the metric "baselining". Edition 3 then becomes the baseline, with no pass/fail verdict this year.
3. Mark where every number came from: published, self-set, or unsourced guess.

**Ticket speed and next-year return rate are weak culture measures.** Both are real signals, so keep them, but as context, not as the verdict:

- Ticket speed moves with price tiers, when you announce the lineup, how much you promote, and the date and venue. Culture is one small factor among these.
- Return rate lags by a year. It is also driven by travel budgets, employer approval, and date clashes.
- Neither tells you whether people felt welcome or met anyone. A conference can sell fast and have a cold hallway.

The sheet in section 4 pairs them with measures that respond to the culture work directly.

## 2. Culture, derived from your purpose statement

I read three values out of the statement. Each has observable behaviors, so you can check it without guessing at feelings.

| Value | Source in your statement | Observable behaviors |
|---|---|---|
| **Everyone who builds wireless software belongs here** | "wireless developers", with no seniority or employer filter | Newcomers get a named welcome. Beginner questions are answered without condescension. Talks avoid unexplained jargon and acronyms. The speaker lineup isn't only the organizers' network. |

Add one non-negotiable layer, because the community is open and run by volunteers: a **code of conduct** with a named contact and a published response process. Without it, the three values are only intentions.

## 3. The plan

### Before the event (T-12 weeks to T-0)

1. **Write a culture one-pager** from the table above. Put it on the site and in every speaker, sponsor, and volunteer briefing.
2. **Publish the code of conduct.** Include:
   - two named contacts, one of whom is not an organizer
   - an anonymous reporting route
   - a stated response time you can actually meet
   - what happens after a report
3. **Brief everyone who is visible:**
   - speakers: no gatekeeping, define terms, leave Q&A time
   - volunteers: how to welcome and how to escalate an incident
   - sponsors: conduct rules apply to booths and swag
4. **Recruit first-timer hosts.** Ask returning attendees to be "familiar faces" for newcomers. Offer a free pre-event video call or chat thread: "first time? say hi."
5. **Widen the speaker pipeline.** Send an open CFP and review it with anonymized submissions. Reserve a share of slots for first-time speakers, and offer them a talk-review buddy.
6. **Lower the barriers to entry.** Offer a small number of free or discounted tickets for students and underrepresented attendees. Publish accessibility details early: step-free access, quiet room, captioning if you can afford it, dietary options.

### During the event (two days)

1. **Opening, 3 minutes:** state the culture and the code of conduct from the stage. Say how to reach the contacts, and say it plainly.
2. **Welcome and mixing:**
   - badge stickers for "first time"
   - a hosted newcomer table at the first break
   - one structured mixer, such as topic tables ("SDR", "antenna design", "embedded") so people have a reason to talk
3. **Protect hallway time.** Keep breaks at 30 minutes or longer, and don't let overruns eat them.
4. **Workshops:** one helper per roughly 8-10 participants, so nobody is stuck alone. This ratio is a starting guess, so adjust it to your format.
5. **Visible conduct response.** Brief your contacts and make sure they walk the floor, not just sit at a desk.
6. **Run a 3-question pulse on day 1** (a QR code on the badge), so you can fix problems on day 2.

### After the event

1. **Send the full survey within 48 hours**, while memories are fresh. Keep it short (under 5 minutes) and anonymous.
2. **Send a lapsed-attendee question** to people who registered but didn't show: "What got in the way?" People who left or never came hold information the happy respondents don't.
3. **Publish a short "what we heard and what we'll change" note** within 3-4 weeks. This tells attendees that feedback matters, and it improves the response rate next time.
4. **Keep the community channel active** between editions with a monthly prompt or a small meetup. Then the networking value continues.
5. **Review at 90 days:** a short follow-up survey on connections and collaborations that actually happened.

## 4. Measurement sheet

Eight metrics at most. Each has a formula, a source, a baseline rule, a threshold rule, and an owner. Fill the owner and the numbers yourself.

| # | Metric | Formula | Source | Baseline | Threshold rule (write before ticket sales open) | Action if it misses |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Welcome (first-timers)** | % of first-time attendees agreeing "I felt welcome" (5-point scale, top 2 boxes) | Post-event survey | Edition 2 if you have it, else "baselining" | Not lower than edition 2, and gap vs returning attendees ≤ 10 points (self-set) | Review the newcomer hosting and the opening |
| 2 | **Connections made** | % of attendees who met ≥ 3 people they didn't know | Post-event survey | Same | Not lower than the edition-2 value | Add structured mixing, review break length |
| 3 | **Conduct confidence** | % agreeing "I knew how to report a problem and trusted it would be handled" | Post-event survey | Same | Not lower than baseline | Re-brief contacts, make the process more visible |
| 4 | **Conduct response time** | Hours from report to first human reply | Incident log | Edition 2, else "baselining" | Reply within the time you publish (e.g. same day on-site) | Add on-call coverage |
| 5 | **Speaker openness** | Share of talks from first-time speakers and from outside the organizers' network | CFP and program data | Edition 2 | Do not fall below edition 2 | Reopen the CFP, outreach |
| 6 | **Recommendability** | % answering 9-10 on "How likely are you to recommend Antenna Conf to a colleague?" | Post-event survey | Edition 2 | Not lower than the edition-2 value | Read open comments, fix the top 2 issues |
| 7 | **Ticket-sales pace** (context) | % of capacity sold at fixed days before the event (e.g. T-60, T-30, T-7) vs edition 2 | Ticketing data | Edition 2 curve | Read alongside a log of price changes, announcements, and promotion | Do not conclude from this alone |
| 8 | **Return rate** (context, lagging) | Of edition-2 attendees, % who attend edition 3. Report first-timers and repeaters separately. | Ticketing data | Edition 1 to 2 return rate | Read against a log of date, venue, and price changes | Interview lapsed attendees |

Notes on reading the sheet:

- **Survey response rate matters.** Report it next to metrics 1, 2, 3, and 6. A 90% score from 15% of attendees says little. Below roughly 30 responses in a segment (self-set floor), show counts, not percentages.
- **Satisfied people answer more often.** Metric 1 only counts respondents, so also follow up with non-respondents and no-shows.
- **Thresholds are self-set, not industry standards.** I'm not giving you benchmark numbers on purpose. Compare to your own history, not to other conferences.
- **Don't use sentiment classifiers on free-text comments.** Read them yourself. Blunt technical feedback gets mislabeled as anger.
- **Privacy:** report aggregates only. Don't publish per-person responses, and tell respondents how the data will be used.
