# LoopWorks Community Standards

**Version:** 1.0 draft for board review
**Applies to:** LoopWorks conference (about 250 people), all official venues, online channels, and side events
**Owner:** [Conduct lead name], with the [organizing committee]

## A note for the board before you read

I made two decisions that differ from the proposal. Both are easy to reverse.

1. **One document, three layers.** The Code of Conduct (Part 1) stays short and stable. The procedures (Parts 4 and 5) and the policies (Parts 2 and 3) can change every year without reopening the values. Attendees read Part 1. Staff and volunteers also read the rest.
2. **The in-jokes are now a design requirement.** Your in-jokes are part of what makes LoopWorks feel like home, and I kept them. The risk is that a newcomer sees a room laughing at something they cannot decode. Part 2 keeps the jokes and makes each one explainable. Without it, the rest of the document would work against the goal of being newcomer-friendly.

Square brackets mark values you must fill in. I did not invent contact details, names, or legal terms. Have a lawyer review Parts 3 and 5 before publication, especially the photo policy and the data retention rules.

# Part 1. Code of Conduct

## Our promise

LoopWorks is a community conference. Everyone here is either part of the community or on their way in. We want a first-time attendee to leave feeling they belong, whether they arrived alone or know nobody.

## Expected behavior

- **Be kind and assume good faith.** Ask before you conclude.
- **Include people.** Invite the person standing alone into your conversation. Leave circles open.
- **Explain, don't gatekeep.** If you use a community reference, say what it means.
- **Respect boundaries.** Ask before touching, photographing, or following up. Accept "no" the first time.
- **Respect the venue, staff, and volunteers.**
- **Speak up safely.** If you see something, you may report it. You never have to confront anyone.

## Unacceptable behavior

- Harassment, intimidation, or discrimination based on age, disability, ethnicity, gender identity or expression, neurotype, nationality, race, religion, sexual orientation, appearance, experience level, or any other personal characteristic.
- Deliberately misgendering someone after they have told you their name or pronouns.
- Sexual language or imagery in talks, slides, badges, stickers, or conversation.
- Unwelcome physical contact, stalking, or persistent unwanted attention.
- Photographing or recording someone against their stated preference (see Part 3).
- Sharing private information about someone without consent.
- Disrupting talks or events.
- Retaliating against anyone who reports in good faith.
- Using "it's just an in-joke" or "it's just banter" to excuse any of the above.

## Who this applies to

Everyone: attendees, speakers, sponsors, volunteers, staff, and organizers. Sponsors and speakers are held to the same standard in booths, talks, and swag.

## Consequences

Violations are handled under Part 5. Consequences range from a private conversation to removal from the event without refund, and a ban from future editions.

# Part 2. Social rules for sessions and shared spaces

## 2.1 Session norms (for all talks, workshops, and panels)

1. **Questions are welcome at any level.** Moderators actively thank beginner questions. Nobody says "as everyone knows" or "obviously."
2. **Q&A is moderated.** Moderators take questions from different parts of the room, not only the first raised hands. Questions are also accepted through [anonymous channel, e.g. a shared Q&A tool].
3. **Wait for the microphone.** Questions are short and end in a question mark. The moderator may cut off a speech that is not a question.
4. **Phones and laptops are fine.** People may take notes, step out, or rest. Nobody needs a reason to leave a session.
5. **Sit anywhere.** Reserve the first two rows of every session for people who want a closer view or have accessibility needs. Do not reserve them for "regulars."
6. **Silent spaces and noisy spaces.** Mark at least one room as low-stimulation and quiet at all times.
7. **Speakers get a conduct briefing.** Every speaker receives this document and the in-joke rules (2.2) at least [2 weeks] before the event.

## 2.2 Keeping the in-jokes without excluding newcomers

The in-jokes stay. We follow the rule **"every reference must be explainable in ten seconds."**

### For organizers

- **Publish a "LoopWorks Decoder."** It is a one-page glossary of in-jokes, nicknames, recurring bits, and badge symbols. Each entry has two lines: what it is and where it came from. Send it with the attendee confirmation email, print it on the back of the schedule, and put a QR code on the registration desk.
- **Badges.** Every in-joke badge element (icons, titles, ribbons, colors) is listed in the Decoder. Give each one a short caption or key on the back of the badge or the lanyard card. Add one **"first time here"** badge marker, such as a ribbon. Veterans can use it to start conversations. It never replaces a name or an invitation to participate.
- **Opening slides.** Treat the opening as a newcomer's first impression. Show no more than one unexplained reference per slide. A host says one sentence aloud for each one ("For anyone new: the [X] is…"). Turn the explanation into a ritual so the veterans enjoy retelling it.
- **A newcomer's welcome.** Run a 20-minute **"First-timers' orientation"** before the opening. It covers the schedule, the Decoder, the conduct team, and the quiet room. End with [a host-led introduction round or a buddy assignment].

### For speakers

- Explain any community reference on first use, in one short sentence, before using it as a punchline.
- Put a plain-language summary of the talk's context on the first or second slide.
- Do not use an in-joke at the expense of a person, a first-time attendee, or a group.
- Talks that depend on shared history must say so in the abstract ("assumes familiarity with X"), so newcomers can choose with full information.
- The program committee gives feedback during review when a talk relies on inside references.

### Buddy program

- Newcomers can sign up for a buddy at registration. Buddies are returning attendees who agree to meet them on arrival, sit with them for one session, and introduce them to three people.
- Buddies are trained for 30 minutes on this document.

## 2.3 Names and pronouns

- **Badges have a pronoun field. It is optional.** It offers a few preprinted options (she/her, he/him, they/them, and a blank) plus stickers. Nobody is required to display pronouns or to explain their choice.
- **Never assume.** If you do not know someone's pronouns, use their name or "they."
- **Correct yourself gracefully.** If you misgender someone, say "sorry, [correct form]" and move on. Do not make it a drama.
- **Do not ask people to justify** their name, pronouns, or identity.
- **Speakers and hosts** are asked to share their own pronouns if they want to, never to ask the audience to do so.
- **Names.** A chosen name appears on the badge. Registration offers a "badge name" field that is separate from the legal name.

## 2.4 Alcohol, evenings, and social events

- Every social event has non-alcoholic options that are equally visible.
- Nobody is pressured to drink, stay late, or join an activity.
- Evening events state an end time and a quiet exit route.
- Hosts of after-parties receive the conduct briefing and know how to reach the conduct team.

## 2.5 Accessibility

- Venue information (step-free routes, quiet rooms, seating, lighting) is published before registration closes.
- Slides use readable font sizes and captions where possible. Live captioning is provided for [main stage].
- Attendees can request accommodations at registration or from the team at [contact]. Requests are kept confidential.

# Part 3. Photo and recording consent policy

## 3.1 Principle

Photography should be an invitation, never a surprise. **If someone is identifiable in a photo or video, they should have chosen it.**

## 3.2 The badge system

Every badge has a colored dot, sticker, or lanyard. The same system is explained on signs at every entrance.

| Marker | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 🟢 Green | Photos are fine. Still be courteous, and ask when you take a close portrait. |
| 🟡 Yellow | Ask first. Photograph me only after I say yes. |
| 🔴 Red | No photos. Keep me out of the frame. |

- The default at registration is **yellow**. Attendees can change it at any time at the help desk.
- Photographers, official or otherwise, must check the marker before publishing a photo that identifies someone.
- Red-marked attendees may sit in designated areas that photographers avoid. Mark those areas on the floor plan.

## 3.3 Official photographers and recorders

- Official photographers wear a visible identifier and are briefed on this policy.
- They do not photograph people in restrooms, quiet rooms, or while eating or drinking where it is avoidable.
- They never publish red-marker photos. If a red-marker person is incidentally in a wide shot, they are removed before publication, or the image is not used.
- Official recordings of talks are announced in the program. Audience members in the front rows are not intentionally filmed. Signs at the room entrance say where cameras are.
- Attendees may ask the team to remove an image of themselves at any time, for any reason. The team acts within [2 working days] and removes it from channels it controls. Anyone who asks gets a reply.

## 3.4 Attendee photography and social media

- Photos of slides are fine, unless the speaker says otherwise.
- Do not post photos that identify others without their consent. If in doubt, crop or ask.
- Do not post photos of children, or of someone in a vulnerable moment, even with a green marker.
- Livestreaming or streaming from your own device requires explicit organizer approval.

# Part 4. Incident reporting channel

## 4.1 Design goals

- **Easy to find.** Printed on the badge back, the schedule, and every room entrance.
- **Easy to use.** A newcomer who is scared, new, and alone can still report.
- **Multiple paths.** Not everyone feels safe approaching a person.
- **Fast.** Someone responds quickly, with a visible commitment.

## 4.2 How to report

Choose whichever feels safest:

| Channel | How | Response target |
|---|---|---|
| **In person** | Find any team member in a [color] shirt, or go to the Conduct Desk at [location]. Staffed [hours]. | Immediate |
| **Phone/message** | [Number], reachable [hours]. During the event, also via [chat tool]. | Within [10 minutes] |
| **Email** | [conduct address], received by at least two people | Within [24 hours]; within [2 hours] during the event |
| **Anonymous form** | [URL/QR code]. Anonymous reports may limit what we can do, but we read all of them. | Reviewed at least [twice daily] during the event |
| **Report about a team member** | [Alternative address received by someone outside the organizing team, e.g. a board member or external contact] | Within [24 hours] |

You can report for yourself or for someone else. You do not need to be sure it violates the code. You do not need evidence.

## 4.3 Who responds

- A **conduct team** of at least [3] trained people, with mixed genders and experience levels, and at least one from outside the organizing committee.
- Team members are identifiable and introduced during the opening.
- A team member steps back from any case in which they are involved or have a close relationship with someone involved. Another team member takes over.

## 4.4 What happens after you report

1. **Acknowledge.** The reporter gets a reply that confirms receipt and names a point of contact.
2. **Safety first.** If anyone is in immediate danger, the team acts first and documents later. This can include escorting someone, separating people, or calling venue security or emergency services ([emergency number]).
3. **Listen.** The reporter describes what happened, in their words. The team asks what the reporter wants to happen. It does not promise outcomes.
4. **Record.** Two team members write down facts, not interpretations.
5. **Decide.** Per Part 5.
6. **Follow up.** The reporter learns what category of action was taken, within what privacy allows. If no action is taken, the team explains why.

## 4.5 Confidentiality

- Reports are shared only with the people who need them to act.
- The reporter's identity is not shared with the reported person without the reporter's consent, unless the law requires it.
- Records are stored securely, access is limited to [named roles], and they are retained for [period] before deletion. [Insert applicable data protection details.]

## 4.6 Protection against retaliation

Retaliation against someone who reports in good faith is itself a violation and is handled as a serious one. Reports made in good faith are never penalized, even when they turn out to be mistaken.

## 4.7 Reports about the conduct team or organizers

Use the independent channel in 4.2. The organizing committee does not review these reports. [Name an external reviewer or panel.]

# Part 5. Enforcement

## 5.1 Principles

- **Proportionate.** The response fits the behavior and its impact.
- **Consistent.** Similar behavior leads to similar outcomes.
- **Safety over comfort.** The reporter's and community's safety outranks a harasser's convenience.
- **Fair.** The person reported gets to tell their side before a consequence beyond an immediate safety measure.
- **Documented.** Every decision has a written record and a reason.

## 5.2 The ladder, with skip rules

You proposed a three-strike ladder. It is a good default for minor and moderate issues. I added skip rules, because a strict "three strikes" system tells serious offenders they get two free violations.

| Step | When | Typical response |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Conversation** | First minor issue (a thoughtless remark, a missed consent check, an unexplained reference used at someone's expense) | Private, calm conversation with a conduct team member. Names the behavior, its impact, and the expectation. Logged. |
| **2. Formal warning** | A repeat of the same issue, or a first moderate issue | Written warning that states what happened, what is expected, and that further violations lead to removal. May include a requirement such as an apology if the affected person wants one, or a talk removal. Logged. |
| **3. Removal** | A repeat after a formal warning, or a first serious issue | Asked to leave the event and any related spaces. No refund. Sponsors and speakers can lose their slot or booth. Banned from [defined period] or permanently. Logged. |

**Skip the ladder entirely** and go straight to removal, with a possible ban and referral to law enforcement, for:

- Physical or sexual assault, or threats of violence.
- Stalking or sustained harassment.
- Sexual harassment, including after being told to stop.
- Discriminatory harassment aimed at an individual or group.
- Retaliation against a reporter.
- Any behavior that puts someone in immediate danger.

**Counts reset?** A step 1 or 2 record stays on file for [two editions] and then expires, unless a new incident occurs.

## 5.3 Who decides

- The conduct team recommends. For steps 2 and 3, **two team members** must agree.
- For bans longer than the current event, the decision requires [the conduct lead plus one board member who is not involved].
- During the event, the team can apply **immediate temporary measures** (a seat change, a time-out, an escort out) before any investigation. These are safety measures, not punishments.

## 5.4 Appeals

- The person sanctioned may appeal in writing within [14 days].
- Someone not involved in the original decision reviews the appeal. [Name the role.]
- A pending appeal does not lift a ban or a safety measure.
- The reporter may also ask for a review if they believe the response was inadequate.

## 5.5 Conflicts of interest

A person with a personal, professional, or financial relationship with either party recuses themselves. Prominent members of the community get no special treatment. The team notes any recusal in the record.

## 5.6 Transparency

After each edition, publish a short **transparency summary**: the number of reports, categories, and outcome types, with no identifying details. This builds trust, and it shows newcomers that the system works.

# Part 6. Rollout checklist

**Before registration opens**
- [ ] Appoint the conduct lead and team. Train them (a half-day session with scenario practice).
- [ ] Fix the reporting channels in 4.2 and test each one.
- [ ] Publish this document on the website, linked from registration.
- [ ] Add a registration field for badge name, pronouns (optional), photo marker, accessibility needs, and "first time?"
- [ ] Have counsel review Parts 3 and 5 and the data retention rules.

**Six to eight weeks before**
- [ ] Draft the Decoder with veterans. Ask each person: "What would confuse someone new?"
- [ ] Redesign the badge with the key on the back, the photo marker, and the first-timer ribbon.
- [ ] Brief speakers and sponsors (2.1 and 2.2).
- [ ] Recruit and train buddies.

**Two weeks before**
- [ ] Send the Decoder and the conduct summary to all attendees.
- [ ] Review all slide decks for unexplained references.
- [ ] Brief photographers and volunteers.

**At the event**
- [ ] Run the first-timers' orientation.
- [ ] Introduce the conduct team in the opening and say aloud how to report.
- [ ] Hold a daily 10-minute team check-in.

**After**
- [ ] Debrief with the team, and survey newcomers: "Did you understand the references? Did you feel welcome?"
- [ ] Publish the transparency summary.
- [ ] Review and update this document. Version it with a changelog.

## Measuring whether it worked

Add three questions to the post-event survey, sent separately to first-time and returning attendees:

1. "I felt welcome at LoopWorks." (1 to 5)
2. "I understood the references and jokes, or I knew where to find an explanation." (1 to 5)
3. "I know how to report a problem and would feel safe doing it." (1 to 5)

If the first-timer scores trail the returning scores by more than about 0.5 points, the in-joke layer is still excluding people. Revise the Decoder, the badge, and the opening before the next edition.
