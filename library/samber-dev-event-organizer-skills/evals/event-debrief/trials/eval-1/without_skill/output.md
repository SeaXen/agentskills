## Is he right?

No, but his instinct has a fair core.

- **Where he's right:** an outage post-mortem exists to find a failure. No failure means no post-mortem.
- **Where he's wrong:** a debrief isn't only for failures. The closer work-world analogy is a sprint retro or after-action review, which also captures what worked, near-misses, and what to repeat.
- **"Nothing went wrong" is only what the three of you saw.** You haven't asked the 350 attendees, speakers, or sponsors.
- **Near-misses and workarounds are invisible in the incident count.** A venue fix or a favour that saved a session counts as nothing went wrong, but next year it may not be there.
- **Your exhaustion is a finding.** If three people are wiped after a flawless event, the workload split is the problem to fix, and no incident will flag it.
- **Memory fades fast.** Why you chose a vendor or a schedule order is in your heads. Planning starts in about 5 weeks, and by then much of it is gone.
- **Success hides what to keep.** If the budget landed on plan, write down why, so you can repeat it.

## Lightest version (about 3.5 person-hours total)

1. **This week, 10 min each, solo, async, in one shared doc.** Answer three prompts:
   - What should we keep exactly as is?
   - What almost went wrong, or took more effort than it should have?
   - What drained me, and what would I hand off or drop?
2. **This week, 30 min for one person.** Send attendees a 5-question survey. You're at day 8, so it's already late.
   - Overall rating, 1 to 5.
   - Best session or moment.
   - One thing to change.
   - Would you come back, yes or no?
   - Anything else (optional).
3. **Around week 3, a 45-min call for all three.** Read the doc and survey results first, then produce one page:
   - Top 3 changes.
   - One thing to stop doing.
   - One owner per change.
   - Any workload fix.
4. **Pin that page at the top of the planning doc.** It becomes the input to the kickoff.
