# What went wrong, and what to do differently

## What went wrong

The 15 items were never decisions. They were observations written as intentions.

1. **No owner.** "Team" means nobody. Nobody was asked, so nobody could say no, and nobody noticed when nothing happened.
2. **No deadline.** "Before next year" has no date to miss. Nothing came due, so nothing felt late.
3. **No trigger.** Most items only matter at a specific point in the cycle. "Improve speaker AV checks" matters in the weeks before the event, in about September. In the previous October it was a sentence in a doc. By September, nobody was looking at the doc.
4. **Items weren't tasks.** "Improve AV checks" and "start outreach earlier" are goals. Nobody can start them on a Tuesday because nobody can tell what the first step is.
5. **Fifteen items with no ranking.** Nothing was cut, so every item competed with day-to-day work and lost. Volunteer capacity is finite, and 15 items spread it so thin that none got done.
6. **The doc wasn't connected to planning.** Planning kicked off without the debrief as an input. A document nobody has to open will not get opened.
7. **No verification.** Nobody checked in the middle of the year, and nobody checked at kickoff. The failure surfaced only after the event repeated it.

The three repeated mistakes confirm this. They didn't repeat because you lack knowledge. They repeated because the knowledge sat in a doc and not in the plan.

## Why the tool and monthly meetings won't fix it

Your co-organizer is right that you need follow-through. The proposed fix treats the symptom, though, and it has costs.

- A tool holds whatever you put into it. If you import the same 15 vague items, you get 15 vague cards with the same "team" owner.
- Twelve monthly meetings means a year of overhead to fix a problem that exists in about three places: ownership, dates, and triggers.
- Most of the year has nothing to review. The work is seasonal and concentrates in roughly Jun–Oct. Monthly reviews in Dec–Mar will be empty, and people will start skipping them.
- It adds a new thing to maintain. If volunteers already let a doc rot, a tool becomes a second doc that rots.

A tool can help later. Adopt one only after the items below are in place, and only if you hit a real limit with a shared spreadsheet.

## What to do this time

Do this in the three weeks before planning opens.

**1. Triage the 15 old items (one 60-minute session).**
For each item, decide one of three things:
- **Do it:** pick at most 5. Rank them by cost of repeating the mistake, and make sure each of the 3 repeated mistakes is among them.
- **Drop it:** say so explicitly and delete it. An honest "no" beats a zombie item.
- **Fold it into a process:** if it's really a checklist step, move it to the checklist (see item 5).

**2. Rewrite each kept item as a task.**
Every item gets:
- **One named person.** They agree in the room, not by assignment in a doc. Never "team".
- **A concrete first action.** For example: "Send AV spec sheet to all speakers and book a 15-minute tech check per speaker", not "improve AV checks".
- **A calendar date**, not a season. Work back from October: if outreach should start earlier, say "first sponsor emails out by 15 March".
- **A "done" test** someone else can check. For example: "all speakers have confirmed a tech slot by 1 September".

**3. Put the dates in the event timeline, not in a debrief doc.**
Merge the kept items into your master plan, the same schedule that holds venue booking and CFP dates. The debrief becomes the plan. If the item is in the timeline, it gets looked at when everything else does.

**4. Give each repeated mistake a trigger and a backstop.**
For each of the three repeats, write down:
- the date it must start,
- the person who starts it,
- a second person who gets a calendar reminder two weeks earlier and asks "has this started?"

The backstop matters because the original owner can go quiet. Someone else's reminder is cheap and catches it.

**5. Build a short pre-event checklist from past mistakes.**
Take the lessons that are really recurring steps (AV checks, outreach timing) and turn them into a dated checklist. A checklist is read every year. A debrief is read once.

**6. Replace monthly meetings with two or three checkpoints tied to risk.**
For example, one in about March (sponsor outreach started?), one in about June, one in about August (AV, logistics). Each is 20 minutes with one question: "Which dated items are late?" Anything late gets a new date or gets dropped on the spot. Outside checkpoints, people only talk when something is blocked.

**7. Change how you run the next debrief.**
- Cap action items at 5 to 7.
- Every item leaves the room with owner, first step, date, and done test, or it doesn't go in the doc.
- Within a week, merge them into the timeline from item 3.
- At the next kickoff, the first agenda item is "what did we do from last year's list, and what did we skip and why".

## How to settle it with your co-organizer

Their concern is legitimate: accountability needs a mechanism. Propose a trade.

- Try the cheap version above for this cycle: a spreadsheet or the existing timeline, named owners, dates, and two or three checkpoints.
- Agree on a test now: by a fixed date such as 1 March, at least 4 of the 5 items should be on track. If they aren't, you add the tool and the monthly cadence with evidence behind the decision.
- If a tool is adopted anyway, use it with the structure above. It will only be as good as the fields you fill in.
