## Why not wait

1. A code of conduct is about people, not the venue. Venue and date don't change it.
2. Nobody can report anything before the event. Early community members, speakers, and sponsors judge the event by what you publish now.
3. Speakers and sponsors often ask for a code of conduct before they commit. If you send CFP invites or sponsor pitches without one, you lose some of them.
4. Without one, you have no basis to act on early problems. Those include harassment in the announcement thread, in a Slack or Discord channel, or in a DM to a speaker.
5. A mission-critical rule written "closer to the event" gets written in a rush, usually by one person.

## Do now (blocks Friday)

1. **Publish a short code of conduct (about 45 minutes).**
   - Adapt an existing one. Good bases are the Contributor Covenant, the Berlin Code of Conduct, or the Geek Feminism and confcodeofconduct.com templates.
   - Cover these points: expected behavior, unacceptable behavior, scope (event, online channels, social media), how to report, and what happens after a report.
   - Mark venue-specific details such as security, medical help, and on-site contacts as "added once the venue is confirmed".
2. **Stop using your personal Gmail as the contact.**
   - Register a domain, or use the one you already have, and create two role addresses: `hello@` and `conduct@`.
   - `hello@` can forward to you, so you still check one inbox.
   - `conduct@` must reach at least two people from different companies.
   - Reason: a report about one organizer must not land only in that organizer's inbox. With four companies, one of you may be the person reported, or the reported person's colleague.
   - Personal Gmail also exposes your address to scrapers and spam. It breaks if you are away or leave the project, and it makes the event look like a side project.
3. **Decide who handles reports (15 minutes).**
   - Name two people.
   - Agree on this rule: if the report involves one handler or their company, the other handler takes it alone.
   - Write down what you can do: warn, remove from the event, ban, notify the venue or the person's employer.
4. **Fix the "no venue" wording.** Write "Rotterdam, date and venue to be announced" and add a way to stay informed, either a mailing list or an announce-only channel.

## Do before Friday if you can (about 1 hour total)

1. **Name the owner.**
   - Four companies with no shared entity means nobody owns the domain, the money, or the contracts.
   - Settle who holds the domain registration, the email accounts, and the social handles. Don't leave them in one person's name.
   - Settle who signs the venue and sponsor contracts and who carries liability.
   - Options in the Netherlands: a stichting or vereniging, a fiscal host, or one company acting as organizer under a written agreement. Agree this in writing, even if it is one page, before any money moves.
2. **Check the name "QueueCraft".** Search trademark registers (EUIPO, Benelux) and the domain and social handles. Renaming after launch costs more than checking now.
3. **Add a basic privacy notice if you collect emails.** A mailing list or interest form falls under GDPR. State what you collect, why, and who sees it.
4. **Publish a one-line neutrality statement.** Four companies run this. Say whether sponsors can buy talks or influence the program. Attendees will ask.
