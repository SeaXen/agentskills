# Faborate DevCon: two answers for Thursday's exec review

## 1. Do you need organizers from three companies?

**No. Don't go recruit outside organizers.**

- **The rule fits a different kind of event.** The three-company guideline is for community-run conferences that have no owner and no budget. Several companies share the risk and no single vendor controls the event. Faborate DevCon is company-funded and company-staffed, and you and your events manager are the organizers.
- **The real risk is credibility, not legal structure.** A vendor-run conference can look like a sales event. Outsiders can address that without being co-organizers:
  1. **External program committee**: 3-5 respected practitioners, ideally from non-competing companies, who review CFP submissions. This is the biggest credibility gain for the least commitment.
  2. **Public CFP**: publish selection criteria and a decision date.
  3. **Code of conduct**: publish it before you announce anything else.
  4. **Optional advisory seat or two**: security community leaders who can tell you when something smells like marketing.
- **Nothing is blocked on this.** You can lock the date, venue, budget and CFP timeline now. The committee needs weeks of lead time, so start recruiting it this month.

**Line for the exec review:** "We own the event. We're adding an external program committee for credibility. That doesn't change who is accountable."

## 2. Polishing the agenda

**I can make the brief look finished, but I won't make seven unconfirmed talks look confirmed.** I also don't have the brief file, so below is the structure to apply.

Here is why the "all nine look confirmed" version is risky:
- **Execs will plan against it.** Budget, headcount, promotion and sponsor conversations will all assume a nine-talk line-up. If five speakers decline, the problem shows up after Thursday and lands on you.
- **Seven people are named without having agreed.** If that document gets forwarded or screenshotted, those people are publicly attached to an event they haven't said yes to. That damages the relationships you'll need for outreach.
- **Slots and names for unconfirmed talks are invented precision.** Execs can't tell what's real, so a confident-looking page makes everything on it less trustworthy.

A clean status-tagged agenda still looks confident. Use a table like this:

| Slot | Talk title | Speaker | Status | Next step / date |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10:00 | *(title)* | *(name)* | ✅ Confirmed | none |
| 11:00 | *(title)* | *(name)* | ✅ Confirmed | none |
| 13:00 | *(title)* | *(name)* | 🟠 Invited, awaiting reply | follow up *(date)* |
| 14:00 | *(title)* | *(name)* | 🟠 Invited, awaiting reply | follow up *(date)* |
| 15:00 | *(title)* | *(name)* | ⏳ Not yet approached | outreach by *(date)* |
| … | … | … | … | … |

- **Add a one-line headline above the table:** "2 of 9 talks confirmed. 7 in outreach. All confirmations due by *(date)*."
- **Add a fallback line:** "If fewer than N are confirmed by *(date)*, we open a CFP for the remaining slots." The public CFP from question 1 doubles as this fallback.
- **Use ✅ for confirmed, 🟠 for invited, and ⏳ for not yet approached.** I used ✅ and ⏳ as they appear in your conventions, plus 🟠 for in progress.
- **Keep speaker names for unconfirmed talks inside the internal brief only.** Anything that leaves the building should show confirmed speakers and unnamed "topic slots."
- **Treat the time slots as a draft grid** unless the venue and format are locked.

This framing makes the brief read as a plan with a visible path to nine, which is stronger than a nine-row list the first question can unravel.
