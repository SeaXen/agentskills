# PulseConf two-track plan: sanity check

**Verdict: ⚠️ don't announce yet.** Your premise is wrong, and the plan doesn't solve what it targets. I haven't seen your submission or review data, so the numbers below are my inference.

## 1. The CFP isn't broken

- 105 submissions for 22 slots is about a 21% acceptance rate (4.8 submissions per slot).
- That is healthy. Well-regarded conferences often sit at 5–15%.
- High rejection of good talks means demand, not a broken process.
- ✅ Signs of a broken CFP: few submissions, weak ones, slow decisions, speakers complaining about opaque criteria, a lineup that doesn't match the pool.
- Check those instead. Don't treat the ratio as the symptom.

## 2. "Accept twice as much" does not follow from "many great talks"

- Doubling to 44 slots means accepting 42% of submissions.
- How many of the 105 scored above your bar? If it was about 30, you'd be filling 14 slots with talks you would have rejected.
- The cut at slot 22 is noisy. The talks ranked 23–30 are close to the ones ranked 15–22, but there probably aren't 22 more of that quality.
- Frontend and backend submissions are unlikely to split 50/50. One track may be thin.

## 3. What you'd lose

- 🔴 **Single-track identity.** Everyone sees every talk, and that is likely a reason people come. Two parallel tracks means each attendee misses half the content.
- 🔴 **Audience dilution.** With 300 attendees, each room gets about 150 unless attendance grows. Rooms feel empty and speakers get smaller audiences, so speaker satisfaction drops.
- 🟠 **Cost.** You'd need a second room, AV, volunteers, and double the speaker travel and hotel. Check this against your budget first.
- 🟠 **Weak split.** For a TypeScript audience, many attendees are full-stack. "Frontend vs backend" forces a choice that doesn't match them. If you split, pick themes instead, such as type-level, tooling, or runtime.

## 4. Cheaper ways to use the extra good talks

Pick one or two:

1. **Lightning block.** Ten 5-minute slots in the single track. This is the best fit and keeps one room.
2. **Shorter talks.** Use 25-minute slots to get from 22 to about 28 without changing format.
3. **Workshop or hands-on half-day.** Run it before or after the conference. It is also a ticket upsell.
4. **Satellite meetup or online series.** Invite strong rejects to speak there year-round.
5. **Rejection feedback.** Send a short note or a score band to every rejected speaker. It costs almost nothing.
6. **Waitlist.** Keep a ranked list to replace speakers who drop out. Some will.

## 5. Check these before you decide

1. How many of the 105 talks scored above your bar, by reviewer scores?
2. What was the frontend, backend, and other split of the submissions?
3. Is the conference sold out, and is there a waitlist? If not, a bigger program has no audience to absorb it.
4. What did attendee surveys say? Do they want parallel tracks, or do they value seeing everything?
5. What are the room, AV, and speaker costs of a second track?
6. Do you have the sponsor and budget headroom?

## Recommendation

- Keep the single track for edition 4.
- Add a lightning block, and optionally a workshop half-day, to use the surplus.
- If the data shows 40 or more strong talks and a growing audience, pilot one parallel half-day first.
- Announce only after steps 1–6 are answered.
- An early announcement sets expectations you'll find hard to reverse.
