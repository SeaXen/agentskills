### 2. A reversal benchmark has already fired

The skill names four reversal benchmarks. One is "the organizing team reports the work has become actively unpleasant." You described exactly that: you dreaded the production weekends, and your co-organizer half-joked about quitting.

The skill's rule is that any one firing means hold or shrink the next edition, not push through. Green demand doesn't override it.

The record backs this up. In two independent documented cases, organizer stress came before any financial trigger. XOXO doubled to about 2,300 and reversed the next year. Its organizers called administering it "actively unpleasant," and they accepted a roughly 50% production-budget cut to shrink. The organizer later said the smaller edition was "the most relaxed and most fun I've had in seven years running the event."

### 3. Ceiling diagnostic

You are not at saturation, because the waitlist proves there is more audience. You are not at channel decay either. This looks like **quality collapse** (a bigger edition degrading what the team valued), here in your own experience as organizers. The skill says that argues against growth even when demand looks green.

### 4. Reach vs intimacy

A hands-on hardware festival depends on tools, benches, mentors, and conversations over a half-built board. That is an intimacy event. Going to 400 would also add parallel tracks and more production, and that dilutes the thing people come for.

Brooklyn Beta is the cautionary case. It went from about 300 to a one-day 1,300-person format and heard "I'm just not sure it scales well." The skill deletes that kind of leap from the menu.

### 5. The lever: deliberate shrink to about 200

I'd pick exactly one mechanism from the non-growth menu, and I'd pick **deliberate shrink**.

**Rejected rungs:**
- **400:** it is a 43% jump, and the whole-product layer (bench depth, tooling, volunteers) didn't scale to 280 either.
- **Hold at 280:** it is the default and costs nothing, but it keeps the thing that made you miserable.
- **Application + lottery and group-purchase ban:** the first is a second mechanism, and the second only matters if group buyers start dominating.

**Why about 200:** it is a judgment call, not a documented figure. It is between 140, which you ran well enough to grow from, and 280, which broke you.

⚠️ **One condition flips this.** If the misery came from a thin volunteer bench, not from headcount, then shrinking won't fix it. In that case hold at 280 and fix the bench first. This is also the "no bench for the step" routing in the skill, and it points to `event-team-structure`.

### 6. Financial gate

I don't know your venue terms, so check these before announcing:
- **Deposit and attrition clauses:** a smaller headcount may leave you owing a room-block or F&B minimum shortfall. Venues rarely renegotiate these now.
- **Smaller room:** if one is available, take it.
- **Sizing rule:** size every commitment to the proven number (200), with no aspirational buffer.

Shrinking is also a pricing event. Revenue falls by about 28% if you keep today's price (computed from 280 → 200). Plan it explicitly; don't let the old price ride on a smaller product.

### 7. Pricing posture

- Decide it before the sale opens, and say it publicly.
- Default: hold individual prices flat, with sponsorship cross-subsidising accessible tickets (PyCon held prices flat through five years of larger venues).
- Optional: a supporter tier whose surplus funds other attendees' tickets.
- Price-setting mechanics belong to `event-ticket-pricing`.

### 8. Paired mitigation

A smaller room means a longer waitlist, and first-come-first-served favours people with time and money. Do these:
1. Keep first-come-first-served with a numbered waitlist.
2. Give waitlisted people priority next year.
3. Publish build guides or kit files so people who miss out still get something.
4. Explain the decision publicly: "we're keeping it hands-on."

If group buyers start dominating, add the group-purchase ban next. Don't start with it.
