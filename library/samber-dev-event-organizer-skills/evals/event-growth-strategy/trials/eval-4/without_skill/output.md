## Recommendation

**Add registration with a cap first. Treat a bigger venue as a later step that the registration data will justify.**

## Why registration first

- **You are guessing at demand.** "About 2,500 showed up" is a head-count estimate. Registration gives you real numbers: sign-ups, no-shows, and interest per session. You need those to size a venue or to ask sponsors for money.
- **Your actual problem is room-level crowding, not total attendance.** Popular rooms were impossible to enter and hallways were packed. A bigger venue doesn't fix a keynote-sized crowd trying to fit into a 60-seat room. Per-session reservation for the hot talks does.
- **It's cheaper, faster, and reversible.** A registration system costs a few weeks of volunteer time and little money. A paid venue means finding a budget, signing a contract, and taking on cancellation risk. Your volunteer team of about 30 can't easily absorb that.
- **Demand keeps growing, so a cap is needed either way.** Any venue fills eventually. Without a cap you hit the same problem again, only at a larger size and a higher cost.
- **It protects the free, open character of the event.** A paid venue usually pushes you toward sponsors with strings attached or toward ticket fees. Registration keeps entry free.

## How to avoid the usual downsides

1. **Keep it light.** Collect only an email and a name. Skip accounts and passwords. Ask for consent and state how long you keep the data.
2. **Plan for no-shows.** Free events typically see 30–50% of registrants not show up. Issue more tickets than capacity, and tune the ratio with your data. Send a "confirm or release your spot" email a week before.
3. **Use a waitlist** that auto-promotes people when others release their spots.
4. **Keep a walk-in reserve.** Hold 10–15% of capacity for same-day arrivals. This preserves the drop-in spirit.
5. **Reserve only the popular sessions.** The general entry cap covers the whole event. Add opt-in seat reservation for the 5–10 rooms that overflowed last time, and leave everything else first-come.
6. **Fix the hallways separately.** Add signage, one-way flow where possible, more seating and food points, and a livestream or overflow room for the biggest talks. These are cheap and help even with a cap.

## When to move to a bigger venue

Revisit the venue after one edition with registration data. Move if:

- sign-ups are consistently 2× or more your cap, and
- the waitlist is long even after you adjust the overbooking ratio, and
- you have a credible funding path (sponsors, in-kind venue from a university or company, or a city partnership).

Look for a free or in-kind venue before you pay for one. Universities, convention-center partnerships, and a sponsoring company's campus are all options.

## Next steps

1. Pick a ticketing tool. Options are Eventbrite, Tito, Pretix (open source, self-hostable, a good fit for your community), or Luma.
2. Set the cap from the venue's fire-code limit, not from last year's attendance.
3. Pull the list of rooms that overflowed last year and put reservation on those.
4. Decide your overbooking ratio and walk-in reserve. Start conservative and adjust.
5. After the event, publish the numbers (sign-ups, show rate, waitlist size). That becomes your sponsor and venue pitch.

One assumption to check: I assumed the current venue can't expand and that you have no budget today. If a larger venue is already available at low cost, the calculation changes, but I'd still add registration.
