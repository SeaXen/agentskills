# OrbitCon: the move to ~700 seats and the pricing

## Short answer

- **Move to ~700.** Don't raise prices 60% across the board.
- **Choose the base price** after checking the venue maths (below), which may show that no raise is needed.
- **Put the big increase on a company-paid tier**, and keep a community tier at roughly today's price.
- **Sign the venue only with downside protection**, and get the sponsor commitments in writing first.

## 1. Why the treasurer's argument doesn't hold

- **Selling out fast shows your price is below what the market would pay.** It doesn't show how much demand falls at +60%. You have never tested a higher price, so you have no elasticity data.
- **A 30-minute sellout is a race, not a measure of demand.** People buy "just in case", and some buy to resell. Some of the 200 on the waitlist would never have bought at a higher price.
- **The waitlist (~200) almost exactly fills the 200 new seats (700 − 500).** At today's price you may already be close to filling 700, with little excess demand left. A +60% raise on top of that could leave seats empty. That would be the first non-sellout in four editions, and it would hit the venue cost, the sponsors and the community mood together.
- **The raise may not be needed.** Check this first:
  - Extra contribution from the new seats at the current price = 200 × (P − v), where P is the ticket price and v is the variable cost per attendee (food, badge, swag).
  - If the venue's extra fixed cost ΔF is below that number, the move pays for itself with no raise.
  - A raise only has to cover the gap, and a gap is usually far smaller than 60%.
- **Community-run and cloud-native means many attendees are self-funded** (students, juniors, freelancers, people between jobs). A flat +60% prices out exactly those people, who are the core of the audience.

## 2. Pricing structure

Tiers capture the willingness to pay of company-funded attendees without punishing everyone else.

| Tier | Share of seats (illustrative) | Price | Who |
|---|---|---|---|
| Community | ~30% | ≈ today's price (P) | Self-funded, students, OSS maintainers, juniors |
| Standard | ~50% | ~P × 1.2 | Default |
| Supporter / company | ~20% | ~P × 1.6 | Employer-paid, no questions asked; includes a "thank you" on the website |

- **Illustrative revenue:** 140P + 420P + 224P ≈ 784P at full sell-out, against 500P today. That is close to the treasurer's 800P at 500 seats, with no loss of access.
- **Treat the percentages as hypotheses.** They aren't measured.
- **Release in waves, not one 30-minute race.** For example, 3 waves over 3–4 weeks, with some seats held back for sponsors, speakers and community tickets.
- **Let the waves test the price.** If the supporter tier sells out and the standard tier is slow, you have learned your price ceiling at no real risk. Adjust the prices of later waves.
- **Set price steps by release date.** Early waves are cheaper, later waves higher. This rewards committed attendees and reduces last-minute reselling.
- **Add anti-scalping and no-show rules.** Tickets are named and transferable only through your platform at face value. Refunds close at a fixed date, and refunded or released seats go to the waitlist automatically.
- **Keep the waitlist data.** Ask the ~200 people one short question: "What is the most you'd have paid?" This is cheap and beats guessing.
- **Don't change the price AND the format in the same edition without an easy way back.** Moving venue and pricing at once gives you two variables and no clean read on either.

## 3. Sponsors

Two unsolicited approaches is a good signal, but treat it as a pipeline, not a plan.

- **Get written commitments before the venue deposit.** Use them to cover ΔF or to fund community tickets.
- **Aim for sponsors covering part of ΔF, not all of it.** Heavy reliance on one or two companies changes who the event answers to.
- **Cap the number of sponsors and fix the tiers publicly.** Sponsors can't buy talk slots (keep the CFP independent).
- **Make the larger audience the pitch.** 700 attendees is worth more to sponsors than 500, so don't underprice the packages.
- **Use sponsor money for access.** Fund the community tier or a travel/ticket fund, so higher prices elsewhere don't cost you the community.

## 4. Setting up the venue move

1. **Compute the break-even fill rate** at each tier mix. Know whether you survive at 550, 600 or 650 paid attendees.
2. **Negotiate the downside:**
   - A staged deposit.
   - A flexible minimum guarantee for food and beverage.
   - A capacity reduction option, for example "700 seats, can drop to 600 up to X weeks before".
   - A clear cancellation schedule.
3. **Check real capacity, not the brochure number.** Seated capacity for the main room, overflow and track rooms, fire limit, and the break-out room sizes for 700.
4. **Plan for no-shows.** Expect 10–20% of registered people not to appear. Decide whether to oversell slightly or to release unclaimed seats to the waitlist before the day.
5. **Scale operations before you scale tickets:**
   - Volunteers and staff, using the same ratio as today.
   - Registration flow and badge pickup. Queues are where size hurts first.
   - Catering throughput and the code of conduct team.
   - Accessibility, insurance, and transport/hotel guidance for the new location.
6. **Protect what attendees like about it.** Ask in your next survey what they value (hallway track, single track, small rooms). 700 is +40%, so keep room for conversation: a networking space, long breaks, and a size cap of 700 for this edition. Don't let "bigger" become the plan for the next one without checking first.
7. **Treat this edition as the experiment.** Define up front what you'll measure: time to sell out per tier, waitlist size, no-show rate, sponsor satisfaction, attendee NPS. Then decide the next size.

## 5. Sequence

1. Get the venue quote with the real ΔF and the downside terms.
2. Run the break-even maths above, with the treasurer.
3. Collect sponsor commitments in writing.
4. Survey the waitlist and past attendees on price and what they value.
5. Set tiers and the wave schedule. Announce the venue change and the reason for any increase honestly.
6. Sign the venue.
7. Open wave 1, read the sales, and adjust the later waves.
