# KernelFest: should we run the eight-batch sellout plan?

**No. Don't run it, and I won't give you a batch structure for it.** The plan isn't free. It damages the one number your growth decision depends on, and it leads to a contract commitment that cannot be undone.

## 1. Why it fails

- **It corrupts your demand signal.** Sellout speed is the main evidence for growing. The headroom gate needs repeated sub-hour sellouts, a waitlist of about 30-50% of capacity, and unsolicited sponsor inbound. That is a heuristic, not an industry standard. Eight small drops sell out in minutes by construction, whatever the real demand is. Afterwards you couldn't tell whether KernelFest really outgrew 600 seats.
- **It makes the venue decision on fake evidence.** "Sold out eight times" would justify a 1,000-seat venue you may not be able to fill. Venue contracts typically carry:
  - deposits of 25-50% at signing;
  - attrition and F&B-minimum penalties owed even if attendance falls short;
  - a 9-18 month lead time, so you sign before next year's demand is visible;
  - little willingness from post-pandemic venues to renegotiate.

  An over-built edition can lose money at an attendance that looks respectable.
- **The social proof is hollow.** Screenshots of staged sellouts misstate demand to attendees and sponsors. Sponsors budget a year ahead on that story, and a sponsor who sees 600 real attendees in a 1,000-seat room won't renew.
- **Batch-released drops built for sellout optics are a deleted option in this framework.** They are not just ranked low. Manufactured scarcity breaks the signal the growth gate reads.

## 3. What to do instead

1. **Run the real gate.** Sell the normal way, in a single or openly tiered release, for the next edition. Record the true sellout time and waitlist, and count unsolicited sponsor inbound. One strong year isn't enough: you need repetition across editions. If you already have two editions of data, your own history beats the generic thresholds.
2. **Diagnose the ceiling.** Check for saturation (local audience or sponsor pool tapped) and for channel decay (personal networks producing less). Also check for quality collapse, where a bigger event already hurt what attendees valued.
3. **Use one lever per edition, cheapest first.**
   - **Venue squeeze.** Fit more people into the known venue by tightening the layout, within fire-code sign-off. One documented flagship event fit a third more attendees into a hall already "at capacity" this way.
   - **Price-led growth.** Use early-bird tiers and a supporter tier that cross-subsidizes accessible tickets.
   - **Track addition** carries hallway dilution. Pair it with recording and protected unstructured time.
   - **Day addition** means a contract amendment.
   - **Venue step-up** comes last, only after the cheaper rungs are exhausted and demand has visibly exceeded supply across repeated editions.
4. **If you reach the venue step-up, cap the downside.**
   - Negotiate the smallest deposit and the shortest attrition and F&B commitments.
   - Prefer nonprofit venues or flexible room blocks.
   - Size capacity to proven demand plus a modest buffer. 600 to 1,000 is a 67% jump, under the roughly 2x ceiling, but "1,000" is the aspiration, not a proven number.
   - Check the whole product at the new scale: AV, networking infrastructure, and volunteer bench.
   - Then hand off to `event-venue-sourcing`.
5. **Set the pricing posture before scaling.** The recommended default is a public commitment to community prices, cross-subsidized by sponsorship. Price-setting mechanics go to `event-ticket-pricing`.
6. **Install the four reversal watches before sales open.** Declare the trigger values in advance and assign each an owner and a check date. Any one firing means hold or shrink, not push through.
   - Sellout speed slows year over year at flat or higher capacity.
   - No-show rate rises as you scale.
   - A single sponsor exceeds the revenue share you set in advance.
   - The organizing team reports the work has become actively unpleasant.

## 4. If a legitimate reason for staged releases exists

Staged release is fine for real operational reasons, such as sponsor or speaker allocation, or fairness to different communities. If you use it:

- Say so publicly.
- Never present the per-batch sellouts as demand evidence.
- Judge growth on a separate, unstaged signal: the waitlist and the total time to sell the whole allocation.

## 5. Bottom line

Don't run the eight-batch plan. Collect honest sellout and waitlist data this edition, then use the cheapest lever first. Treat the 1,000-seat venue as a last-rung option that needs repeated real demand and capped contract exposure.
