# Should you run the sellout-batch plan?

**No, not as pitched.** Run a version of it that's transparent about batch sizes and that measures demand directly.

## Why the pitch fails

- **The sellout proves nothing about demand.** Batch size sets how fast a batch sells out, not how many people want to come. Eight batches of 75 sell out whether 700 or 7,000 people want in. "Sold out eight times" is arithmetic, not evidence.
- **It points you at the wrong decision.** The plan is built to justify a 1,000-seat venue, which is a large, mostly irreversible commitment. You would be basing it on a signal you manufactured yourself. A venue that's 40% empty next year costs far more than any ticketing trick saves.
- **The social proof is misleading.** "Sold out in minutes" is literally true but implies the whole event is in huge demand. Screenshotting each sellout to suggest that is deceptive scarcity marketing. It risks consumer-protection trouble in some jurisdictions. Your audience is engineers who can do the arithmetic, so it also risks a reputation hit if someone points it out.
- **It isn't free.** Weekly flash drops hurt real attendees: people in other time zones, people with a day job, and people who need a manager's budget approval all lose out. They stay out until next week, or buy from bots and scalpers. Expect more support load, resale requests, and sponsors who can't reserve seats for their teams.

## What to run instead

Run the same drops, but design them to produce real demand data and to claim only what's true.

1. **Open a public waitlist now.** Ask for the number of tickets wanted, role, company, and whether the person could get budget approval. This is your real demand measure, and it feeds the venue decision.
2. **Reserve seats up front and say so.** For example, hold about 120 of the 600: roughly 60 for sponsors and 60 for speakers, volunteers, and partners. Publish the split, so "sold out" is never a hidden claim.
3. **Publish the full schedule before the first drop.** List dates, batch sizes, and prices. Waitlist members get a 24-hour head start on each batch.

   Adjust the sizes so the totals reach 600. The principle is that batches get larger and prices rise as the event approaches. Vary the drop time (for example, one drop at a US-friendly hour) so that no region is always shut out.

4. **Label each drop accurately.** Write "Batch 3 of 8, 80 tickets, sold out in 11 minutes." Avoid "KernelFest is sold out." Post the real numbers, including the waitlist count. The honest version still works as social proof: fast batch sellouts plus a long waitlist is a real signal.
5. **Add a transfer or refund window.** This reduces scalping and gives waitlisters a legitimate way in.

## How to decide on the 1,000-seat venue

Decide from data you can defend, not from the sellout screenshots.

- **Metric 1: unmet demand.** After all 600 seats sell, count the waitlist requests left over. Assume only about 30–50% of waitlist requests would convert at full price.
- **Metric 2: speed by tier.** Check whether the later, pricier batches still sold out fast, since that reflects real willingness to pay.
- **Metric 3: attendance mix.** Look at how much demand comes from outside your existing network.
- **Rule of thumb.** Move to 1,000 seats only if the unmet waitlist demand, discounted as above, would fill most of the extra 400 seats. If it's close, negotiate a venue that can flex between roughly 600 and 800 seats, with an option to expand, instead of committing to a fixed 1,000.

## Bottom line

Small batches are fine as a marketing and pacing tool. Using manufactured sellouts as evidence for a venue decision is not. Keep the batches, publish their sizes, and let the waitlist drive next year's venue choice.
