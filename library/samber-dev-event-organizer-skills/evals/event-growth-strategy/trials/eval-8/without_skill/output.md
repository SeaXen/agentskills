# Should MeshWorks Summit grow next year?

## Recommendation

**Don't decide "grow, cap, or cut" yet. Decide what the summit is for, then check whether each added seat is worth its cost.** Finance and DevRel are both right, and they are measuring different things.

- Finance's number is true. At 99 USD tickets, the event loses about 180k USD against ticket revenue.
- DevRel's evidence is real but it shows only that people want to attend. A sold-out event and a waitlist are measures of demand. They don't show that a bigger event pays back.
- A user conference is a retention, expansion and authority investment. Judging it on ticket revenue alone is a category error. Judging it on attendance alone is the opposite error.

**Default for next year:** run a controlled capacity test (below), not a full expansion and not a freeze. Grow only if the new seats clear the gates in step 4.

## Step 1: Fix the framing

Ask two questions before any numbers.

1. **What is the primary outcome?** Pick one: retention and expansion, pipeline, community, or content and authority. An event that targets all four measures none of them.
2. **Is the 180k a subsidy or a loss?** If the summit exists to retain and expand customers, the 180k is a marketing cost. It should be compared with the value it creates, not with ticket revenue.

## Step 2: Measure what the 600 attendees produce

Pull this from the CRM. Match last year's attendee list to accounts and compare against a similar group of non-attendees.

| Question | Metric |
|---|---|
| Do attendees renew more? | Gross and net retention of attending accounts vs. matched non-attending accounts |
| Do they expand? | Expansion ARR in the 12 months after the event |
| Do they create pipeline? | Opportunities sourced or influenced, using a stated influence window |
| Do they adopt more? | API usage, new products or integrations adopted after the event |
| What does it cost per outcome? | Net cost per attending account, per influenced opportunity, per retained or expanded ARR dollar |

**Watch for selection bias.** Healthy, engaged customers are the ones who attend. A retention gap between attendees and non-attendees may only mean engaged customers go to events. Control for this:

- Compare against matched accounts: same segment, size, tenure and prior usage.
- Check each account's trend before the event, not only after it.
- Use a before-and-after comparison, not only a cross-section.
- If you can, use the waitlist as a natural control. Waitlisted accounts wanted to attend but couldn't. If they renew and expand less than attendees with similar prior engagement, that is real evidence.

## Step 3: Read the demand signal properly

Demand is a necessary condition for growth. It does not justify growth by itself.

- **Who is on the waitlist?** Split it by account tier, existing customer vs. prospect, ICP fit, and role (decision-maker, practitioner, partner, student). A 500-name waitlist of non-ICP people is not a case for more seats.
- **How early did it sell out?** Early sellout at 99 USD may mean the price is too low, not that demand is huge. Price is a lever finance has not been asked about.
- **What do "customers asking for more seats" want?** Ask whether it is more seats for their own team, or access for people who couldn't get in. These need different answers (see the allocation options below).
- **What happens to the people who miss out?** Waitlist conversion into next year's attendance is itself a demand test.

## Step 4: Gates that decide whether to grow

Grow only if the data passes all four. Set the thresholds **before** looking at the results, and have both Finance and DevRel sign them off, so nobody picks thresholds to fit a conclusion.

1. **Outcome gate.** Attendance has a measurable effect on retention, expansion or pipeline after controlling for selection. The effect has to be large enough to cover the net cost.
2. **Marginal gate.** The added seats are worth more than they cost. The average attendee is not the marginal one. If the extra 200 seats go to people further from the ICP, the value per added seat will be lower than the average. Estimate the marginal value from the waitlist's profile, not from the existing 600.
3. **Cost-shape gate.** Know which costs scale with headcount (catering, swag, staff) and which are fixed (venue minimum, production, speakers). If the loss rises roughly in proportion to seats, finance's "growth deepens the loss" is right. If much of the cost is fixed, going from 600 to 800 seats may reduce the loss per attendee.
4. **Capability gate.** The team can run a larger event without hurting what customers like about it. Capacity, speaker quality, hallway density and session fit all degrade with size. Sellouts are partly a product of the event being intimate.

If the outcome gate fails, cap or reshape the event. If the outcome gate passes but the marginal gate fails, hold the size and change the mix of who attends.

## Step 5: Options to compare

Model each in one sheet: cost, revenue, attendees by segment, expected outcome value. Compare net cost per outcome, not net cost alone.

| Option | What it does | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Hold at 600, raise price** | Test price elasticity. A modest increase tests whether demand is real or just cheap | Sellout looks price-driven |
| **B. Tiered tickets** | Higher price for prospects, partners and extra seats from the same account. Free or discounted for priority customers | Waitlist is mixed |
| **C. Modest growth (about 15–30%) with a sponsor offset** | Add capacity plus sponsor or partner revenue to cover the marginal cost | Fixed costs dominate and partners want access |
| **D. Same-size event, second format** | A virtual track, regional dinners or smaller events that serve the waitlist | Waitlist is large but event quality depends on intimacy |
| **E. Large expansion** | Scale well past 600 | Only if every gate passes with room to spare |
| **F. Cut or cap** | Reduce size or spend | Outcome gate fails, or the event serves no clear business goal |

Options B and D are usually the cheapest ways to learn whether the extra demand is valuable, because they test it without committing to a bigger venue.

## Step 6: Run it as an experiment

If the data is inconclusive, which is likely, don't argue it out. Make next year the test:

1. **Pre-register the success criteria** (the gates above) with both Finance and DevRel.
2. **Add a limited number of seats**, allocated by a rule you can evaluate: for example, half to waitlisted ICP accounts and half through a higher-price tier.
3. **Tag every attendee's account** in the CRM with the event source, and capture "why did you attend" and "who else on your team".
4. **Follow up within 24–48 hours** and track opportunities and usage per tagged account.
5. **Review after 6 and 12 months**, because retention and expansion take time. Judge it across two events, not one: the first run of anything underperforms.

## Step 7: What to say to each side

- **To Finance:** the loss is accepted as a marketing investment only if it can be tied to retention, expansion or pipeline. We will measure that, set a cap on the net cost per outcome, and not add seats that fail the marginal gate.
- **To DevRel:** sellout and waitlist size are not enough. We need the account profile of the waitlist, and evidence that attendance changes customer outcomes. Seats go to the people who benefit most, not simply to more people.

## Caveats

I don't have your data, so I haven't assumed the outcome of any of this. If attendance shows no measurable effect on retention or pipeline, finance is right and the event should shrink or be reshaped. If it does, the 180k is probably cheap and the question becomes how to grow without losing what makes it work.
