# Verdict: don't call it demand-proven, don't blame price, don't discount yet

Your data can't separate "price is wrong" from "nobody knows the event exists." The announcement push is the biggest lever, and you haven't pulled it. Run it first, then decide on a discount using numbers you set in advance.

## Why the framing doesn't hold

1. **The waitlist is interest, not demand.**
   - 900 free signups from a form posted before tickets existed measures curiosity.
   - Free-signup waitlists typically convert at roughly 3–8% (my rule of thumb, not your data). That's about 27–72 tickets.
   - It is still a real asset, but it won't fill 262 seats alone.
2. **The price test is confounded.**
   - Speakers weren't announced, there were no emails and no socials.
   - A buyer can't pay for a lineup they haven't seen. Slow sales under those conditions say little about price.
3. **98 sold at T-30 isn't alarming for a first edition.**
   - Conference sales are heavily back-loaded, and many buyers wait for the lineup and for employer approval.
   - 98/360 is 27%, which is workable. Don't cite my back-loading as a guarantee, though. Check your own weekly sales curve.
4. **A discount now has real costs.**
   - You burn the 55 early-bird buyers, and any public cut makes you look like you panicked.
   - You can't cleanly learn anything afterward, because price and marketing changed together.
   - You can't take a price cut back.

## Plan, in order

**1. Do today (about 2–3 h, no designer needed)**
- Make speaker cards in Canva or a template: photo, name, talk title, one line. One per speaker.
- Hire a freelancer for a one-off pack if you want polish, but don't wait for one.

**2. Days 1–3: announce**
- Email the 900 waitlist first, with 48 h early access to the lineup and the link. Don't send a blast to the public first.
- Then post one speaker per day on socials and LinkedIn, tagging speakers and sponsors. Ask them to share. They have 40 comp tickets and a stake in the room being full.
- Send a short "convince your boss" email with a justification paragraph and a team-pack option. Platform engineering tickets are mostly employer-bought.

**3. Instrument it**
- Track four steps: email sent → click → checkout started → purchase.
- Use a UTM per channel. Without this you learn nothing from the push.

**4. Set decision gates now, before you see the numbers**

These thresholds are my assumptions. Adjust them to your actual sales curve.

| Checkpoint | Sold | Action |
|---|---|---|
| T-21 (about 1 week after push) | ≥ 160 | Stay the course, no discount. |
| T-21 | 120–159 | Add team packs (for example 5 for the price of 4) and a waitlist-only code with a deadline. |
| T-14 | < 200 | Run a time-boxed, targeted discount. Compensate early-bird buyers with a perk or a refund of the difference. |
| T-14 | < 130 | Right-size: shrink the room or capacity and renegotiate venue and catering minimums. |

Reading the table: if the waitlist emails produce clicks but few checkouts, price or value is the problem. If clicks are low, awareness is the problem. If checkout starts are high but purchases are low, you have a checkout or approval-friction problem. A price cut only fixes the middle case.

## If you do end up discounting

- Prefer targeted levers over a public price cut: team packs, a waitlist-only code, or a student or community rate.
- Give a hard deadline.
- Make it fair to the 55 early-bird buyers.

## Realistic outlook

Filling all 360 seats is not guaranteed. A first-edition result of 250–300 paid plus 40 comps is a plausible outcome. Size the venue and catering contracts with that in mind.

## Questions that would sharpen this

1. What is the ticket price, and what was the early-bird discount?
2. Have you emailed the waitlist at all since tickets went live?
3. What is your weekly sales curve? In particular, how many tickets did the last 7 days add?
4. How many sales came from sponsors or speakers sharing their own links?
