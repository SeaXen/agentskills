## 2. Your two numbers

### The CFP: 31 proposals for 12 slots

- **The ratio:** 31 ÷ 12 = 2.6:1, which is 39% accepted and 61% rejected. Your friend's venues reject 74%, which is 3.8:1.
- **Why the comparison is wrong:** academic acceptance rates measure peer-review selectivity, which follows a different logic. CHI 2024 accepted 26.4%, and the skill says not to compare against that.
- **Right yardstick:** a first local CFP aims for 2–3:1 (sourced as scaled down from flagship figures; the target itself is self-set). PyCon US 2025 ran about 6.5:1 (sourced).
- **Reading:** 2.6:1 is inside the target band. It is a **positive** signal, not a weak one. Undersubscription is the failure case, and you are not there.
- **What the CFP does not show:**
  - It shows a speaker base. It does not show buyers.
  - Check how many proposals came from outside your organizers' circle, and how many are first-time speakers. DjangoCon Europe 2024 used first-timers (51 of 138 submitters) as a vibrancy signal (sourced).
  - Check whether you could fill 12 slots with good talks. A multi-track conference needs far more than 12.
  - Check whether you declared a threshold before opening the CFP. If not, treat 2.6:1 as directional.

### The hackathon: 60 signups for 40 spots, about half return

- **Signups are not intent.** If the hackathon is free, signups are curiosity. Free events see 40–60% no-shows (sourced). The skill deletes raw free RSVP counts from the evidence menu. A 1.5:1 oversubscription is modest anyway.
- **"About half come back" is real but needs unpacking:**
  - Attendee return above 30% is the sourced floor, and about 50% is a practitioner's "healthy" mark for a recurring event. You clear the floor.
  - The denominator matters: returners as a share of attendees, not of signups. The sample is also small, about 20 people.
  - Return is one of three post-edition signals. The other two are sponsor renewal (aim ≥70%, sourced) and sellout-speed trend. You have neither.
  - For hackathons the skill treats return as self-set and directional. Define the target (for example, "share of participants still contributing within 3 months") and flag it as such.
- **Transfer problem:** hackathon regulars are not automatically docs and DevEx writers who would pay for a conference. Unless the audiences overlap heavily, this is evidence about the hackathon, not about DocsCamp.
- **Can you bank it?** Only as a modest, directional point about community stickiness. It is not a fit signal for a paid conference.

## 6. What to do before committing

The skill's job is the read, so these are the instruments to declare and run. Declare each threshold **before** opening it.

1. **Saturation scan this week.** Effort is near zero.
2. **Deposit-backed waitlist.** Use a small refundable deposit and a conversion target you write down first. It beats a free list at filtering curiosity from intent, though the target is still self-set.
3. **Paid probe, if a large commitment is near.** Run a paid deep-dive or a paid hackathon and measure paid conversion, not RSVPs. The skill promotes it when the next step is a venue deposit or the team disagrees. It has the strongest evidence and the highest effort.
4. **Sponsor conversations.** A conference needs sponsors, and sponsor demand can collapse while attendee interest stays warm (the XO case). Ask two or three prospects what they would pay for, and write down what job they would hire it for.
5. **After a first edition,** track return, sponsor renewal and sellout-speed trend separately, never blended.
