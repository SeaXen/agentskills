# DocsCamp: should it become an annual paid conference?

**Verdict:** neither number settles it. Your friend's benchmark doesn't fit your case, and the hackathon retention isn't evidence for a paid conference. Run a cheap, gated demand test first (steps at the end).

## 1. CFP: 31 proposals for 12 slots

**Your friend's benchmark doesn't fit your case.**

- **Your numbers:** 31 for 12 slots is 2.6:1. You accepted 39% and rejected 61%.
- **Their numbers:** 74% rejection means 26% acceptance, or about 3.8:1. The gap is real, but the comparison is weak for these reasons:
  - **Different incentives.** Academic authors submit because careers depend on publications. Practitioners submit only if they have a talk, the time, and a reason to travel. Practitioner CFPs run at lower volume by nature.
  - **Selectivity depends on supply.** The ratio mostly reflects how many slots you chose to offer. With 6 slots, you'd look "strong" at 5:1 and the audience would be identical.
  - **Selectivity is not interest.** Rejection rate measures how picky a program committee is, not whether people want the event.
  - **Yours was a first CFP.** It came from a meetup, and the niche is small (docs and DevEx writers). I have no reliable benchmark for first-year CFPs in a niche this size, so I won't quote one.

**What the CFP does tell you:** how many proposals cleared your quality bar. That is the number to count.

- Count how many of the 19 rejected proposals you would have accepted with more slots.
- A conference needs more slots than a meetup. At 24 talks and the same 39% acceptance, you'd need about 62 proposals.
- If only 13 to 15 proposals were good, the talk supply cannot fill a conference. You would be accepting weaker talks.
- Also check how many proposals came from outside Berlin and outside your existing speakers. Paid attendance depends on reach beyond the current circle.

**CFP volume is a proxy for speaker supply, not for ticket demand.** It says nothing about whether people will pay.

## 2. Hackathon: 60 signups for 40 spots, about half return

**Don't bank it as a fit signal for the conference.**

- **Oversubscription is mild.** 1.5:1 on a free signup is cheap interest. Free signups cost nothing, so they say nothing about willingness to pay.
- **Retention is a decent sign for the hackathon format.** About half returning each quarter suggests a sticky core.
- **It may be one small pool.** If the same roughly 20 to 30 people cycle through, you have loyalty but not growth. Check how many of the 60 signups are new each time.
- **Wrong audience, possibly.** Hackathon participants are builders. DocsCamp's audience is docs and DevEx writers. Check how much the two groups overlap.
- **Unclear denominator.** "Half come back" could mean half of participants or half of signups. Those are different claims.
- **Free to paid is a steep drop.** Returning for a free quarterly event does not predict paying a conference ticket, plus travel for some.

**What it does support:** the community is engaged and the organizing team can run recurring events. That is useful, but it does not show demand for a paid conference.

## 3. What would actually settle it

**Do this next:**

1. **Compute break-even.** Tickets needed = (fixed costs − sponsorship) ÷ (ticket price − variable cost per attendee). Fill in your own venue, catering, and speaker travel numbers.
2. **Re-read the 31 proposals.** Count how many clear the bar, and how many come from outside Berlin and outside your current speakers.
3. **Sell early-bird tickets before committing.** Open them at the target price, with a refund if the event is cancelled. Set the go/no-go threshold in advance, for example "N tickets in X weeks", where N is a share of the break-even number.
4. **Approach sponsors now.** One or two signed sponsors is stronger evidence than any ratio.
5. **Use meetup data too.** Attendance trend, no-show rate, and attendees from outside Berlin all count.

If steps 2 to 4 pass, go. If they don't, consider a lower-risk intermediate step, such as one larger free or low-price day.
