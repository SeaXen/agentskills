## My read

Both camps are arguing about the wrong thing. "Cancel track 3" and "cut price 40%" are separate decisions with separate evidence. 104 of 380 is 27%, which is weak. It does not prove the market rejects the event. I have no ticket price, sales curve, cost structure or edition history, so treat this as a read, not a verdict.

## Decision 1: cut track 3. Yes, probably.

- A 380-seat plan across 3 tracks becomes thin rooms at 150–250 attendees. Thin rooms hurt the experience of the people who did pay.
- Fold track 3's best talks into 2 tracks. Cut room, AV and catering cost.
- Check contracts first: venue attrition clauses, speaker agreements, sponsor promises about audience size. This is the real constraint, so check it before you announce anything.
- This is a cost decision. It does not fix demand.

## Decision 2: 40% price cut. No.

- **Early buyers.** 104 people paid full price. If you refund the difference, that costs 0.4 × 104 = 41.6 ticket-prices. You then need about 69 *extra* buyers at 60% price just to break even on that.
- **If you don't refund them**, you burn trust with your best advocates, four weeks before the event.
- **Future editions.** The market learns that PulseConf discounts late, so people wait next year.
- **Root cause.** A cut only works if price is the blocker. Observability tickets are mostly employer-paid. The usual blockers are awareness, manager approval and budget cycles, and a lower price fixes none of them.
- **Signal.** A 40% cut tells buyers the event is worth 40% less.

## What to do instead

1. **Team packs.** For example, buy 4 and get 1 free, with invoice payment. This targets employer-paid buying and hits the approval blocker. Effective discount is about 20% and only for groups.
2. **One dated "final call" price** (about 15–20%, only if the break-even math allows it). Announce it with a real deadline.
3. **Give early buyers something.** Examples: a free upgrade, a guest pass or a speaker dinner. This protects trust.
4. **Direct outreach.** Ask speakers, sponsors and past attendees to bring colleagues. At 4 weeks, direct asks beat any price change.
5. **Check the sponsors.** Do their contracts promise an audience size? If so, call them now, not at the event.

## Pull today (the data that settles the argument)

1. **Source of the 104.** How many are network, speakers, sponsors or comps, versus cold buyers? If it is mostly network, the cold market is untested. If it is mostly cold buyers, that is real negative signal.
2. **Funnel.** Page visits, then checkout starts, then paid. Few visits means awareness. Many visits and few checkouts means price or value. Many checkout starts and few paid means approval or friction.
3. **Sales curve.** Compare with a prior edition or a comparable event. Conference sales are usually back-loaded, so 27% at T-4 weeks may be a normal curve or a real miss. Without a comparison nobody knows.
4. **Break-even.** Committed costs versus variable costs, and the cancellation penalty. Do this at 150, 200 and 250 attendees.

## Decision rule

- **Break-even at ≤200 attendees with track 3 folded:** run it. The smaller event is the plan.
- **Loss at every realistic scenario, and cancellation penalty is below the loss:** cancelling is a real option. Only then does "the market doesn't want this" matter.
- **Review gate in 7 days.** Re-read sales after the team packs and outreach. Pre-agree the threshold now so the WhatsApp argument doesn't restart.
