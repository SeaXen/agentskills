# ConduitConf 2nd edition: announcement send and early sales read

## Do this first

1. Split the 3,800 addresses by source (attendees, partner export, business cards). Count each, and dedupe against the 143.
2. Find the exact wording of the "interested but can't make it" checkbox and what it promised.
3. Ask the partner meetup whether they will send your announcement to their own list.
4. Split sponsor tickets from public tickets in the sales sheet before week 1 starts.

## 1. "Email everything on day one": don't send it as one blast

The 3,800 is three audiences with three different relationships to you. They carry different legal and deliverability risk, and I'd treat them separately. I'm not a lawyer. If recipients are in the EU or UK, check this with your DPO or counsel, or read the CNIL guidance on B2B prospecting.

| Segment | Relationship | Day-one plan |
|---|---|---|
| Last year's attendees | Real, recent, expected | Send on day one. They are your best audience. |
| The 143 "can't make it" | Explicit interest, but they said no | Send on day one, with different copy (see below). |
| Partner meetup export | They joined the partner's list, not yours | Do not email directly. Ask the partner to send it, or to send a co-branded invitation. |
| Business-card contacts | They handed over a card, which is not a marketing opt-in | Send one transparent message, later (see below). |

**Why the partner export is the risky one.** Consent given to the partner doesn't transfer to you. The partner may also have breached its own privacy promises by sharing the file. Mailing it cold exposes you to complaints. It also exposes the partner, whose relationship you want to keep.

**The clean route:** the partner emails its own members, with a link to your site. You get the reach without holding or mailing the data. If you already hold the file, don't use it for marketing until you have asked the partner what the members were told. Delete it if the answer is nothing.

**Business cards.** In many jurisdictions B2B email is allowed if the message is relevant to the person's job, the source is disclosed, and opting out is easy. That is not a blanket permission. Send these contacts one email in week 1 or 2:
- Say where you met them ("we met at our booth at X").
- Make one offer.
- Include a one-click unsubscribe.
- Don't enroll them in a sequence unless they respond.

**Deliverability is the practical reason to stagger.** A cold, mixed list on day one can produce bounces and spam complaints. Those damage the sending reputation you need for the whole 12 weeks of sales. Suggested sequence:
1. **Day 1:** attendees and the 143, on a warm, low-risk send. Watch bounce and complaint rates for a few hours.
2. **Days 2–3:** the partner send, plus any card contacts you decide to include. Hold this tranche if bounces exceed about 2% or complaints are above about 0.1%. Those are rules of thumb, so check your sending provider's own limits.
3. **Reminders:** send them only to people who opened or clicked, plus the attendees.

Validate addresses before sending. Scrub the cards list in particular, since handwritten or OCR'd addresses have a high typo rate.

**The 143 are a distinct group, not 143 prospects.** They told you they can't make it, so conversion should run well below the attendees' rate. Don't forecast 143 sales. Write them different copy:
- Lead with what changed (date, location, a virtual option, a lower price).
- If nothing changed, offer something they can use, such as last year's recordings or a "notify me for the next edition" option.
- Track them as a separate cohort, so their results don't pollute the main curve.

If the checkbox wording promised updates about future editions, you have a basis to write to them. If it only said "interested," confirm that it covers announcements.

## 2. Reading the first weeks with a 60-ticket sponsor allocation

The 60 tickets are 29% of last year's total of 210. If they land in the sales count, the early curve will look healthy whether or not public demand is.

**Track four lines, never one total:**
1. Public paid tickets, from the announcement date. This is the demand signal.
2. Sponsor tickets allocated. This is a contract and says nothing about demand.
3. Sponsor tickets redeemed, meaning names registered. This is a weak signal about the sponsor's engagement.
4. Sponsor tickets attended. You'll only know this at the event.

Counting 60 allocated tickets as "sales" is the biggest risk in this analysis.

**Compare like with like.** Check whether last year's 210 included any comps, speaker, sponsor, or staff tickets. If so, strip them out before overlaying this year's curve on last year's. Otherwise the benchmark itself is skewed.

**Last year's curve is a weak benchmark on its own.** Reasons it won't map cleanly:
- Last year was a first edition, with no returning base. This year has one.
- Early-bird deadlines and the announcement email create spikes. Dates that fall differently this year will move the shape.
- Conference sales are typically back-loaded, so a slow first month doesn't predict a slow final month. Don't extrapolate from two or three weeks.

Use the curve for its shape (when spikes happened), not as a target.

**Decide the thresholds now, before you see the data.** Write down what you'll do under each scenario, for example:
- "At week 3, if public paid tickets are below X% of last year's same-week figure, add a targeted reminder to openers and a personal ask to ten previous attendees."
- "At week 6, if still below, revisit pricing or the speaker announcement schedule."

Choose X from your own spreadsheet. I can't derive it without the data.

**Other things to log from day one:**
- Use a unique promo code or link for the sponsor's network. Their push is real public demand, but it is attributable to them, not to your email.
- Tag each sale with its source segment: attendees, the 143, partner, cards, sponsor network, and organic.
- Record the sponsor's registration speed. If they redeem 55 of 60 in week 1, that's good for the contract. It doesn't tell you the public will follow.
