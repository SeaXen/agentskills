# Veriluxe Summit media partnerships: what to do next

Following `event-media-partnerships`. I skipped the one-question-at-a-time interview and made assumptions instead. They're listed at the end.

## 1. What the replies mean

Veriluxe Summit is a vendor-run event funded from marketing. The skill says barter is the exception for vendor-run events. Outlets route a vendor's event promotion through their ad inventory, because bartering visibility for one vendor's marketing event costs them the neutrality their audience trusts.

- **Three rate cards.** This isn't a rejection. Each outlet is telling you its promotional inventory is commercial. It's still a live answer, not a dead end.
- **One ignored.** Silence is not a no. You asked for a vague swap ("promote us, we promote you") and gave them nothing countable to say yes to.
- **Two newsletters, no answer.** They haven't answered yet, which is a different state from refusing. Independent newsletters are your best barter candidates. Their product is the send you want to appear in.

⚠️ Don't push the three rate-card outlets for a free swap. Don't ask any of them for articles either. Coverage is a separate skill (`event-press-relations`), and a media-partner deal can't buy it.

## 2. Decide per outlet: buy knowingly, or barter the small stuff

For each rate-card outlet, choose one of these two paths. Don't blend them.

- **Path A: buy advertising knowingly.** This sits outside the barter skill, so treat it as paid media.
  - Give each ad buy its own tracked link and registration code.
  - Set a target before you pay (for example, cost per registration).
  - Buy only where the outlet's readers match database developers in Texas and the US South, plus people willing to fly in.
  - Ad inventory buys placement only. It doesn't buy editorial.
- **Path B: ask for the formats it will still barter.** These are a calendar or events listing, and a guest slot if the outlet runs a podcast or video show. Reply to the rate card and ask for a free event listing, with a link back from your event site.

You can do both with one outlet: buy one slot and ask for the listing. Keep the two as separate documents. If an outlet counters with "half rate plus a mention", that's a discounted ad. Price the cash at the real rate card and write the mention as its own small deal. Never blend them into one document.

## 3. The ignored outlet

3. If it's still silent, drop it. Three more outlets are enough to learn from.

## 4. The two newsletters (your best barter shot)

Send a specific, small follow-up from a named person, not an events alias. Use the skill's four-part ask:

1. One line on the event: what, when, for whom, how many attend.
2. One real overlap: name an issue or topic of theirs that matches your audience. Generic flattery scores zero.
3. The exchange in one sentence: format, what you give, what you ask.
4. A small ask: a yes or no, or a 20-minute call.

Rules for the exchange:

- **Open at the default rung.** That means a calendar listing plus a logo-and-social swap.
- **Promote to a newsletter mention swap** only if the newsletter's audience really matches yours and its engagement holds. Ask them for open rates and click rates instead of guessing from subscriber counts.
- **Be open about who you are.** You are a vendor, and a newsletter may still decline or quote you a sponsorship slot. If it does, go back to the Path A or B decision in section 2.

## 5. Replace the vague ask everywhere

Your original ask ("promote us, we promote you") is the "unbounded logo wall" failure. Nothing is countable or dated, so silence satisfies it. Use this shape:

- **Event delivers:** logo on the named page until a named date. Two social posts on named dates.
- **Partner delivers:** one 80-word blurb with a tracked link in a named send. One reminder before early-bird close.
- **Plus the standard clauses:** no editorial obligation, no list exchange, branding approval before first publication, exit before delivery starts.

Check what arrives through each channel (ticket-type mix, poll answers) against who you wanted. A high-referral partner sending the wrong audience is a scoring error to fix, not a win.
