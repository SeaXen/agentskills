# Media partner outreach for Veriluxe Summit: next steps

## Verdict

Rate cards are not rejections. A cold "media partner?" email usually lands with ad sales, and ad sales answers with the price list. Three replies means three outlets are willing to talk, but only about money.

Don't pay the rate cards as written, and don't give up on the swap. Counter each outlet with a specific offer built from what it wants. That means a ticket block, speaking and content access, early news and data. A bare logo swap has little value to a publisher.

Any paid placement is advertising, not coverage. Buy it knowingly, label it as sponsored, and keep it in a separate budget line from the partnership.

## Why the swap stalled

- **Your logo is worth little to them.** A 500-person first-year event gives a publisher almost nothing in return for promotion that has a price.
- **They sell the thing you asked for free.** Newsletter slots and site placements are their revenue. Free promotion cannibalizes it, so the default answer is the rate card.
- **Your email asked for a favor.** It didn't offer anything they couldn't get elsewhere.

## Step 1: Decide five things before you reply (about 30 minutes)

Without these, you'll negotiate blind.

1. **Goal:** registrations, brand awareness, or both? If registrations, how many seats are still unsold, and at what ticket price (or is it free)?
2. **Paid budget:** how much of the marketing budget can go to placements, separate from the partnership?
3. **What you can give in kind:**
   - ticket blocks (say 10–25 per partner)
   - a speaking or panel slot
   - a content swap (their editor interviews your engineers, you guest-write for them)
   - first access to speaker or keynote announcements, or to data you hold
   - a partner lounge, a branded session, or a dinner for their readers
4. **Who they reach:** do they actually reach developers in Texas and the US South? Austin is a regional draw. A global audience converts poorly for a 500-seat in-person event.
5. **Success measure:** registrations tagged per partner, with a unique UTM link and promo code each.

## Step 2: Reply to the three that sent rate cards (this week)

**Ask for first, in the same reply:**
- Audience by region, with the Texas or US South share.
- Open and click rates, for the newsletter placements.
- Past results for comparable events.

If they don't have the numbers, you've learned something. If the numbers are weak, pay nothing.

**Then counter with a hybrid, not a swap:**
- A small paid placement, plus editorial or in-kind coverage on the swap side.
- Ask for a discount on the paid slot in exchange for the ticket block and content access. Placements that go unsold are often discounted, but test it. Don't assume a percentage.
- Make the in-kind side concrete: tickets, a session, early news.

**Template:**

> Subject: Veriluxe Summit, Austin: partnership structure
>
> Thanks for the rate card. I'd like to structure this as a partnership, not a pure buy.
>
> Veriluxe Summit is a 500-developer database event in Austin, [dates] in March. For [outlet] I can offer [N] tickets for your readers, [an interview slot / a guest session / first access to our keynote lineup], and promotion to our list and channels.
>
> I'd like one paid placement alongside that. Could you send the Texas and US South share of your audience, plus open rates for [slot]? Then I can propose a package that works for both of us.

Under 150 words, no superlatives, and it names the ask. That follows the pitch discipline for technical press.

## Step 3: The one that ignored you

1. Wait until at least one week after your first email.
2. Send one follow-up with new information, such as the speaker lineup or a ticket block offer. Don't write "bumping this."
3. Use a different route if there is one: a named editor or partnerships contact, not the generic inbox.
4. If there's still no reply after a second try about a week later, drop them. Don't spend more effort here.

## Step 4: The two newsletters

Silence from newsletters is normal: one person often runs the whole thing, and cold email gets buried.

1. Find the actual sender or editor by name from the issue footer and recent bylines.
2. Send a short, specific pitch (about 100 words) built on their readers, not on your event.
3. Offer content value first: a data point, a quote from your engineers, or a short guest piece on something their readers care about.
4. Mention the event second, as the reason for the content.
5. Follow up once after about 3 days with new information, once more after about 7 days, then stop.

## Step 5: Negotiating leverage you have

- **Time:** it's October and the event is in March, so you have months. Early commitments let them plan, and they can run multiple placements.
- **Volume:** one package of several placements costs them less to sell than separate buys.
- **Content:** your engineers are a source they can use all year, not just for the event.
- **Tickets:** a block of free seats is a gift they can pass on to readers or use as a giveaway. It costs you little and has real perceived value.

## Step 6: Don't rely on these outlets alone

For a 500-person, in-person event in Austin, local and community channels often convert better than national news sites:
- Austin and Texas developer meetups and user groups
- local engineering communities and Slack or Discord groups
- university and bootcamp groups
- your own customers, list and social channels, and speaker audiences

Treat the news sites as one layer, not the plan. A few well-chosen partners beat six mediocre ones.

## Step 7: Timeline

| When | Action |

| This week | Make the five decisions, reply to the three rate-card senders asking for audience data |
| Within a week of their replies | Send counter-offers, follow up once with the ignorer, send the two newsletter pitches |
| Week 3–4 | Close or drop each outlet, then send contracts with exact placements, dates and UTM links |
| Months 2–5 | Run placements in a staged cadence: announcement, speaker lineup, early-bird deadline, last-chance |
| After the event | Compare registrations per partner and decide who you'd work with next year |

## Step 8: Measure it

Give every partner a unique UTM link and promo code. Judge each by cost per registration, with ticket revenue compared against the spend. For the free in-kind side, count registrations and the content produced. Never count impressions or reach as success. If a paid placement doesn't produce registrations, don't renew it.
