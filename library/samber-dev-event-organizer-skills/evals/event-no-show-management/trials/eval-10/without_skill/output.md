**Short answer: no, not as stated.** Two separate assumptions are bundled together, and only one is shaky.

## What's wrong

1. **Speakers are the wrong comparison.** Paid speaker guests have an obligation, a slot on the program, and their name on the schedule. Scholars have a free benefit and no public commitment. The two groups have different incentives, so speaker show rates tell you little about scholar show rates.
2. **Being selected after a 300-word letter doesn't predict showing up.** The letter measures motivation to write. It doesn't measure exam clashes, visa trouble, a cancelled train, or a student who also got a place at another event. Students are also the group most exposed to last-minute schedule changes.
3. **Your event is free.** Free events usually lose a large share of registrants on the day, often a third to a half. That's a rough rule of thumb, not your data. Scholars with travel arranged will likely show up better than ordinary free registrants, but probably not at paid-guest rates. I don't have your history, so I can't give you a real number.

## Two decisions you've merged

- **Catering for 60 scholars:** this is cheap and fine as a hedge. If 85% show, you waste about 9 meals. Don't over-engineer it.
- **Holding no seats back for anyone else:** this is the real risk. With 350 seats, 60 reserved for scholars who don't come means empty seats you could have filled. It also weakens the sponsor's impact story ("60 students funded" versus "51 attended").

## What I'd do

1. **Reserve the 60 seats, but add a confirmation step.** Require each scholar to confirm by a set date, for example 7 to 10 days before the event. Release unconfirmed seats.
2. **Use your review work.** You read every letter, so you already have a ranked list. Promote the next applicants from it as a waitlist when scholars drop out.
3. **Set catering from the whole room.** Use expected total attendance, plus a 5 to 10% buffer, not 60 as a separate block. Check your caterer's final-count deadline and align the scholar confirmation date with it.
4. **Check how travel is paid.** If you prebook tickets, a no-show costs the sponsor real money, not just a meal. If you reimburse after attendance, no-shows cost nothing, but some students can't front the cash. Pick deliberately, and say which in the sponsor agreement.
5. **Send reminders.** Send them at T-14, T-7, and T-2 days, and make the T-7 one the confirmation request.
6. **Log check-ins per segment** (scholars, general, speakers). Next year you'll plan from your own numbers instead of guesses.

## Decision for you

How do you pay for travel: booked in advance by you, or reimbursed afterward? That choice drives the no-show risk the most, and I'd settle it before the catering count.
