## 1. Is your co-organiser right?

Partly. One room gives density: every attendee who comes can meet every other one. Three activities split that crowd. The claim fails on one point: "everybody" is only the share who show up.

Judge it on two numbers:

- **Reach** is the unique attendees at ≥1 official social event, divided by total attendees.
- **Density** is the share of the full attendee list in the room at peak.

A single party wins on density. Three activities usually win on reach, because each one fits a different person. A single evening party tends to lose people who don't drink, have caregiving duties, are introverts, are on a tight travel schedule, or just don't like parties.

Whether the party wins depends on your goal:

- **Goal is community cohesion and shared memory:** the party can win, if reach stays near last year's.
- **Goal is the most people connected at all:** three activities usually win.
- **Goal is sponsor value:** a single room is easier to sell. Get the sponsor's actual ask before deciding.

Check three things before you commit:

1. The venue must hold your target share of attendees. A room that caps at 40% of attendees caps the party's reach at 40%.
2. Last year's unique participation across the three activities. If it was 70%, the party has to beat that to claim "more".
3. Who skipped last year's activities. If the quiet-activity crowd was a big share, the party loses them.

My recommendation is to run the party as the flagship and keep one cheap, low-key opt-in alternative on the same night or the day before. A coffee walk, a board-game room or a dinner table sign-up would do. It keeps most of the density win and recovers much of the lost reach.

## 2. Benchmark numbers

I have no verified industry benchmark for either figure, and I won't invent one. Nothing I know of publishes "X% of attendees attend an official social" or "N activities per 500 attendees". Numbers like that circulating in blog posts are usually one organiser's anecdote. Your own data from last year is a better anchor than any of them.

**Share of attendees at an official social event.** Write the target as a measured rate against your baseline, not as a number from nowhere:

- **Definition:** unique badge check-ins at ≥1 official social event, divided by checked-in conference attendees. Do not use registrations as the denominator, because they inflate it.
- **Baseline:** last year's unique participation across all three activities.
- **Target:** the party must match the baseline to call the format change neutral on reach. Set a stretch of baseline +5 points only if you have a specific reason, such as a bigger venue or better promotion.
- **RSVP-to-show rate:** track it separately. Use last year's figure to size capacity. If you have none, plan for a large drop-off and treat the first year as the measurement.

**Activities per 500 attendees.** There is no norm to cite. Derive it from capacity instead:

1. Required capacity = target reach % × attendees ÷ expected fill rate.
2. Number of activities = required capacity ÷ capacity per activity.
3. Check the answer against venue limits and budget.

An illustration with made-up inputs, to show the arithmetic only: 500 attendees, 60% reach target and 85% fill gives 300 ÷ 0.85 ≈ 353 seats. That is one 360-capacity party, or three activities of about 120 each.

## 3. What to write into the plan

- Primary metric: unique attendees at ≥1 official social event ÷ checked-in attendees. Target is ≥ last year's baseline.
- Secondary metrics: RSVP-to-show rate, party peak occupancy ÷ capacity, and a post-event survey answer to "did you meet someone new?".
- Capacity rule: the sum of seats ≥ target reach × attendees ÷ fill rate.
- Instrumentation: badge scan or check-in at the door, in place before registration opens. This is the step people skip.
- Review rule: judge the format on a trend across two or three editions. The first run of a new format underperforms its steady state.
