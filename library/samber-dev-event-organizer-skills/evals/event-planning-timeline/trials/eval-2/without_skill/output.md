# DevPier plan check: don't sign as written

**Verdict:** the caterer discount is worth taking, but not by locking in February. The badge lock-in has no upside. The blanket two-week cushion needs rework.

**Assumption to confirm:** today is 14 Oct 2026, so I read "3 October" as **3 Oct 2027**. That date is a **Sunday**. If it's right, check venue, staff and A/V availability and rates for a Sunday, and expect some speaker and attendee friction.

## 1. Two-week cushion on every milestone

⚠️ **A uniform cushion does little for you.**

- **It stacks.** Twelve sequential milestones means 24 weeks of padding. In practice teams spend all of it (Parkinson's law, student syndrome) and the real deadline is still the same.
- **It targets the wrong risks.** The things that actually hold you up have hard external dates: vendor cut-offs, final headcount guarantees, speaker travel and visa letters, permits, AV load-in. A cushion on your internal "draft schedule" milestone protects nothing.
- **Some tasks need more than two weeks.** Sponsor contracts, visa letters and venue and insurance paperwork are examples. Others, like the run-of-show, need no padding.
- **Early deadlines force early decisions.** Final headcount, dietary counts, names and schedule details arrive late. Pulling the deadline earlier means deciding with less information.

**Better rule:**

1. Pad only critical-path items, sized by risk (a 4-week pad for visa letters, 0 for internal drafts).
2. Put one shared buffer before doors open, not a pad on every milestone.
3. Build the schedule backward from each vendor's real cut-off dates.
4. Track the buffer. If more than a third of it is gone before the half-way mark, escalate.

## 2. Caterer: take the 12%, skip the February deadline

The discount applies to bookings placed **more than six months out**. For 3 Oct 2027, that cut-off is about **3 April 2027**. February is not required, and signing in March gets the same discount with more time to learn your real numbers. A two-week cushion before the cut-off is a good use of your instinct: **target signing by mid-March**.

🔴 **Real risk: headcount commitment.** A discount is only a saving if you don't pay for empty seats.

- If you guarantee N covers at 0.88 × price and A people eat, you win only when **A/N is above 88%**.
- Registrations are not attendance. Free or low-cost conferences often see 20–40% no-shows. For 400 registrations, plan catering for the show-up rate, not the sign-up count.
- Example with illustrative numbers: at $60 per head and 400 covers, 12% is about $2,900. Committing to 400 and getting 320 attendees costs about $1,200 more than booking the 320 at full price. The saving disappears.

**Before signing, get these in writing:**

- How "six months" is measured: signature date, deposit date or event date. Get the discount in the contract itself.
- A **minimum guarantee set well below your expected count** (about 70–75%), a flex band above it, and a final-count date about 10–14 days out.
- Whether per-head prices are fixed or subject to an escalation clause. That is your real protection against "prices moving", so ask for a price hold.
- Deposit size, refund rules and what happens on date change or cancellation.
- Substitution rights, dietary and allergen handling, and service charges and taxes (is 12% off the headline price or the all-in price?).
- A Sunday surcharge, if the date is a Sunday.

## 3. Badge print run: don't lock in February

❌ **No discount is attached, and names don't exist yet.** A February print run means:

- Attendee names, roles, speaker and sponsor tiers are unknown.
- Anything printed early is likely reprinted.
- Badge printing is cheap and fast, so there is no price risk to hedge.

**Do this instead:**

- **February:** pick the vendor, fix the spec and get a written quote. Order only generic stock such as lanyards and holders, if the price is better.
- **About 3–4 weeks out:** print the personalized run from the near-final list, with about 10% overage.
- **On the day:** use on-site printing for walk-ups and late changes.
