# ThreadFest positioning

⚠️ **Challenge first:** naming a category does not make a moat. Anyone can host a "docs-ops" event next month. A moat comes from things rivals can't copy fast: trusted curation, a repeat audience, a speaker network, and original data. Treat the category name as a hypothesis to test, not the foundation.

## Why the plan is shaky

1. **Nobody searches for your category yet.** Sponsors, speakers and attendees look for problems they already feel, such as "our internal docs are rotting" or "onboarding takes six weeks". Nobody searches "docs-ops summit".
2. **The term may not be free.** "DocOps" and "docs-as-code" already circulate among technical writers. Check before you commit. If it's taken, you don't own the category, you borrow it.
3. **"Summit" over-promises.** 150 people at a first edition reads better as a focused one-day event. "Summit" invites comparison with events 20× your size.
4. **"Nothing covers exactly this" is a weak signal.** It can mean an open niche, or it can mean demand is too thin. Test demand before you build on it. Adjacent events (Write the Docs, platform engineering days, DX and developer-productivity conferences, DevRel events) all touch your topic. Attendees may already get partial coverage there.
5. **You are three part-timers.** A category-creation campaign takes years of content and evangelism. You have about seven months and weekends.

## What to build instead

**Positioning = a sharp audience, a felt pain, and a point of view.** The category label comes after.

### Step 1 – Pick the audience (decide this week)

The overlap you describe joins two tribes that rarely share a room:

- **Builders of internal docs:** technical writers, docs engineers.
- **Owners of developer experience:** platform engineers, DX teams, eng managers.

Pick one as the primary attendee and the other as the "bridge". My recommendation is **platform/DX engineers as primary**. They hold the budget, they feel the pain, and writers already have Write the Docs.

### Step 2 – Write the positioning statement

Fill this template and test it on 10 people:

> For **[platform/DX engineers and the writers who support them]** who **[watch internal docs rot and slow every new hire and every internal tool]**, ThreadFest is the **[one-day, 150-person conference]** about **[making internal knowledge usable by developers]**. Unlike general DevRel or tech-writing events, we **[talk only about internal docs as a developer-experience problem]**.

Draft tagline options:

- "Internal docs are developer experience."
- "Where DX meets documentation."
- "The conference for the docs your own engineers depend on."

### Step 3 – Treat "docs-ops" as a test

Use it as a **track name or tagline phrase**, not the event name. ThreadFest stays the brand.

Run a cheap test over about 4 weeks:

1. Publish 3 short posts using the term (LinkedIn, a blog on the event site).
2. Ask 15–20 target people: "What would you call this?"
3. Track which phrase people repeat back unprompted.

Keep the term if the audience adopts it. Drop it quietly if they don't.

### Step 4 – Build the real moat

Rank these by how hard they are to copy:

1. **Original data.** You like statistics, so run a short "State of internal docs and DX" survey, target 200+ responses, and publish the results. Whoever publishes the numbers owns the conversation. This is the closest thing you have to category ownership.
2. **A speaker bench nobody else has.** Recruit 6–8 credible practitioners early, with a named lead for each of the two tribes.
3. **A repeat audience.** Run a free monthly online session or a newsletter between now and May. Build the list before the ticket page opens.
4. **Strong editorial taste.** Reject talks that are general DevRel or general writing. Narrowness is the brand.
5. **A shared vocabulary.** Publish a short glossary or manifesto. It's a cheap way to define terms, and it counts only if people use it.

### Step 5 – Sponsor positioning (you have zero)

Sell **audience density**, not category prestige. At 150 people, a sponsor cares about how many of those people are buyers.

- **Target vendors in:** docs platforms, developer portals, knowledge tools, and AI documentation tools.
- **Offer:** a small, honest package such as 3–5 sponsors, a demo slot, and a lead list with consent. Add the survey data as a bonus.
- **Lead with proof:** registration numbers by role and company type, updated monthly.
- **Don't pitch** "join the new category". Pitch "reach 150 platform and DX decision-makers in one room."

| Nov | Publish the positioning statement. Open the CFP. Launch the survey. Start the newsletter. |
| Dec | Test the category term. Secure 3 anchor speakers. Draft the sponsor deck. |
| Jan | Open early-bird tickets with a clear role breakdown. Start sponsor outreach. |
| Feb–Mar | Publish survey results. Announce the lineup. Run one free online session. |
| Apr | Push ticket sales. Close sponsors. Share attendee-role stats. |
| May | Run the event. Publish a recap and a "year-one" dataset. |

## Go/no-go checkpoints

- **End of Nov:** fewer than 5 of 10 interviewees say "I'd attend" → the topic is too thin. Broaden it to general DX.
- **End of Jan:** under 40 paid or confirmed registrations → cut the venue size and scope.
- **End of Feb:** zero sponsor conversations → run it as a community-funded, ticket-only event. Don't spend money you don't have.

## Questions for you

1. Which tribe do you know best, writers or platform/DX engineers? That decides the primary audience.
2. Is the event in person or online, and in which city? That changes the sponsor pitch and the budget.
3. What is your ticket price target? It sets how many sponsors you need.
