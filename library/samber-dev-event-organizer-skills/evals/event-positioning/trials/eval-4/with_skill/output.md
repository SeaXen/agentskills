# Foundry Days: contrast line and positioning draft

## Decision: PlatformScale is not named

The skill I followed (`event-positioning`) deletes naming a rival rather than ranking it low. None of its sourced examples (Strange Loop, DevOpsDays, OSCON, XOXO) names a competitor. Naming one has two costs:
- It invites a public rebuttal from a firm with a bigger marketing budget.
- It ties your identity to their name. If they change format or rebrand, your line goes stale.

A contrast with the *archetype* (an event whose purpose is selling something) still lets the right people draw the conclusion. Anyone who has sat through a PlatformScale keynote will know who you mean.

## The line

**Recommended contrast sentence**

> There are many platform engineering conferences built to sell you something. This is not one of them.

**Alternatives**

- B: "Volunteer-run. Tickets at cost. Nobody on stage is selling you their services."
- C: "Platform engineering, by the people doing it. Not by the people billing for it."

I recommend A. It uses the same shape as Strange Loop's "This is not one of them." B states your governance as fact and works well as a second line. C is punchier but sounds more like an attack, so use it with care.

**Pair it with a positioning statement.** The segment is my guess, so confirm or correct it.

> For platform engineers who want practitioners' lessons rather than a vendor's pitch, Foundry Days is the volunteer-run, at-cost platform engineering conference where the talks come from people doing the work.

## Why these claims are honest

- **Governance:** you are on the community/volunteer pole. Volunteer-run, tickets at cost and no profit are structural facts, as with DevOpsDays' "not for individual or corporate profit". The contrast therefore rests on governance, not tone.
- **Warm language proves nothing:** the vendor pole also sounds like a community. Lead with the structural facts (volunteer-run, at cost), not "community" or "friendly".
- **Your program must back the claim:** "nobody is selling" fails if more than about 30–40% of the program is single-vendor product content, sponsor talks included. Audit the edition 2 CFP before publishing.
- **Price is a proof point, not a headline:** "at cost" is a verifiable fact. Put the ticket price next to it and let readers compare it with £900. Don't write the £900 figure.

## Your side note is the real finding

Five organizers gave five different answers to a sponsor. Under the skill's rubric that scores **5–6** ("organizers describe the event differently from each other"). The pass mark is 7, so the positioning is unfinished until the team aligns.

A great contrast line won't fix this, because the line says what you *aren't*. Sponsors asked what you *are*. Do this before publishing anything:

1. Each of the five of you writes one sentence answering "who is Foundry Days for, and why them?". Do it alone and don't share until all five are in.
2. Compare the five. The differences show where you disagree.
3. Pick the best-fit attendee segment (for example, "senior platform engineers running platforms in production"). Narrow it, and don't say "anyone in platform engineering".
4. Agree the statement above, edited. Then everyone uses that sentence word for word.
5. Re-score it. Pass means all five answer identically.

The skill's workshop format is 2–3 hours. Include whoever handles sponsors, since they hit the problem first.

## Re-run triggers

Revisit the positioning next edition if any of these happen:
- Attendees or sponsors describe the event wrongly to colleagues.
- Arrivals expect things you don't deliver, such as an expo hall or a vendor track.
- Single-vendor content passes the ~30–40% line.
