# Relay: should you book bigger keynotes, and where should the effort go?

## Short answer

**No, don't lean harder into big-name keynotes.** They work against the asset that makes Relay worth planning a year around.

That asset is trust in Ana's curation, delivered through a 40,000-subscriber channel she owns. A star keynote borrows someone else's audience and reputation. A curation brand compounds, and Relay is one. Put the effort into three things: making the curation visible and repeatable, converting the newsletter into a year-round registration engine, and removing the single-person risk.

I haven't seen your registration data, so what follows rests on how single-track conferences generally work and on what you've told me. Verify the numbers below against your own five editions.

## 1. The "30% lift" claim

- **It has no stated source.** Unless you measured it, it's a generic industry line. A sponsor's CFO who asks "30% of what, measured how?" will find that out.
- **It's confounded.** Keynotes are usually announced at the same time as the marketing push, so registrations rise anyway. Registrations also climb toward the event regardless of speakers.
- **It's probably wrong for you.** Your buyers are loyal subscribers who trust Ana's picks. Generic data from star-driven multi-track events doesn't describe them.

**Test it with your own data (about half a day):**

1. Pull the daily registration curve for each of the five editions.
2. Mark the day each headline speaker was announced.
3. Compare registrations in the 7 days after an announcement against the 7 days before it, and against the same days in other editions. Separate newsletter sends from speaker announcements.
4. Check which source (newsletter, social, referral) each registration came from after a headliner announcement.

Whatever you find, use that number in the deck. If the effect is small, say what does move registrations, such as newsletter sends or alumni referrals. Sponsors care about attendee quality and who is in the room, and your single-track format gives them a captive 700.

## 2. Why big names are a poor primary bet

- **They dilute the signal.** "Ana personally picked every speaker" is a differentiated promise. A fee-driven headliner weakens it, and attendees may start to wonder whether the picks are still pure.
- **Fees shift your cost structure.** Big-name fees often come with a rider (travel, handling, sometimes non-negotiable terms). Money spent here is money not spent on the experience that brings people back.
- **They attract one-time attendees.** People who come for a star don't always return. A decade-long ritual needs people who come for the format, whatever the lineup.
- **Single-track magnifies the risk.** One weak keynote slot means 700 people hear it, with no hallway track to escape to.

**The exception:** one keynote, chosen by Ana's standards. That means someone she'd have picked anyway, with a distinctive point of view and a talk made for this audience. Cap the budget and don't build the marketing around the name. Don't let the sponsor deck imply a headliner is guaranteed.

## 3. Where the effort should go (nine months)

### A. Make the curation visible, because it is the product

- Publish the selection principles: what Ana looks for, what she rejects, how she scouts. Short, specific, opinionated.
- Show the record. "Speakers we booked before they were famous" is a real claim if true, and a strong one for a decade-long brand. List them.
- Run a visible scouting loop (open nominations, a "talks I'm watching" column in the newsletter) so the audience feels part of the curation. Ana still decides.
- Give each speaker a short written "why I picked this" note. It costs little and gives attendees a reason to trust the pick before they see the talk.

### B. Turn the newsletter into a registration engine

40,000 subscribers is your biggest lever. Measure it before spending on anything else.

- Find the funnel: subscribers → site visits → registrations. A typical figure is a small single-digit percentage of the list, but check yours.
- Segment the list by past attendance, location, role and engagement. An alumni send and a never-attended send should differ.
- Build a year-round cadence. Announce dates early, then run speaker reveals, behind-the-scenes curation notes and ticket-tier deadlines.
- Add referral mechanics: "bring a colleague" pricing, and alumni who bring someone new.

### C. Build the ritual, because "plan your year around it" is a calendar claim

- Announce the dates for the next two or three editions now. A fixed time of year, a stable venue or city, and consistent format let people plan.
- Keep the traditions people already like (opening, format, closing), and add one signature element attendees remember.
- Offer alumni the earliest access or a loyalty price. Track the **returning-attendee rate**. It's the best single indicator of whether Relay is becoming a ritual. If you don't know it today, find out.

### D. Stay present between editions

- Publish the best talks on a predictable schedule so the brand has a year-round presence.
- Support small alumni gatherings or a speaker-alumni network.
- Keep the community connected (a channel, a monthly digest) without turning it into a second job.

### E. Remove the single-person risk

Ana curates everything and writes the newsletter. That's the strength, and for a decade-long ambition it's also the largest risk.

- Document the curation process so a small trusted group (two or three people) could help with scouting and first-pass screening without changing who decides.
- Decide what happens if Ana can't run an edition. Don't leave this vague.
- Don't dilute the "Ana picks" promise, but make the machinery around her repeatable.

### F. Fix the sponsor story

- Drop the 30% line unless your data supports it (section 1).
- Sell what you can substantiate: audience composition (role, seniority, company type), single-track attention, newsletter reach, and alumni retention.
- Collect post-event sponsor outcomes (conversations, follow-ups) so next year's deck has real numbers.

## 4. A suggested sequence

| Window | Focus |
|---|---|
| Month 1 | Run the registration-curve analysis, compute the returning-attendee rate, segment the newsletter, and announce future dates |
| Months 2–3 | Publish curation principles and the track record, draft the sponsor deck with measured claims, and decide the keynote policy (at most one, Ana's pick) |
| Months 3–6 | Speaker reveals paced through the newsletter, alumni early-access launch, and referral mechanics live |
| Months 6–8 | Ticket-tier deadlines, between-edition content, and sponsor outcomes planning |
| Month 9 | Event, with post-event measurement set up in advance |

## 5. What I'd measure

- Returning-attendee rate
- Newsletter-to-registration conversion
- Registration source mix
- Time-to-sell-out
- Sponsor-reported outcomes
- Net promoter-style "would you plan next year around Relay?"

## 6. Open questions that would change this advice

1. What are your registration curves and returning rate? They decide whether a headliner matters at all.
2. Is the event cash-constrained? If so, keynote fees get even harder to justify.
3. What do sponsors actually buy from you today, and do they renew?
4. How dependent is revenue on the newsletter list versus other channels?
