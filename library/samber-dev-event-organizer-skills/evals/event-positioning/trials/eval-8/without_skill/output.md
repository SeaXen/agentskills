**There is no industry benchmark linking positioning quality to ticket sales, so don't put one on the dashboard.** Nobody publishes a number for it. Positioning quality isn't a standard measurable unit, and ticket sales also depend on price, speaker lineup, timing, and the company's travel budgets. Any figure I gave you would be invented. With four editions, you also can't fit a correlation of your own.

## What to track instead

| Dashboard metric | Why it works |
|---|---|
| Sponsor "one-sentence test": ask each sponsor and attendee "how would you describe Kestrel to a colleague?" Code the answers, and report the % that match your positioning statement. | Measures the actual problem, "recruiting fair". Target: a majority matching, rising each edition. |
| Attendee role mix (SRE/platform practitioners vs. job seekers) | The evidence behind the positioning. |
| Ticket source and a self-reported "why did you come" at checkout | A link to sales you can defend. |
| Sponsor renewal rate | The best sign of sponsor satisfaction. |
| Sponsor-close cycle days, per stage | Problem 2, below. |
| Qualified conversations per sponsor | What sponsors say they got for their money. |

## Yearly repositioning: advise against it

Positioning works by repetition, and sponsors don't yet repeat yours. Changing it each year resets that clock, and it would make the "recruiting fair" label harder to shake. Keep one positioning statement for at least three editions. Keep the event fresh through programme, speakers, and formats. Your head of marketing gets the freshness she wants that way, without the damage.

## Problem 1: sponsors say "recruiting fair"

Positioning copy rarely causes this label. It usually comes from what sponsors see and buy. Check these in order:

1. **Ask sponsors why they bought.** Ask 8–10 of them, 15 minutes each. If most bought for hiring, the label is accurate for them. Then decide whether recruiting is a revenue line you want, and sell it as one tier.
2. **Audit what you sell.** Check whether the packages, the prospectus, and the sales emails lead with attendee headcount and "reach talent".
3. **Audit the floor.** Look for resume drops, giveaway-for-scan booths, and the expo hall's layout. Sponsors take their cue from these.
4. **Check the audience data.** If you can't show role mix and seniority, "recruiting fair" is the default story.

Fixes:

1. Write one sentence: who Kestrel is for, and what they get. For example: "the working conference for engineers who run production systems".
2. Rebuild the packages around outcomes: workshop slots, a help-desk booth staffed by engineers, and technical sessions. Move job-board and recruiting items into one clearly labelled add-on.
3. Publish an audience data sheet (role mix, seniority, company size, returning-attendee rate) before you publish prices.
4. Brief each sponsor on booth conduct. Technical staff who debug a stranger's problem beat a recruiter with swag.
5. Publish 2–3 sponsor outcome stories that aren't hires.

## Problem 2: five-month close cycle vs. roughly six weeks

First check the "six weeks" figure. It comes from conferences you know, so ask 2–3 of them for the stage breakdown. Then time your own deals by stage: first contact, proposal, negotiation, legal and procurement, signature. The longest stage is your problem. Likely causes:

- **No fixed packages or prices.** Every deal gets custom negotiation. Fix: publish a price ladder with fixed tiers and a standard contract.
- **No deadline.** Nothing forces a decision. Fix: dated early-bird pricing and tiers that sell out. Fix a contract deadline roughly a quarter before the event, as large conferences do.
- **Wrong buyer.** The recruiting label routes you to HR or talent budgets, which approve slowly. Fix: find the real buyer (devrel, developer marketing, engineering leadership) and pitch them. This is also how problem 1 may be feeding problem 2.
- **Renewals treated as new sales.** Fix: offer last year's sponsors first right of refusal until a set date, and a multi-edition discount.
- **Slow proof.** Fix: send the audience data sheet and last edition's outcome report in the first email.

Set a target per stage, for example proposal within 3 days and signature within 3 weeks of the proposal. Review it each edition. The five-month cycle is a hypothesis until you've timed the stages.
