# JSConf Atlantique: handling the three offers

**Next action:** accept #1 with changes, accept #2 with a written deal, and decline #3 as framed. About 30 minutes of emails.

| # | Offer | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Newsletter: pass + logo for 2 pieces + recap | 🟢 Yes, with changes | Cheap in-kind barter. Audience fit is the open question. |
| 2 | Creator: pass for 3 tagged posts | 🟢 Yes, with a written deal | Low cost, but "tagging us" is too vague to hold her to. |
| 3 | Trade site: feature if you buy a half-page ad | ❌ No, as framed | Paid editorial presented as coverage. Only worth it if the ad stands alone. |

## 1. Newsletter (20k subscribers)

**Real risk:** it's a Kubernetes newsletter and you run a JS conference. Subscribers who are infra people may never convert, and 20k only matters if the overlap is real.

**Before you say yes, ask him for:**
- Open rate and click rate on past sponsor links.
- Subscriber geography. Atlantique is a regional event, and 20k worldwide may mean about 800 reachable people.
- Past sponsored placements and what they drove.

**Terms to ask for:**
- **Angle:** pitch the platform and infra side (Node on k8s, edge runtimes, frontend infra, SSR deployment). A generic "come to JSConf" piece will flop with his readers.
- **Schedule:** the two pre-event pieces land at set dates, the first at least 6 weeks out and the second about 2 weeks out. The recap runs within 7 days of the event.
- **Tracking:** a UTM link plus a subscriber-only discount code, so you can measure what he drove.
- **Disclosure:** pieces are labelled "media partner" or "sponsored". French rules require commercial collaborations to be identified, and readers trust labelled content more anyway.
- **Pass:** one pass, personal and non-transferable. Say plainly that a pass is not a sponsorship tier.
- **Written agreement:** half a page by email is enough. It should cover deliverables, dates, and what happens if he doesn't deliver (you can pull the pass or the logo).

**Your side:** list him as a media partner on your site and in your event emails. That costs you nothing and makes the deal feel fair.

**Don't:** let him dictate sponsor-row placement among your paying sponsors. His sponsor row is on his property, so the logo there is his to give. Yours stays yours.

## 2. Mid-size creator (pass for 3 posts)

**Check first:** her engagement rate (not follower count), whether her audience is developers in your region, and 3 or 4 past partnership posts. If her audience isn't devs in Europe, decline politely.

**If she's a fit, put this in writing:**
- **Deliverables:** 3 posts on named platforms, with dates. At least one must come before the sales deadline.
- **Content:** she posts something real, not just a tag. For example, a talk she's excited about, or a "what I'll be doing there" post. The post includes your link and a discount code.
- **Disclosure:** she labels the posts as a collaboration. French law requires the commercial-collaboration label for this kind of exchange.
- **Pass timing:** release the pass after the first post is live, not before.
- **Approval:** you don't script her posts. You do get to check facts like dates, venue and prices.
- **Shortfall:** if she posts fewer than 3, you pull the pass or she pays the difference.

**Protect yourself:** write one standard comp-pass template and reuse it. When creator #2 and #3 show up next week, you can say "same terms for everyone" instead of renegotiating each time.

**Also decide now:** a cap on total comp passes (say 10, or 3% of capacity). Without a cap these deals pile up, and every comp is a seat plus catering you could have sold.

## 3. Trade site (feature if you buy a half-page ad)

**What this is:** a pay-to-play offer. If you buy the ad and the "feature" runs, it's advertorial, and it must be labelled as sponsored or paid. Unlabelled, it's deceptive for them and a reputation risk for you. Labelled, it carries far less credibility than real coverage. Either way you're paying for an ad, not earning a feature.

**What to do:**
1. **Ignore the bundle.** Ask for the rate card, readership numbers, and the audience's geography and job titles.
2. **Judge the ad alone:** does the audience fit and is the price reasonable per expected click or ticket? If yes, buy it on its merits, with no feature promise attached. If no, skip.
3. **Reply in writing:** "We're happy to discuss advertising separately. We'd only run a feature if it's editorial and independent of the ad." This shows you know the difference and keeps the door open.
4. **If they insist the feature is part of the deal,** ask for it in writing, including how it will be labelled. If they won't label it as sponsored, walk away.
5. **Pitch the editorial team separately,** with a real story angle (a speaker, a talk, a trend). If they cover it, that coverage is worth far more than anything bought.

Note that trade-site links from paid content are usually nofollow, so don't count them toward SEO.

## One consistent rule for all three

Before accepting any exchange, check three things:
- **Cost:** what do we give up, in seats and in dollars?
- **Fit:** does their audience match ours (devs, in or near the region)?
- **Proof:** can we measure the result with a UTM link or a code?

If an offer fails two of the three, decline.

## Questions that would change my answer

1. **Capacity:** is the event close to sold out? If so, comp passes cost real revenue and you should be pickier.
2. **Goal:** do you need ticket sales now, or brand awareness for next year? Awareness makes #1 and #3 more attractive.
3. **Ticket price and sponsor tiers:** is a free pass plus a logo too close to what paying sponsors get? If so, give #1 a "media partner" label that is clearly not a tier.
4. **Ad budget:** is there any? If it's zero, #3 is already decided.
