**Verdict:** all three plans have problems. Fix them in the order below.

## 1. Email gate on logo pack and fact sheet: ❌ drop it

- Journalists on deadline leave when asked for an email. Many won't hand over an address for a logo, and they just grab one from Google Images instead.
- Speakers, sponsors, partners and bloggers also use the logo pack. Gating it slows the people who promote you.
- You get little from the capture. Download emails from an open asset page are mostly noise, not press leads.
- GDPR applies in the EU. You'd need a stated purpose, a privacy notice and consent for any marketing use. A "download = newsletter signup" bundle is not valid consent.

**Do instead:**
- Make the logo pack and fact sheet a direct download with no form, and put a press contact (name and email) beside them.
- Gate only things that are worth a form:
  - the accreditation application, where the form is the point
  - an opt-in "press list" for embargoed announcements and speaker lineup drops
- For marketing's visibility, use aggregate download counts (privacy-friendly analytics). That tells them how many people downloaded the pack without collecting identities.
- Add UTM-tagged links in outreach emails if marketing wants to know which pitch drove interest.

## 2. Accreditation wording copied from Web Summit: ❌ rewrite it

- **Legal.** Copying their text verbatim is copyright infringement. The terms are also drafted for their entities and jurisdictions, not yours.
- **Wrong promises.** Their wording describes their eligibility rules, media tiers, passes, photo and recording rights, data handling and liability. If you publish it, you commit to things you may not do.
- **Scale mismatch.** A 70k-attendee event needs elaborate tiering. At 1,200 attendees a short page works better.
- **Search.** Duplicate text can hurt the page's ranking.

**Do instead:** use Web Summit's page only as a checklist of sections. Write your own for:
1. Who qualifies (staff, freelance with recent bylines, creators or podcasters, yes or no).
2. What to submit, with the deadline.
3. What accreditation includes and excludes (pass, press room, speaker access, embargoed material).
4. Conduct rules, including recording and photography.
5. Privacy notice for the data collected in the application.
6. A named contact and a response time.

Have whoever owns legal for JVMFest read the data and consent section.

## 3. KubeCon numbers beside yours: ❌ drop the comparison

- It backfires. KubeCon is roughly an order of magnitude larger, so a side-by-side shows you as the small one.
- It invites an apples-to-oranges argument (submissions, sessions and attendees are not comparable across event types).
- Their figures change by edition and region, so you'd have to cite year, source and edition for each one, and keep them current.
- It implies a connection to a third party's brand.

**Do instead:** build credibility from your own verified figures:
- Attendance: 900 last year, and "1,200 expected" this year. Label the second as a forecast. If you can, use the registered count to date instead.
- Growth: +33% year on year (900 to 1,200).
- CFP submissions, sessions accepted and acceptance rate, if you can share them.
- Speaker, country and company counts, and the attendee role mix (for example, share of senior engineers and architects).
- Notable past speakers and past press coverage.

If you still want an external anchor, compare to events of similar size, cite the public source and year, and ask someone to verify each figure before publishing.
