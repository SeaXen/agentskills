# Nightshift emergency plan

**Not legal or insurance advice.** This is an operational plan, not a fire-safety certification. Occupancy limits, fire-code compliance for overnight sleeping, and insurance go to the venue, your local fire authority, counsel and a licensed broker.

I couldn't interview you, so these are my assumptions. Correct any that are wrong.

- Nightshift is community-run or lightly funded.
- The venue's own emergency procedures exist but you haven't seen them.
- Nobody has told the fire authority or emergency services about the event.
- Sleeping is on the floor, in sleeping bags, in the open hall and possibly on the mezzanine.

The plan has four parts: things to settle with the venue before the event, the contacts page, one section per scenario, and the on-site rules.

## Part 1: Settle with the venue contact before the event

"You should have an emergency plan" is the opening of a conversation. Ask for these in writing.

1. **Written emergency procedures.** Ask for evacuation routes, assembly points, alarm type and panel location. Adopt what the venue already owns and say so in the plan.
2. **Permission for overnight sleeping and 180 occupants.** Ask whether the venue's fire and occupancy approval covers people sleeping there. If the answer is unclear, get it confirmed before Friday. This is the largest unknown on your list.
3. **Exits.** Count the usable exits for the main floor. Ask whether the mezzanine's single staircase is an approved route for the number of people you plan to put up there.
4. **Electrical capacity.** Get a map of circuits and the breaker panel location. Ask what load the building can sustain.
5. **Insurance.** Ask whether the venue requires proof of liability cover as a condition of the contract. If so, that is a hard gate.
6. **A first-aid kit, AED location and fire extinguisher locations.** Walk them with the venue contact.

Open decision: decide before the event whether the mezzanine is for sleeping, quiet work, or storage only. My recommendation is not to allow sleeping there. People asleep, plus one staircase, plus smoke rising, is the worst combination in the building. Make the call with the venue contact, who may already have a rule.

## Part 3: Scenarios

These five are not ranked. Plan all of them, or state that the venue's own procedure owns one.

Every scenario has the same first rule. Whoever is nearest acts first. They radio or message the on-duty organizer on the event's organizer channel, with a phone call as backup.

### Fire or smoke

1. Raise the alarm. Trigger the venue alarm or shout "FIRE, EXIT NOW". Do not stop to look for the source.
2. Call the emergency number. Give the street address and say "warehouse, about 180 people, some asleep."
3. Evacuate to the assembly point: `[name the spot, upwind and away from the building and the road]`.
4. Mezzanine sweep: a named organizer clears the mezzanine and staircase first, because it has one exit. Keep that person's name on the rota at all times.
5. Sleeping area sweep: two named organizers walk the sleeping zones and wake people.
6. Do not fight a fire beyond a small, contained one with a nearby extinguisher.
7. Head count at the assembly point, using the badge or sign-in list.
8. Nobody re-enters until emergency services or the venue contact gives the all clear.

**Hackathon-specific fire sources to control in advance.**
- **3D printers.** They are unattended heat sources. Put them near an exit, on a non-flammable surface, away from sleeping areas and away from bags and cables. Use a named owner for each, a smoke detector or watcher nearby, and a rule that nobody leaves a print running unattended overnight without a named watcher.
- **Laptop and phone charging.** Lithium batteries can ignite. Ban charging under bedding or sleeping bags. Keep sleeping areas clear of charging clusters.
- **Power strips.** Ban daisy-chaining. Count load per circuit (see below).
- **Sleeping bags near equipment.** Keep a clear gap between sleeping zones and printers, power strips and heaters.

### Power failure or overload

1. Before the event, map which circuits your power strips are on and distribute load across circuits. 180 laptops plus printers, monitors and chargers is the likeliest cause of a trip.
2. If power trips, locate the panel (known from Part 1) and reset only if the cause is clear. If it trips again, do not keep resetting. Reduce the load.
3. In the dark: phone torches, plus a stock of battery lanterns placed at the exits, the staircase and the sleeping areas. Do not use candles.
4. Hazard check: unplug 3D printers on power loss. Warn teams about unsaved work and tell them to commit and push.
5. If power does not return and the venue is unsafe in the dark with 180 people, treat it as an evacuation.
6. The network and the venue's internet link die with power. Keep the plan's paper copy and a mobile hotspot for the organizers.

### Medical emergency

1. Call the emergency number first for anything serious: unconscious, chest pain, severe bleeding, breathing trouble, seizure, severe allergic reaction.
2. Send someone to the entrance to meet the ambulance and guide it in.
3. A trained first-aider attends and stays with the person. Name them on the rota. If nobody is trained, find one before Friday or contract a first-aid provider.
4. Know where the first-aid kit and AED are.
5. A 36-hour overnight event brings predictable cases: exhaustion, dehydration, caffeine overdose, fainting, panic, and an ankle on the stairs. Provide water and food, enforce a quiet zone, and tell people it's fine to leave and sleep.
6. Ask at registration for allergies and an emergency contact, with explicit consent, and keep the data restricted to the organizers.
7. For any mental-health crisis, stay with the person, call the emergency number if there's risk of harm, and don't leave them alone.

### Theft

1. 180 people with laptops in a converted warehouse is a theft target, and sleepers can't watch their bags.
2. Prevention: control the entrance with a badge check and a sign-in list, keep exterior doors closed overnight and watched, and ban propping doors open for smoking.
3. Tell attendees at kickoff to keep valuables on them when sleeping, or use a supervised storage area with a tagged-bag system.
4. If theft happens, an organizer takes a written report, notes the time and place, and secures the area. The victim calls the non-emergency police line, or the organizer does it for them. Use the emergency number only if the thief is present or someone is in danger.
5. Event gear (AV, network, printers) gets an inventory list and a named owner.

### Evacuation for any other reason

Covers gas smell, structural noise, flooding, a threat, or anything else the venue calls.

1. Use the same routes and assembly point as for fire.
2. An organizer with a loud voice or megaphone makes the call. Who calls it is named on the rota, with a backup.
3. Sweep order: mezzanine, sleeping zones, toilets, side rooms.
4. Head count at the assembly point.
5. A named person confirms with the venue or emergency services that the building is clear. Nobody else announces that it is.
6. If people can't return, have a fallback: where 180 people go at 3am in the cold. Ask the venue contact now about a nearby warm space, and keep that on the contacts page.

## Part 4: On-site rules

1. **One organizer awake and alert at all times.** Overnight for 36 hours, this needs a written rota with named people, shift times and handover, made before the event. Two awake at night is better. A person on shift does not sleep on shift.
2. **Night duty kit.** The on-duty organizer holds the printed plan, the contacts page, a charged phone, a torch and the master key or venue contact.
3. **Print the plan.** Put copies at the registration desk, the organizer table, and the entrance. Digital-only fails when power, Wi-Fi or phones die. Brief every volunteer, not only the leads.
4. **Safety briefing at kickoff, 2 minutes.** Cover the exits, the staircase rules, the assembly point, "no charging in bedding", and "tell an organizer if you smell smoke".
5. **Signage.** Mark exits, the assembly point, the first-aid location and the extinguishers. Do not block exits with sleeping bags, tables or gear.
6. **Keep aisles to exits clear** at all times, including at night.
7. **Notify local emergency services in advance.** Tell the local fire service and the police non-emergency line that a 180-person overnight event is running at that address. They may have a large-event protocol and may add a visit or on-call coverage.
8. **Alcohol.** If any is served, treat it as an amplifier. Prefer none at an overnight event.
9. **Code-of-conduct incidents** are a separate, named process. Have a named responder and a private escalation path.

## Risk register: a short extension

The emergency plan covers row 9 of the taxonomy. A full event would also score the other rows, for example demand, sponsor loss, speaker dropout, network failure and organizer loss. For Nightshift I would score these amplifiers high: overnight duration, hands-on hardware, a single staircase, and 180 people in a former industrial building. Sleeping people and fire plus a single exit give the highest impact, so any scenario that touches them is orange or red until the venue confirms the points in Part 1. Re-score the register at each decision date.
