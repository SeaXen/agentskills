## Why the clause doesn't deliver a refund

1. **It excuses performance. It doesn't move money.** It says neither party is liable for failing to perform. It never says what happens to payments already made.
2. **A refund clause that is silent decides against you.** In the pair of cases the skill cites, a New York court ordered a full deposit refund because the contract expressly said the venue "shall refund all payments". A Hawaii court denied a refund on a near-identical fact pattern because the clause was silent. Your clause is the silent kind.
3. **Those cases are US illustrations.** I don't know your governing law, so I can't say how a court would read your clause. Counsel can.
4. **Risks that are now foreseeable need to be named.** Industry guidance since 2020 says disease, terrorism, labor disputes and hazardous weather must be named. Otherwise a court may find the organizer accepted the risk.
   - **Named:** terrorism, strike, governmental action.
   - **Not named:** communicable disease, weather, venue closure, utility failure.
   - You are relying on the generic phrase "causes beyond its reasonable control" for those.
5. **It is mutual.** The venue is excused too. Nothing says it must return your deposit when it is the one excused.
6. **It only covers failure to perform.** Most of what actually ends events isn't covered:
   - Low ticket sales.
   - Sponsor or exhibitor defection.
   - A keynote dropping out.
   - The team deciding not to run it.
   - Each of these is a documented cancellation cause, and none is "beyond reasonable control".
7. **It leaves the fee schedule untouched.** If the clause doesn't apply, the venue's cancellation-fee step-ups still do.

## What to do before Friday

1. **Send the clause and the fee schedule to counsel.** Ask them to cover these points. I'm not drafting wording:
   - Whether payments already made are returned or credited if the clause applies.
   - Whether a rebooking credit is available.
   - Whether disease and weather should be named perils.

2. **Treat the 30k EUR as at risk until counsel says otherwise.** That includes the budget and the board.
3. **Ask the venue for the cancellation-fee schedule in writing** if you don't have it.
4. **Book a broker call this week.** Put these questions to them:
   - Does the cancellation cover carry a communicable-disease buy-back, and at what price?
   - What are the binding deadlines for each cover?
   - What must you disclose as a known circumstance? Non-disclosure voids the policy.
5. **Record the buy-back decision either way, with the reason.** Insurers excluded disease market-wide from late January 2020. The exclusion attaches to the peril, not to who orders the closure. SXSW's government shutdown order didn't rescue its coverage.
6. **Check the quarter-of-budget trigger.** The skill's working default is that cancellation cover becomes worth asking about when non-recoverable committed spend passes about a quarter of the budget. That is a default, not an industry standard. 30k EUR may or may not cross it, depending on your budget.
7. **Set go/no-go dates now.** Put one just before each fee step-up. Agree the thresholds before pressure arrives:
   - Registration pace against break-even.
   - Sponsor and speaker confirmation.
   - Contractual exposure.
   - Safety and travel conditions.
   - Payment and refund capability.
   - If more than two people's judgment has to be reconciled, use a formal poll where a single no-go halts.
8. **Don't wait for legal certainty.** Waiting costs percentage points of the contract at each step-up. Cancelling earlier at a lower step can beat waiting for a clause outcome that is never guaranteed.
