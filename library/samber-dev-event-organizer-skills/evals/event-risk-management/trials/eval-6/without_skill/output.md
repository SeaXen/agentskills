# Is your head of ops right?

**No.** The clause as quoted doesn't give you your 30k EUR back. Don't sign Friday without adding the refund mechanics below.

I haven't seen the full contract or the amendments, and I'm not your lawyer. Have counsel review the final text. The points below are the ones to raise with them.

## Why the clause doesn't do what he thinks

1. **It excuses liability, not payment.** It says neither party is liable for failing to perform. It doesn't say what happens to money already paid.
2. **It has no consequences.** It has no termination right, no refund, no credit, no rescheduling, and no deadline after which you can walk away.
3. **Silence goes to the venue.** Without a refund clause, the default is whatever the deposit clause and the cancellation schedule say. Many venue contracts make deposits non-refundable, or make them a cancellation fee.
4. **It covers less than "anything big".**
   - It only applies to events beyond reasonable control that actually prevent performance.
   - Listed: acts of God, war, terrorism, strike, governmental action. "Including" makes the list non-exhaustive, but the "beyond reasonable control" test still applies.
   - Not clearly covered: pandemic, severe weather, power or utility failure, venue damage, supplier failure, transport strikes, a speaker or sponsor dropping out, low ticket sales, a recession.
   - Disputes will come down to wording like "strike" (whose strike?) and "governmental action" (an order, or just advice?).
5. **Payment obligations are often not excused.** Courts generally don't treat "I can't pay" or "the event is no longer worth it" as force majeure. If the venue is still available and you choose not to hold the event, expect to forfeit the deposit.
6. **It cuts both ways.** If the venue can't deliver, it's excused too. Without a refund clause, you could end up with no event and no money back.

## Governing law matters

Which law governs? If it's French (likely if the venue is in France), the default rules help you more than this clause does:

- Civil Code art. 1218 defines force majeure as an unforeseeable event beyond control whose effects can't be avoided. A definitive impossibility ends the contract, and a temporary one suspends it.
- On termination, parties are released and payments are restored. The deposit's legal character then decides the outcome. An *acompte* (a payment on account) is generally refundable. *Arrhes* or a stated *dédit* (a forfeit-to-withdraw clause) may not be.

Check how your contract labels the 30k. Also check whether it adds a clause that overrides the default rules. Ask counsel to confirm for your jurisdiction.

## What to ask for before Friday

1. **Refund on termination.** If a force majeure event prevents the event from happening, the venue refunds all prepayments, less only documented, non-recoverable costs already incurred.
2. **A termination right.** Either party can terminate if the event lasts more than X days, or if it begins within Y days of the event date.
3. **A reschedule or credit option.** You choose between a refund and a transfer of the deposit to a new date.
4. **Notice duty.** Notice within 5 business days, with the parties working together to mitigate.
5. **A wider list.** Add epidemic or pandemic and public-health restrictions, severe weather, utility failure, venue damage, and transport disruption. Say that government advice or restrictions on gathering size count.
6. **A cancellation schedule.** The venue's fee should scale with how late you cancel. Check that the 30k isn't a flat forfeit.

Sample wording to adapt:

> If a Force Majeure Event prevents the Event from taking place on the agreed dates, or reasonably makes it unsafe, unlawful or impracticable, either party may terminate on written notice. The Venue shall refund all sums paid within 30 days, less only reasonable, documented, non-recoverable third-party costs actually incurred before notice. Alternatively, the Client may elect to transfer all sums paid to a rescheduled date within 12 months.

## If the venue refuses

- **Event cancellation insurance.** It covers deposits and sunk costs for named perils. Buy it before an event is foreseeable, because known risks get excluded. Check exclusions for pandemic, strike and terrorism.
- **Negotiate the deposit instead.** Ask for a smaller deposit, staged payments, or a refundable portion.
