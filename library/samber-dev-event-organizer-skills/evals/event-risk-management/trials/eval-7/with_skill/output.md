## Short answers

- **What do we do?** Don't cancel by reflex, and don't improvise. Within 48 hours, lock the facts. Within a week, announce a replacement or a reframe and give ticket-holders a clear option. Hold a formal go/no-go call before the venue's next fee step-up. Losing an uninsured marquee speaker is one of the standard conditions that moves a decision toward no-go. Run the call even though a single speaker is unlikely to kill a 520-person event.
- **Should we have had insurance?** Probably yes for this one risk, but it wouldn't have covered most of your actual exposure. Details are in the insurance section below.

## Next 48 hours (in order)

1. ⏳ **Write to the keynote.** Be kind and brief. Ask three things:
   - Whether she'd do a short recorded or live-remote segment later, only if she wants to.
   - Whether she agrees to a wording for the announcement.
   - Whether she consents to her name and image staying in past material while you transition.
2. **Pull three documents.** Get the speaker agreement (fee, travel, cancellation terms), the venue contract's cancellation-fee schedule with step-up dates and percentages verbatim, and the ticket terms (what the program promises, and refund wording).
3. **Send the ticket terms and the speaker agreement to counsel.** The question is whether ticket buyers have any refund right now that the keynote is gone. This turns on wording and jurisdiction, so I won't guess.
4. **Freeze paid promotion that features her.** Don't delete anything yet. Stop spending on ads that now over-promise.
5. **Get the ticket data.** Pull sales by date and channel, and check whether sales spiked after her announcements. Your "a third" is a guess, and the actual number sizes the refund exposure.
6. **Call a broker** with the questions listed in the insurance section.

## Next 7 days

7. **Shortlist 3-5 replacement keynotes, ranked.** Ask the best fit first and the next only after a no, so you never hold two offers at once. Treat a speaker who can say yes in a day or two as the better fit over one who is a bigger name but slower.
8. **Pick the story before you announce.** There are three options:
   - **Replacement:** a named keynote.
   - **Reframe:** the program and the remaining speakers carry it.
   - **Hybrid:** her recorded segment plus a local lead.
9. **Announce proactively.** Send the email yourself before attendees see the news elsewhere. Say what changed, what is unchanged, and what the attendee can do.
10. **Decide the ticket-holder option with counsel.** A time-boxed opt-out window for people who bought for her is a common commercial answer, but the legal minimum is counsel's call.
    - **Exposure formula:** (attributable tickets) × (ticket price) × (opt-out rate).
    - **Planning bounds:** use your real attribution from step 5, and model the whole range from no refunds to everyone attributable refunding.
11. **Check sponsor commitments.** If any sponsor bought on her draw, tell them before they hear it elsewhere. Wording of any sponsor remedy goes through the sponsor agreement and counsel.

## Go/no-go calendar

1. **Find the next fee step-up.** Read it from the venue contract. Set an internal decision date just before it, not on it.
2. **Run the polled call.** Each area owner votes go or no-go, and a single no-go halts. Polling is warranted here because more than two people's judgment has to be reconciled under time pressure, and sponsors may hold leverage.
3. **Fix the thresholds before the call, in writing, while nobody is under pressure.** For each of these, fill in your own number:
   - Refund-adjusted revenue vs break-even.
   - Replacement or reframe confirmed by a set date.
   - Sponsor confirmations.
   - Remaining cash exposure if you cancel at this step vs the next.
4. **Repeat before each later step-up.** Re-score the register at every date.
5. **Remember the cost of waiting.** It is priced in percentage points of the contract. Cancelling at a lower step-up can beat waiting for certainty that never arrives.

## Insurance: the honest answer

- **Right category:** non-appearance / key-speaker cover. It exists for an event where one named person carries the program and the ticket sales, and you said she drove about a third of sales.
- **No clear answer on whether it would have paid.** These forms are typically written around a named person's death, injury, illness, or transit delay. Whether a family emergency fits is wording only a broker can read. Voluntary withdrawal is a typical exclusion. Ask the broker directly.
- **It's too late for this loss.** Cover generally must be bound at least 14 days before the event, and a withdrawal you already know about is a known circumstance. Non-disclosure at binding can void a policy.
- **Most of your exposure was never insurable.** Poor ticket sales or lack of interest and voluntary cancellation are standard exclusions on cancellation policies. Refunds and lost demand are mostly yours to absorb either way.
- **Questions for the broker now:**
  1. Can we still bind cover on the replacement keynote, and what must we disclose about today's events?
  2. Does the form include a communicable-disease buy-back? Record the answer either way, with the reason if you decline.
  3. Which binding deadlines for our date have already passed?
  4. Does our existing venue liability policy respond to anything here? Venue liability doesn't cover your own financial loss.

## What to change next edition

- **Single-person marketing dependency:** cap how much of the campaign rests on one name, and keep a named backup keynote on standby once sales depend on a headliner.
- **Speaker agreement:** have counsel check substitution, notice period, and fee and travel handling.
- **Ticket terms:** have counsel check "program subject to change" wording and refund language.
- **Insurance decision:** make non-appearance cover an explicit, recorded yes or no at the moment you commit marketing spend to one person.
- **Register re-read:** bind a re-score to every decision date so it happens without anyone remembering.
