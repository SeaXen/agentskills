**Do now (15 min):** Reply to her. Kind, no pressure. Ask one thing: can she give a 10–20 min recorded or remote slot, or a short Q&A, if her situation allows? Do not push. "No" is fine.

## Plan

1. **Today (about 1 hour): get facts.**
   - Read her speaker agreement for cancellation, force majeure, fee and travel deposits, and any replacement or recording clause.
   - Read your ticket terms for "lineup subject to change" and refund language.
   - Call your broker or insurer. Ask whether you hold any policy and whether it covers speaker non-appearance.
2. **Days 1–3: pick the replacement path. Recommend A plus B.**
   - **A. Her in some form.** Pre-recorded or remote segment, if she offers.
   - **B. A new headliner.** Rank 3 names with a similar audience draw, then ask them in order. Speakers often say yes to a 6-week ask for a good fee and a short brief. Ask her for intros.
   - **C. Fallback.** Restructure the keynote as a panel or fireside with 2–3 of your strongest confirmed speakers. This weakens the draw, but it can ship.
3. **Days 3–7: announce.**
   - Send one short, honest email to all 520 ticket holders, and update the site. Say she is out for a personal reason, name the replacement or the plan, and give the refund and transfer policy.
   - Agree wording with her first. Do not share her family details.
   - Send it before attendees notice on social. Silence reads worse than bad news.
4. **Week 1: scrub the marketing.**
   - Pull her from the site, ads, scheduled posts, schedule pages, structured data, email templates and speaker graphics.
   - Replace her. Do not delete her without a replacement.
   - Check that old posts and ads are paused. Selling tickets on a lineup you know is false creates refund and consumer-law risk.
5. **Week 1–2: size the damage with data, not a guess.**
   - Your "a third of sales" is a guess. Check ticket sales by date against campaigns that featured her, and any "how did you hear" field.
   - Expect refund requests. I would plan for a low double-digit percentage of the people she drew. That is my rough estimate, not data. Hold cash for it.
   - Decide your refund window before you announce. A generous 14-day window costs some refunds but reduces chargebacks and bad press.

## Should you have had insurance?

**Probably not the kind that fixes this. Check your broker anyway.**

- Event cancellation insurance usually pays when the whole event is cancelled or disrupted by listed causes, such as venue loss, severe weather or a venue's failure to perform.
- Some insurers sell a "key person" or non-appearance add-on for a named speaker. It must be declared and bought up front. Many require a medical certificate, and family emergencies are often excluded or only partly covered. I do not know your policy or jurisdiction. Your broker can say.
- Even where it applies, it typically reimburses costs or lost revenue from cancellation. It does not pay for a lineup that sells fewer tickets.

So the real gap was **concentration risk**, not missing insurance:

- One person drove about a third of demand, with no contract fallback, no recorded backup and no reserve headliner.
- Next time, contract for a recorded or remote fallback and a replacement clause, and keep a backup speaker warm.
- Spread marketing across 3 or more names so no single one carries the campaign.
- Price a key-person add-on once you have a quote.
