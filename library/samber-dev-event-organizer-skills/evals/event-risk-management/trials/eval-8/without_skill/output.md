**Short answer:** "The event ran fine" can't tell you whether the risk process worked. Judge the sheet by what it did, not by how the event went. Run a one-hour audit of the three incidents against the sheet, then decide. I'd reuse the sheet as a seed list, not as a process.

## Why "it ran fine" proves little

- **One event is one sample.** A good result can come from luck or improvisation just as easily as from planning.
- **The sheet was opened twice in seven months.** A document nobody consults can't have driven the outcome, good or bad.
- **Three risks did materialise.** Two of them cost real money or trust (4 h of ticketing outage on launch day, 12k EUR unpaid). Whether the sheet helped with them is the thing to test.

Your co-organizer is right on one point. The event succeeded, and the sheet may be a decent list. You are right on the other: using the sheet and having a risk process are different things.

## The audit (about 1 hour, both of you)

Build one row per incident: ticketing outage, unpaid invoice, AV crew swap. Fill in these columns from the February sheet.

| Column | Question |
|---|---|
| On the sheet? | Was this risk listed at all? |
| Rated | What likelihood and impact did you give it? |
| Owner | Did a named person own it? |
| Mitigation | Was an action written down, and was it done? |
| Last looked at | Did anyone review the row before it happened? |
| Actual cost | Money, hours, attendee impact. |
| Cheapest prevention | What would have cut the damage, and when? |

Read the result per incident:

1. **Not on the sheet.** This is a coverage gap. The sheet missed a risk class, so add the class (see below).
2. **On the sheet, no action or owner.** This is a usage failure. The sheet recorded the risk but nothing happened.
3. **On the sheet, mitigated, still hit.** The process worked at the level you can reasonably ask. Check whether the mitigation was strong enough, and whether the impact would have been worse without it.

Then do two more checks:

- **Look at the other 19 rows.** Mark each as "happened", "didn't happen", or "never assessed". If most of them are generic ("bad weather", "speaker cancels") with no owner or trigger date, the sheet is a brainstorm rather than a plan.
- **List near-misses nobody wrote down.** Ask who improvised a fix during the event. Each improvisation is a hidden risk event the sheet didn't cover.

## How to decide, and agree on it before you audit

Settle the rule with your co-organizer first, so the result isn't argued afterwards:

- **0 or 1 of the 3 incidents were on the sheet:** the sheet's coverage is weak. Rebuild it from this year's incidents plus a short pre-mortem.
- **2 or 3 were on the sheet but nothing was done:** keep the content and fix the process. This is the likely outcome given two openings in seven months.
- **Most were on the sheet with mitigations that worked or limited the damage:** reuse it with light edits.

My prediction: the sheet's content is mostly fine and the habit around it is the weak part. I haven't seen it, though, so the audit decides.

## If you reuse it, change these things

1. **One owner per row.** A person, not "the team".
2. **A trigger date per row.** For example "confirm named AV crew 2 weeks out" or "invoice paid before doors open".
3. **A fixed review slot.** Put it in the calendar: monthly, then weekly for the last 4 weeks. Fifteen minutes is enough.
4. **Cut to the top 8-10 live risks.** Rank by impact × likelihood. Archive the rest. 22 rows is too many to keep alive.
5. **Record the actual cost** of every incident right after the event, so next year's ratings rest on data.

## Fixes for the three incidents

- **Ticketing down 4 h.** Keep an offline attendee list and a manual check-in fallback. Get an uptime commitment in writing. Decide in advance who communicates to attendees.
- **12k EUR unpaid.** Invoice sponsors earlier, with a deposit or payment due before the event. Set a cut-off date after which unpaid sponsors lose their placement. Decide who chases the invoice, and when.
- **Different AV crew.** Name the crew and lead technician in the contract. Add a substitution clause. Confirm the crew by name two weeks out, and again the day before.

Add a standing row for **"supplier or partner doesn't deliver as promised"**. All three incidents are the same type of failure, which is a third party not delivering what was agreed.
