# Caldermoor Conf: running it without a "who's in charge"

**Yes, it's different, and skipping the formal structure is riskier for you than for a company.** A company has structure that exists whether or not anyone writes it down. You have none of it unless you write it down.

## What a company has that you don't

| Company conference | Caldermoor (5 volunteers) |
|---|---|
| Legal entity signs the venue, AV, and catering contracts | A person signs. Their name is on the liability. |
| Company insurance, legal, and HR | Nothing, unless you buy event insurance |
| Staff assigned to the event as their job | Five people with day jobs and two talks |
| A manager has final say when people disagree | Five equals, so a 2–2 split with one abstaining |
| Security, first-aid, and facilities teams | Nobody |

Fewer people and fewer safety nets call for more clarity, not less.

## Why "whoever's closest" fails on the day

1. **Three of five will be unavailable.** Two are speaking and one is MCing. They'll be prepping, on stage, or recovering at the moments things go wrong. "Closest" often means a volunteer who isn't one of the five.
2. **Nobody owns the call.** When a problem needs money, a schedule change, or a stop, "closest" has no authority. Two people may also give opposite fixes.
3. **Everyone assumes someone else has it.** In a group of friends, each person thinks another already handled it.
4. **Some problems can't go to whoever's near.** These include a medical emergency, an evacuation, and a code-of-conduct report about a friend. The last one needs someone neutral and trained.

## What to do instead (about an hour of work)

A single page, agreed before the event. You don't need hierarchy. You need named owners.

1. **Day-of incident lead.** Pick one of the two who is neither speaking nor MCing. They make the final call on anything unresolved. Name a deputy.
2. **Safety and venue.** One person knows the exits, first-aid kit, and venue contact, and has the emergency numbers.
3. **Code-of-conduct contact.** Publish a CoC and name two contacts. At least one should be outside the five, so reports about a friend don't go to a friend.
4. **Money and signing.** Decide who signs contracts, who holds the funds, and the spend limit the incident lead can approve on the spot.
5. **Speaker and AV liaison.** This person covers no-shows, tech failures, and overruns. The MC needs a named person to signal to.
6. **A backup for every role while its owner is on stage.** Build a simple grid of time slots against roles, using the actual schedule.
7. **Pre-decided answers for the likely failures.** Write one line each for these cases:
   - A speaker no-shows.
   - A session overruns.
   - Projector or mic dies.
   - Someone needs medical help.
   - Evacuation.
   - Weather or transport trouble.
   - CoC incident.
   - Cancelling the event.
8. **A comms channel.** Use one group chat or radio channel for all volunteers, plus a phone number printed on volunteer badges.

## Before the event

- ⚠️ **Contracts and liability.** Decide whose name goes on the venue contract. Consider a fiscal host or a small non-profit, or at least event liability insurance. Check what your local rules require for 200 people.
- ⚠️ **Money.** Don't hold ticket or sponsor money in one person's personal account without a written record of where it goes.
- ⚠️ **Attendee data.** You'll hold names and emails for 200 people, so decide who is responsible for them.
- ℹ️ **Extra volunteers.** Five people can't cover registration, doors, and the room for 200. Recruit 4–6 helpers and brief them on who the incident lead is.

## What is the same as a company

For normal planning, informal works fine between friends. The issue is the day itself, and the cases where the plan breaks.
