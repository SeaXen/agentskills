# What to measure at your next event, and what targets to set

There are no published targets for these metrics, so I won't invent any. Every metric below is one you set, not a benchmark. Your last edition left no numbers, so this edition's results become the baseline. Fixed targets can only come after that.

I assumed a multi-room day with an organizer on duty, run by organizers or volunteers rather than a hired host. I also assumed you will run this event again. A recurring event is why the metrics below feed a reusable template.

## Why "nobody escalated" tells you nothing

Zero escalations usually means staff could not reach anyone, not that nothing happened. A quiet day and a day where problems never reached you look identical from where you sat. The 40-page document can't resolve that either, because nothing in it recorded what happened.

Before doors open this time, check two things:
1. Every staffed post can name the organizer on duty for their block without asking anyone.
2. The org channel works live. Send one message and get an answer from each post.

## Gates: pass or fail before doors open

These are not metrics. Each is a yes or no, and the target is 100%.

| Gate | Pass condition |
|---|---|
| On-duty visibility | Every staffed post names the on-duty organizer for their block, unprompted |
| Cue-sheet completeness | Every segment has a named owner |
| Handoff completeness | Every shift boundary has a handoff record, with an entry or an explicit "none" in each section |

## Metrics: pick these three and record them on the day

1. **Transition dead air.**
   - **What to count:** seconds between one segment ending and the next starting. Count scripted transitions and unscripted ones separately.
   - **Target:** none yet. This edition is the baseline.
   - **What it triggers:** if scripted transitions don't beat unscripted ones, the extra cue-sheet depth isn't paying off. Drop it next edition and keep the cheaper timed grid. If they do beat them, keep scripting.

2. **Escalations that reached the on-duty person.**
   - **What to count:** how many reached the on-duty organizer. Write each one down with its time and room.
   - **Target:** don't aim for a low number, because that rewards silence. The target is that every escalation that happened reached someone, and you can only know that if you also log problems staff say they handled alone or couldn't get through on. Ask the post leads for that list at closeout.
   - **What it triggers:** a block with zero escalations and a non-empty "couldn't reach anyone" list is a comms failure. Move the channel setup up a rung, for example from one text channel to a text-plus-live pair, or radios if coverage is the problem.

3. **Cue-sheet drift.**
   - **What to count:** rows that turned out wrong. Examples are an owner who was elsewhere, tech that wasn't where the sheet said, or a segment that didn't exist.
   - **Target:** fewer wrong rows than this edition. This edition sets the number.
   - **What it triggers:** log the wrong rows the same day, on the text channel. They go straight into next year's template. Also record which pages of the 40 anyone used. That tells you what to cut.

**Optional fourth, only if something breaks:** count live changes made against the published disruption order versus improvised ones. The improvised number is the one to drive down, and the direction is zero. That is a direction, not a benchmark.

## How to run it

- **Write the targets and the revision each metric triggers before the day.** Don't set them afterward.
- **Assign one person to log**, ideally the organizer on duty or someone beside them. A person on stage can't log, and neither can anyone busy running a room.
- **Capture the numbers the same day.** Cold retrospectives recover the decisions and lose the timings.
- **When you present these numbers, say they are self-set.** Don't present them as industry standards.
