# Section: Disruption priority order and grid corrections

**Scope.** This section covers a speaker no-show, a speaker arriving late, a room or AV failure, and a badly overrunning session. It is the order decided in advance. How staff carry it out live belongs to the run-of-show.

**Fields marked `[ ]` are ours to set.** No published conference benchmark exists for them. Fill them in before the grid goes public and record the values here.

## 1. Priority order

When something breaks, make the fix in this order. Stop at the first rule that decides it.

1. **Protect plenary slots.** The opening, keynotes, lunch and closing are the only moments all 300 people share. A fix must never move or shorten them.
2. **Protect the headline sessions.** `[list the sessions people came for, by name]`. Don't move these unless the disruption hits them directly.
3. **Disturb as few other sessions as possible.** A repair that moves five sessions to save one is the wrong repair. Prefer a fix that touches one session in one room.
4. **Lock the revised grid.** After the fix, nothing else moves until the lock ends (section 4).

## 2. Repair ladder for a no-show

Try the options top to bottom. Use the first one that works.

1. **Run the pre-assigned backup.** Each block has a named standby item for each room: `[standby speaker, repeat talk, panel, or open-space session]`. It is chosen and briefed before the day. This touches nothing else.
2. **Swap within the same room and block.** Exchange the empty slot with a later session in the same room, only if that speaker is confirmed on site.
3. **Turn the slot into a published break.** The room's session is cancelled and the grid shows a break. Nothing else moves.

**Never move a session earlier than its published time.** Attendees arrive at the published time and speakers' travel windows are built on it. Moving sessions forward is what broke last year's grid.

A repair that touches more than `[2]` sessions needs the decision owner's explicit sign-off (section 5).

## 3. Trigger and late arrivals

- **Trigger.** A speaker not confirmed on site `[N]` minutes before their slot is treated as a no-show. The decision owner then picks a rung of the ladder.
- **The first decision stands.** If the speaker turns up after the repair, the repair is not reversed. Reversing it is the move that made the published grid wrong for the rest of last year's afternoon.
- **Late speaker.** They give their talk in a slot that is still unlocked and unpublished-as-fixed, or `[a designated catch-up slot]`. If neither exists, the talk is not reinstated at this event. Offer to record it.

## 4. Grid corrections: communication and lock

The manufacturing guide's "30 minutes" and "4 hours" don't carry over. They are tuned to a factory shift, not to a conference day. A conference block is not four hours, and attendees learn of changes through different channels. Here the timing is tied to the grid's own structure.

**Lock rule.** After a change, the revised grid is locked until the next plenary or break re-sync point. This is the stretch of the day where the grid already re-aligns. A further change inside the lock needs a new disruption (a room failure, a second no-show), not a better idea. With the grid's current shape, the locks end at: `[list break and lunch times]`.

**Communication rule.** A change is not done until it is published. The first-moved session's published start is the deadline. If a session starts before the correction is out, the repair failed. Aim for `[N]` minutes from decision to publication. Set N from how long the update actually takes, measured in a rehearsal.

**Correction channels.** The owner updates all of these in one pass, working from a checklist:

1. The public grid (web or app), with a visible `v[n] – [time]` marker showing what changed. The marker lets someone who planned their day see what moved.
2. Door and room signage, and screens where they exist.
3. A spoken announcement at the next break, and in the affected rooms.
4. A staff message so every volunteer repeats the same version.

A change that skips any channel isn't finished. Use one source of truth, and everything else is copied from it.

## 6. Record

After the event, log each disruption: time, rung used, sessions moved, minutes from decision to publication, and whether the lock held. The log shows next year whether `[N]` and the standby plan were right.

## What I changed from the ops suggestion, and why

- **30-minute communication and 4-hour freeze:** dropped as numbers. They come from manufacturing and would be wrong here. A freeze shorter than the rest of the block repeats last year's re-shuffle, and a fixed 4 hours may outlast the day.
- **What I kept:** the idea of communicating fast and freezing after a change. Both are expressed as rules tied to the grid, with the time left for you to set.
- **What I added:** the repair ladder and the no-reversal rule are my judgment. They aren't sourced from published event practice. They are aimed directly at last year's failure: three sessions moved forward, two moved back.
