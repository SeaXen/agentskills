**Short answer:** I can't tell you whether you'd be liable, whether you need a signature, or how much cover to ask for. Those turn on Irish law and the exact facts. The skill I followed bars me from stating legal thresholds, instruments or monetary figures, because a confident wrong number does more harm than none. What I can give you is what decides your exposure, what to do this week, and the questions to put to a solicitor and your broker.

## 1. What decides whether you're exposed

Three of the five exposure categories in the skill apply here, and all three go to counsel:

- **A person hurt at a third party's venue.** Here that's the stairs.
- **Alcohol served by someone else.** It's an open bar, and it's their bar.
- **Apparent endorsement from listing an event you didn't run.** This is the one you control, and it's the likeliest route from "their party" to "you're in the picture".

The facts a lawyer will ask about, so find these out now:

1. **What does your fringe page actually say?** "Recommended" or "official fringe" is very different from "events happening nearby, run by others, not vetted by us."
2. **Did you vet it, approve it, or only list what they submitted?** Don't claim you didn't vet it if you did.
3. **Did you license your name or logo to them, or only use theirs?**
4. **Did you send people there**, through programme slots, emails or on-site signage?
5. **Is any of your team involved on the night**, as a host, a speaker or a sponsor of the event?
6. **Who owns the Ashgrove Tech Week name and the organising entity**: you, a parent body, or nobody in particular?

## 2. Do this now (none of it is legal advice)

1. **Add a non-affiliation line to the fringe listing.** It has five parts: who runs it, who doesn't, what you didn't do, who to complain to, and what still applies. Draft:
   > The Foundry Sessions is organised and run by [co-working space]. It is not organised, staffed or vetted by Ashgrove Tech Week. Questions or complaints go to [their named contact]. [If you require it: the organiser has agreed to follow a code of conduct; report concerns to the same contact.]

   Keep the sentence identical everywhere you mention the event, and have your solicitor look at it before you publish.
2. **Be honest about the limits of that line.** Research on disclaimers (trademark and advertising studies) finds they often fail to correct the impression they target. They help but don't protect you. The framing does more work: present the listing as events happening nearby that you encourage people to support, not as your programming.
3. **Get a named, reachable human at the venue**, available on the night itself, not a generic email address.
4. **Ask the co-working space two things in writing:**
   - Do they hold their own cover for events and alcohol?
   - Have they adopted a code of conduct with a contact person?

   Record the answers. Whether an answer is adequate is a question for your solicitor and broker.
5. **Run the two cross-checks.** If a competitor of one of your paid sponsors runs or funds the event, you have an exclusivity problem independent of the stairs. Your code of conduct only reaches the event if its scope clause says so, so adoption is a condition you impose, not something you inherit.
6. **Keep a short record.** Write down who asked, what you listed, the wording used, and the date. If anything goes wrong, that record is what you'll want.

## 3. Your three questions, routed

| Question | Where it goes |
|---|---|
| Are we on the hook for a guest who falls drunk on their stairs? | **Irish solicitor.** Bring the answers to section 1 and a screenshot of the listing. Ask specifically about premises and alcohol-service responsibility, and whether your listing language changes your position. |
| Do we need them to sign something? | **Same solicitor.** A signed agreement is the heaviest option and only works if both sides are legal entities that can sign. For a one-night listing, the written confirmations in step 4 plus the non-affiliation line may be proportionate, but that call is theirs. |
| How much public liability cover should we ask them to carry? | **Your insurance broker and the solicitor.** Ask your own broker whether your policy reaches events you list but don't run, and what they'd expect a host of an open bar to hold. I won't name a figure. |

## 4. If you want it simpler

If nobody on your team wants to keep tracking this, the cleanest option is to take the listing down and say nothing about the event. That gives up discoverability but removes the endorsement route. Only do it if the listing is easy to reverse and you haven't sent anyone there already.
