# Arclight Connect: speaker relations takeover plan

**Pole assumed:** vendor/user conference. Travel, hotel and crew are real, so the risk is "did anyone tell the speaker", not "can we afford it". Adjustments for that pole are my judgment, not established practice.

**Dates assume the event is about Thu 2026-11-19.** Replace them once you have the real date. Every ladder date below is a placeholder until the agency gives you the print and A/V ordering deadlines.

## The three problems are one problem

Two terms were promised before anyone with authority approved them: an open-ended fee and a 45-minute slot. The agency's "no packet needed" gives you a third gap, because nobody currently owns what the speaker is told.

The skill's rule applies to all three. You deliver what was promised, you never invent a figure nobody confirmed, and anything that can't be delivered goes to whoever promised it now, not at the venue. A term quietly downgraded between acceptance and arrival is what speakers tell each other about. So the order is to find out exactly what was said, get decisions fast, then correct each speaker personally.

## 1. The fee

You don't decide the terms and you can't create a fee. "We'll sort out a speaker fee" is an open promise, and each speaker is probably reading it as a yes. Three honest outcomes exist, and a budget owner has to choose one.

| Option | What it means | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| A. Approve a fee or honorarium | Finance creates a line. Amount, payer, paperwork and date are written down before anyone tells a speaker | Direct payment brings tax, contract and employer conflict-of-interest questions. Route it through whoever handles event money and contracts. |
| B. No fee, in-kind package only | Travel, hotel, ticket and speakers' dinner are the offer. You tell the speakers plainly and early | Some may decline. That is better than a refusal in week 5 or a no-show. |
| C. Some other approved gesture | For example a donation in their name, or a gift. Only if the budget owner actually approves it | Don't float this before it is approved. |

**My recommendation:** push for a decision, not a drift. Fees are not normal practice for most developer conferences, and travel plus lodging is the standard floor. Whichever option wins, the budget owner makes the decision and puts it in writing before you speak to any speaker. A vague "usual range" can't be fixed by being vague again.

**Don't:**
- Confirm, hint at or quote a number.
- Say "we're working on it" without a date.
- Let the agency or the original inviter reassure speakers informally.

**If the answer is B,** lead with what is real. Say that flights and hotel are booked for them, what else is covered, and that there is no speaking fee. Say plainly that earlier messages were unclear and that you're correcting it. Take responsibility as the organizer, and don't blame the previous person. Offer a way out: if the fee was a condition of their yes, they can tell you now.

## 2. The 45 minutes against the 30-minute grid

The grid is published and 1,400 attendees plan from it. Changing it five weeks out costs more than correcting three speakers. The grid owner decides, using the schedule-design skill's territory.

1. **Default: hold the grid at 30 minutes** and tell the three speakers now. This is my judged default. It's the cheapest, and each week of delay makes it worse.
2. **Exception:** if one of the three is effectively a headliner and a 45-minute slot exists or can be created without breaking the grid, the grid owner approves it explicitly. Then deliver it as promised.
3. **Offer something real for the lost 15 minutes** only if it is deliverable: for example a hosted Q&A or office-hours slot in a room with a time. Don't promise it speculatively.

When you tell them, give the actual reason (the grid is published and every breakout is 30 minutes), give the new length, and ask whether they can still do it. A speaker who built 45 minutes needs time to cut, so give the news early. If a speaker says a 30-minute version doesn't work for them, take that decision back to the owner, not to the speaker.

## 3. The agency and the speaker packet

The agency is right that it handles logistics, and wrong that this makes a packet unnecessary. The packet is how the speaker finds out what the agency has done. At a vendor conference, a big production machine hides the speaker's own experience, so the packet and a named contact matter more, not less.

**Say it this way to the agency:** "You own booking and logistics. I own what the speaker is told, and I need your facts to tell them. A packet is one document that carries your work to them."

**Ask the agency for:**
- Status per external speaker: flights and hotel, dates, who pays, who the traveller contacts.
- The reimbursement mechanism for anything not booked directly, plus claim deadline and payout turnaround.
- Whether the agency itself has been talking to speakers. If so, you need to see it, and it should go through your single contact from now on.
- The venue's A/V ordering deadline, the programme and badge print dates, and catering lock date.
- Whether there is a green room, a house A/V technician, a confidence monitor and wired internet.
- Arrival day and time per speaker, and ground transport.
- Visa letters, if any speaker needs one. Check current official requirements and don't guess lead times.

**The speaker packet** (one document, one link, updated in place) contains:
- Slot, room, format and length (30 minutes, if the grid holds), and how strictly the length is enforced.
- Who introduces them, name pronunciation, and the pronouns to use.
- Every deadline, with the reason behind each.
- The recording policy for their format.
- Travel and hotel: who books, what's covered, caps, claim deadline and payout date. Say "not covered" explicitly wherever that is the case.
- Arrival instructions, badge, and where the speaker room is.
- What the room is like: capacity, lectern, confidence monitor, time signals, whether there are questions.
- Whether the deck is collected after the talk.
- The commercial-content rule in one line: no sales pitch or commercial demo. This matters more because it is your own company's conference and speakers may feel pressure either way.
- The code of conduct and how to report something.
- **You as the one named contact**, with one address and a stated response window. Add a same-day channel for the event days, and name the agency's on-site person in advance if they take over there.

## 4. Single point of contact

That's you. Tell the agency, the original inviter and any team member to forward every speaker message to you or CC you. Use per-speaker threads, never one CC'd broadcast.

| Event day | Host meets each speaker, then tech check on the real stage | run-of-show |
| Within days after | Thank-you, then feedback, recording link, travel-document deletion | follow-up |

**Reminder shape.** Each reminder carries new information (the real reason and the consequence), and ends with an offer to renegotiate. A reminder that only repeats the last one teaches speakers your deadlines are decorative.

**Cadence:** packet plus two checkpoints for everyone. Add milestone messages and a check-in call for the three affected speakers, keynotes, first-timers, and anyone with international travel or unusual A/V.

## 6. Recording consent

The three were invited, so they never saw a submission form. Present the recording, streaming and publication terms in the packet and record each answer per speaker. Keep one row per session with format, date, where the terms were shown, record yes/no, stream yes/no, publication and channels, and any condition. Ask now, before any camera is scheduled. Check Q7 below: whether you record at all, and per format, is still open. Silence is not consent. Collect from invited speakers using whichever level you pick.

## 8. Open questions (they change the plan)

1. What exactly was promised, and did any speaker get travel or hotel terms in writing? Answers set the ledger.
2. Is anyone among the three a headliner or keynote? That decides the 45-minute exception.
3. Who owns the speaker budget, and who owns the grid? You need names for the escalation.
4. How many speakers in total, and how many travel internationally? That decides cadence and whether concierge rungs matter.
5. What is the effort ceiling: your hours per week and whether any expense line exists for a speakers' dinner and gifts?
6. Who is speaking for the first time at this scale?
7. What is the recording intent per format, and where were speakers told about it? Does any speaker present employer-restricted material?
8. What does the venue give you: green room, house A/V technician, confidence monitor?
9. Is the room mixed (non-technical attendees alongside developers)? If so, add the depth gradient and an anonymous question channel.
10. Is the event recurring? If yes, promote the speakers' dinner, since it is the rung that turns speakers into next year's referrals.

## 9. Measure two things

Pick two and record them before the event:
- **Material completeness at each deadline** (a gate): every speaker has their bio, headshot, title, A/V needs and travel details by the date each becomes unusable.
- **Consent-record completeness** (a gate): every published recording has a record naming format, licence and opt-out answer.

Also worth counting: A/V surprises discovered on stage, with the target at zero.

- The decision date slipping. If Oct 21 passes without answers, escalate again rather than telling speakers more than you know.
