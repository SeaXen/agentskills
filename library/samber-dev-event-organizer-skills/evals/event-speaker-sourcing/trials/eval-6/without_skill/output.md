**Don't put the keynote in the blind-review queue.** Run a structured, scored shortlist instead.

## Why blind review fails here

1. **You can't blind it.** All three are known by their recorded talks, so anonymity is impossible.
2. **Blind review judges abstracts.** A keynote is a booking. What matters is delivery, draw and fit, and those are invisible in a blind pool.
3. **It asks for unpaid work.** None of the three submitted to your CFP. Asking for a proposal pushes a cost onto people you want to pay.

Fairness comes from the process, not from anonymity: same criteria, same evidence for all three, independent scoring.

## How to pick (about 3 hours of committee time)

1. **Fix criteria before looking.** Write the keynote's job in one sentence, e.g. "Send 400 attendees into day one with one idea they repeat in the hallway." Then score each candidate 1–5 on these, weighted:
   - **Stage delivery (30%).** Watch one full recording each, not clips. Judge pacing, energy and handling of a big room.
   - **Topic fit (25%).** Does the theme serve your attendees' problems? Does the speaker have a point of view, not a survey?
   - **Freshness (15%).** Do the recent talks repeat the same material? Will they write a new talk for you?
   - **Reliability (20%).** Run the reference check below.
   - **Practical fit (10%).** Date availability, travel burden, English fluency for your audience, recording consent.
2. **Score independently.** Three to five committee members score alone, then compare. Anyone with a tie to a candidate declares it and sits that candidate out.
3. **Close the "nobody vouches for them" gap.** Find two organizers per candidate who hosted them, from their talk history. Send the same five questions to each:
   - Did they arrive and deliver on time?
   - Did they match the brief?
   - Were they easy to work with?
   - How did the audience react?
   - Would you book them again?
4. **Hold a 20-minute call with the top two.** Ask about their idea for your event and their availability. Their answers, and how they handle the call, are evidence.
5. **Decide, then rank.** Pick a first choice and a backup. Record the scores so you can justify the pick later.

## What to hand the invitation writer

Give them these fields:

- **The offer.** EUR 3,000 fee plus flights. Confirm before sending whether hotel, ground transport, meals and visa costs are covered. The brief only says flights, and this is the most common source of friction.
- **The event.** Name, date, city, venue, expected audience size and profile, and the slot (opening keynote, length in minutes, Q&A or none).
- **The brief.** The one-sentence job above, plus 2–3 themes you'd like. Say whether they can reuse an existing talk or you want a new one.
- **Why them.** One specific sentence about a talk of theirs you watched. It shouldn't sound like a template.
- **Terms.** Recording and distribution rights, slide sharing, A/V and rehearsal, code of conduct, payment timing (invoice terms and currency), and cancellation terms.
- **The ask.** A reply deadline (7 days is reasonable), one named contact, and how to say no. Make declining easy.
- **Tone and constraints.** Short, warm and specific. Don't name the other candidates or say you're comparing them.

The writer should draft in this order: why them, the offer, the logistics, the deadline.

## Sequencing

- Invite your **first choice only**, with a deadline.
- If they decline or go silent, invite the backup on the same terms.
- When the speaker is confirmed, send a courteous note to the others.
