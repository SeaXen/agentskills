# Verdict

**Do not make either sponsor carry 1M/2M insurance. Do not delete the section either. Shrink it to a short conditional clause.**

The 1M/2M section is built for sponsors who create their own on-site risk. Yours don't. The real insurance question is whether *you* and the venue are covered.

## Why neither sponsor needs the full limits

| Sponsor | On-site risk they create | Insurance ask |
|---|---|---|
| A: 1,500 USD + swag | None. No staff, and swag is delivered | None. Only require swag to be new, safe, and legally distributable |
| B: 2 mentors + pizza | Two people on site for one day. Pizza is bought from a caterer or delivery vendor | Short written confirmation that their staff are covered by the employer's own workers' compensation or employer's liability |

- **Pizza:** food liability sits with the vendor who makes it, not with the sponsor who pays. Buying pizza is not a hosting activity.
- **Mentors:** they are employees of Sponsor B and covered by their employer, so you don't need a certificate. Add a clause that they follow your code of conduct and the venue rules.
- **Cash:** money carries no physical risk. Cash sponsors sometimes ask *you* for insurance or indemnity, so check whether their paperwork does.
- **Swag:** the risk is low unless it's electronics, batteries, food, or anything edible. Ask for those to be cleared with you first.

## Where the real exposure is

1. **The venue's policy covers the venue, not necessarily your event.** Many coworking policies cover the operator's premises liability only. Get the venue contract and check three things:
   - Does the policy cover third-party events held by tenants or renters?
   - Does the venue require *you* to carry event liability, or to name them as additional insured?
   - Who is liable if a participant is injured or equipment is damaged?
2. **Your own cover.** If you run this through a non-profit, check whether its existing civil liability policy covers a one-day event with 60 guests. If not, a one-day event liability policy is usually cheap. I can't quote a price for your jurisdiction, so get a quote.
3. **Participants.** Add a short waiver or code-of-conduct acceptance at registration. 60 people is small, but this costs nothing.
4. **Alcohol.** If beer appears, the risk changes: host liquor liability and venue rules apply. Pizza only keeps it simple.

## What to do with the template

Replace the big section with this. It keeps the insurance protection but only triggers when it's needed.

> **Insurance.** Each Sponsor is responsible for insuring its own personnel, property, and products. A Sponsor whose personnel attend the Event confirms that they are covered by the Sponsor's workers' compensation or equivalent employer's liability insurance. Sponsors that provide only funds, merchandise, or catering purchased from a third-party vendor are not required to carry additional insurance. If a Sponsor proposes an on-site activity beyond attendance of its personnel (such as a booth, demonstration, equipment, vehicles, food preparation, or alcohol service), the Organizer may require commercial general liability insurance of at least [1,000,000] per occurrence and [2,000,000] aggregate, with a certificate provided before the Event. The Organizer maintains the insurance required by the venue and will provide proof on request.

Keep the original 1M/2M text in your master template. It is what you need when a sponsor brings a booth, a demo, or a car.

## Next steps (about 30–45 minutes)

1. Read the venue contract and insurance clauses. Ask the venue for their certificate and requirements for tenant events.
2. Check your organization's own liability policy for this event.
3. Swap in the clause above and send it to both sponsors.
4. Get Sponsor B's one-line confirmation about staff coverage.
5. Add a participant waiver or code-of-conduct acceptance to registration.

This is practical guidance, not legal advice. If the venue contract or a sponsor's paperwork has unusual indemnity language, have a lawyer read just that part.
