## Verdict on the ZoomInfo export

**Do not use it as a mailing list. Treat it as an optional lookup only.**

1. **Wrong people.** DevRel and marketing staff mostly do not own a sponsorship budget. A cold email to 4,000 of them goes to the people who can't say yes.
2. **Wrong companies.** Nordic infrastructure vendors are a title filter, not a Rust filter. Most of the 4,000 have no Rust hiring, no Rust product and no reason to be in front of 250 Rust developers.
3. **Licence risk.** ZoomInfo terms normally forbid passing an export to third parties. Your friend likely breached them by giving it to you, and you would be using the data.
4. **Data-protection risk.** The contacts are EU residents. You would be the controller of data with unknown origin, with no notice given to the people on it. Finnish rules on electronic direct marketing add more. Ask a lawyer or Traficom before any bulk send. I am not sure of the exact current rule, so treat this as a flag, not a ruling.
5. **Reputation risk.** Spam from a first-edition conference with no track record burns the Rust-company goodwill you need. The Rust community talks.
6. **It does not fix your real gap.** You have no sponsor history and no warm path. A bigger list does not give you either.

**Safe use, only if you still want it:** after you pick 30–50 target companies yourself, check whether your friend's data names the right person at those companies. Prefer finding that person yourself on LinkedIn or the company's own events and careers pages. Send individual, personal emails only.

## Where sponsors come from

Ranked by how likely they are to say yes, best first. Four organizers with no corporate ties and no history means trust has to come from somewhere else.

1. **Employers of your speakers.** Companies fund the travel and visibility of their own staff. Once you accept a talk, ask the speaker who at their company handles events budget. This is your warmest path.
2. **Sponsors of peer Rust conferences.** Anyone who paid for RustWeek, EuroRust, Oxidize, RustLab or Rust Nation UK has already shown they buy Rust events. Start with the sheet from the next action above, then look at the other lists in your own search.
3. **Companies hiring Rust engineers in Europe.** Recruiting is the strongest reason for a first-edition sponsor. Search job boards for "Rust" in Finland, the Nordics and remote-EU roles. A sponsorship is cheap compared with agency fees.
4. **Tampere and Finnish local players.** Candidates to check, none confirmed as Rust users: Tampere's embedded, telecom and industrial employers, Tampere University, and local dev-tooling shops. Also ask Business Tampere and the city's convention bureau about venue support, grants or in-kind help. Local public bodies often fund a first-edition international event more readily than companies do.
5. **Rust-ecosystem vendors.** Embedded and safety-critical specialists, tooling firms, cloud and database companies with Rust in their stack. Expect small cheques but real interest in 250 Rust developers.

**Also worth a message:** the Rust Foundation and community-grant programs. They are unlikely to give much, but they sometimes help with visibility or travel scholarships.

## Make the offer easy for a first edition

Sponsors fear paying for an event that then fails. Remove that fear.

- **Founding sponsor tier.** Cap it at 3–4 places at a clearly low price, with the name "founding" for good. Starting hypothesis, to adjust to your costs: about €1.5k community, €4k supporting, €8k founding.
- **Attendance remedy.** If attendance is under a stated number, refund part of the fee. It is a strong trust signal for an unproven event.
- **Sell on attendee mix, not headcount.** Publish role split, seniority and country for registrants once you have early data. Developers hired through you are your best argument.
- **Team tickets.** Companies that won't sponsor may buy 5–10 tickets. That is revenue and a sponsor lead in one.
- **In-kind.** Venue, AV, coffee, speaker travel. Accept these from the first edition, but get deliverables in writing.
- **Keep the conduct rules visible.** No pitch-first booths, no attendee-list sales, no purchased talks. This is what Rust developers want, and it separates you from trade-show events.

## Timing (today is 2026-10-15; event is May 2027)

1. **Now to end of October:** build the target sheet, publish a one-page sponsor prospectus, open the CFP.
2. **November–December:** first messages. Many calendar-year companies set 2027 budgets now. After January your ask lands in a spent budget.

4. **February–March:** second wave, using the announced sponsors as proof.
5. **Reserve:** keep 15–20% of sponsorship targets unclaimed for late buyers.

## Outreach rules

- One personal email per company, to a named person, referring to something real (their Rust job posting, their talk at another event).
- Never send a bulk blast, never buy or borrow a list.
- Target about 40 companies in the first wave. Expect roughly 10–20% to reply and a few to sign, so plan for 4–8 sponsors, not 40.
- Log every reply and every no, and ask the no's why. That is next year's pitch.
