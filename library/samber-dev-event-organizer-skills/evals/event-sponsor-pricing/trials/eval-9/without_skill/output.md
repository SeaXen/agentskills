## Willingness-to-pay study

Three problems with the study as the board framed it:

- **Small sample.** A sponsor base is probably 30–80 companies. A statistical survey will not hold up. Mix quantitative and interview methods.
- **Strategic answers.** Sponsors know the answers set next year's price. Expect lowballing. Do not use a pure stated-price question.
- **Survivorship bias.** Current sponsors tell you nothing about the ones who left or never bought.

Do this instead:

1. **Use revealed preference first.** Past prices paid, renewal rates by tier, sell-out speed and rejected quotes are stronger evidence than any answer.
2. **Run 12–15 interviews, not a mass survey.** Cover current sponsors, lapsed ones, and 5 prospects who never bought. Ask what each sponsorship replaced in their budget, and what a lead or meeting is worth to them.
3. **Use MaxDiff or a simple conjoint on package components.** This ranks what they value, such as speaking slots, leads, the AI track and regional presence. It works better than asking for a price.
4. **Use Van Westendorp only as a sanity band.** It was built for consumer products and misleads in B2B.
5. **Test the new numbers in the market.** Release the 2027 tiers in waves and watch sell-through. Use a waitlist or sealed bid for scarce items.

Keep the survey data and each sponsor's pricing confidential. Never let competing sponsors see each other's prices or discuss prices together.

## AI track exclusivity

Define the product before you price it:

- **Scope.** Is it category exclusivity (no other AI-platform vendor on the track), naming rights, or both? Say what "exclusive" excludes.
- **Competitor list.** Name the categories that are blocked. Fix the term, the renewal right and the exit conditions.
- **Price floor.** The premium must be at least the revenue you give up by turning away other AI-track sponsors. Add up what 3–5 non-exclusive slots would earn. Exclusivity must beat that number, or sell the non-exclusive slots instead.
- **Editorial independence.** The program committee chooses speakers. The sponsor gets no say over CFP selection. Say so in the contract and in public.
- **No history.** The track is new, so you have no audience data. Use registration interest by track and early signals. Offer a founding-partner discount plus a first-renewal right, and avoid a high fixed price for an unproven asset.
- **Vendor conflict.** If the parent competes in AI, decide whether it may speak on the track. Settle this before you sell exclusivity.

## Three-event bundle

Reply to the two sponsors this week with: "Interested. Terms follow once the 2027 rate card is set, by [date]." Do not quote or sign before then. They would anchor your whole price structure.

Policy points to settle first:

1. **Discount cap.** Set a maximum bundle discount, for example 10–15% off the sum of standalone prices, and justify it by committed volume and lower sales cost.
2. **Different assets.** A 2,000-person flagship and a 400-person regional day are not the same product. Price each on its own value, then add the bundle discount.
3. **Revenue allocation.** Decide how bundle revenue splits across the three events, especially if they have separate P&Ls or legal entities.
4. **Exclusivity conflicts.** The bundle must not carry AI track exclusivity unless the track exists at all three events. If it does not, say exactly what the bundle includes.
5. **Contract terms.** Handle cancellation, force majeure and make-goods per event. Decide whether the price is locked for 2027 only or longer. Add no most-favoured-nation clause.

Expect the same ask from every large sponsor once the bundle exists. Write the bundle as a standing offer with published rules, not a one-off deal.
