# What to tell a small sponsor of Larkmoor Days

## The short answer

Don't sell her a package. She can't use one, and you can't honestly promise leads from 250 people. Offer her three things:

1. A named line item her money pays for.
2. Recognition that's true: she's from the community and has spoken twice.
3. A direct conversation about what she wants, before either of you commits.

Her money is a patron's gift. Call it that.

## What not to offer

These go against the norms for community events:

- **A speaking slot in exchange for money.** If she wants to speak, she goes through the CFP like everyone else. Her two earlier talks are her credential.
- **The attendee list or badge scans.** Attendees didn't consent to that. A small startup also can't work 250 scans.
- **Promised leads, hires or pipeline.** You already know the numbers don't support it. Saying so is better than overselling.

## What her money can honestly buy

Pick one line item, then name it publicly. A named item is concrete for her, and for the attendees it reads as support, not advertising.

| Option | What she funds | What she gets |
|---|---|---|
| **Speaker travel or hotel** | One speaker's costs | Named thank-you on the speaker page and from the stage |
| **Scholarship or ticket fund** | Free tickets for students or people between jobs | Named as the fund's supporter. This one lines up with her values best. |
| **Food or coffee for one session** | The after-lunch coffee, say | "Coffee by [her company]" signage |
| **After-party round or drinks tab** | A social slot | A short toast, and the chance to talk to people who come over |
| **Community-room table** | A small table, if you have space | Staffed by her engineers, so she meets practitioners one on one. This is the only option that gets her real conversations. |

My recommendation is the **scholarship fund, plus a table if she wants one**. It costs her little time, and it fits the standing she already has with this community. A four-person company can't staff a booth for two days, so don't suggest one.

Give her a short, honest list of recognition:

- Her name and logo on the sponsors page, at the same size as other small sponsors.
- A thank-you from the stage, with at most a one-sentence mention.
- Her company named next to the line item she funded.

## Ask her one question first

Before you quote anything, ask what she'd like out of it. Her answer decides the package, and it may turn out she wants no return at all.

Here is a message you can send:

> Hi [name],
>
> Thank you. That means a lot, and it's very much in the spirit of the event. I want to be upfront: Larkmoor Days is about 250 people, so I can't promise leads or hiring, and I'd rather not oversell it. We also don't sell speaking slots or attendee data.
>
> What I can offer is to put your money toward something specific and name you for it. For example:
>
> - the scholarship / free-ticket fund,
> - a speaker's travel,
> - the coffee or social slot,
>
> and, if you'd like, a small table so your team can talk to people directly.
>
> Which of these feels right? And is there anything you'd hope to get out of it, even something small like meeting a few people or giving back? Tell me and I'll shape it around that.
>
> Whatever you decide, thank you for backing the event.

## A note on her expectations

- If she says "I just want to support it," take the money, name the line item, and thank her. Don't invent benefits.
- If she wants hires or customers, tell her plainly that 250 people is a small pool. Suggest the table, and suggest she keep speaking through the CFP, which does more for her visibility than a logo.
- Agree the amount and the line item in writing, even if it's two lines in an email. That avoids a misunderstanding later about what she paid for.

## Questions I couldn't settle from what you gave me

- Does Larkmoor Days already have a scholarship or ticket fund, or would this be new?
- Do you have table space?
- Roughly what amount is "small"? It affects which row fits. A ticket fund works at almost any size, but a speaker's travel may not.
