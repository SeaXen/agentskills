# Kestrel Summit: moving to anonymous review for this year's CFP

## Recommendation

Switch to anonymous review on Monday, but do these things:

- Tell submitters first.
- Use two stages, not pure blind.
- Measure the effect.
- Don't claim it as the fix for last year's imbalance.

Anonymous review can reduce bias, but the evidence for fixing a gender gap this way is mixed. The main driver is usually who submits. If 10% of the 310 proposals come from women, a fair blind process will still produce about 3 women in 28 slots. Check the submission mix before you announce anything.

## Step 0: Diagnose (30 minutes, before Monday)

Someone who is not on the committee should pull two numbers for last year and this year:

1. **Submission mix:** the share of submitters who are women or from other underrepresented groups. Your intake form may not hold this data. Don't guess gender from names. If you don't have it, add an optional self-reported demographic survey, described below.
2. **Acceptance rate by group:** accepted divided by submitted, per group.

What the numbers mean:

- **Acceptance rates differ.** Review bias is plausible, so anonymization targets the right problem.
- **Acceptance rates are similar and submissions are skewed.** The problem is the pipeline. Anonymization won't help much, and you need outreach (see "Measures that move the number").

## Step 1: Tell submitters (do this first)

Your published call said reviewers would see names and bios. Changing the rules is your right, but submitters wrote their proposals under the old terms. Send one short email before Monday that says:

- Review is now anonymous for stage 1 (names, bios and affiliations hidden).
- Bios are revealed at stage 2, for the shortlist only.
- Nobody needs to resubmit. Offer a 48-hour window to edit the abstract if it names the author, employer or past talks in ways that identify them.
- The decision date is unchanged.

Don't change the terms silently. People who invested in strong bios will notice, and the trust cost is higher than the delay.

## Step 2: Two-stage review design

**Stage 1: anonymous scoring of all 310 proposals**
- Reviewers see title, abstract, outline, target audience and level, and what attendees will learn.
- Reviewers don't see name, bio, employer, photo, links to personal repos or sites, or past-talk history.
- Score on a written rubric with a 1–5 scale. Suggested criteria: topic relevance, clarity of the abstract, takeaway for attendees, originality, and evidence the talk is concrete (demo, data, case).
- Assign at least 3 reviewers per proposal, and randomize assignments.
- Recusal: reviewers who submitted proposals, or who recognize an author from the content, skip that proposal and flag it.

**Stage 2: shortlist of about 60–70 (roughly 2.5× the slots), de-anonymized**
- Reveal bios for the shortlist only.
- Use them for delivery risk, conflicts of interest, duplicate topics and program balance.
- Don't re-score on the person. Bios can break ties or flag real concerns (for example, no evidence the person can present the topic).
- The final selection of 28 is made by the chair plus 2 reviewers, using the stage 1 scores as the default order.

## Step 3: Redaction by Monday

Redacting 310 proposals by hand in four days is the main practical risk.

1. **Who:** a coordinator who is not on the committee. They hold the key linking proposals to authors.
2. **How:** export from your CFP tool (Sessionize, Pretalx, a spreadsheet or whatever you use). Most CFP tools have a built-in anonymous-review or hidden-speaker mode, so check that first. If yours doesn't, export only the content fields and assign a random ID per proposal.
3. **Scrub:** search abstracts for the author's name, employer, "my book", "our product", repo and blog URLs, and "at [Company], we". Replace with `[redacted]`. Don't rewrite content.
4. **Spot-check:** a second person reads 30 random redacted proposals to see whether they can still identify the author.
5. **Access control:** reviewers get a view without author fields. Verify by logging in as a reviewer account before Monday.

**Fallback if redaction isn't done by Monday:** start the committee on the batches that are ready, or move the start to Wednesday. A late, clean process is better than a half-anonymous one announced as fully blind.

## Step 4: Measure whether it worked

- Collect an **optional, self-reported** demographic survey from all submitters, stored by the coordinator and never shown to reviewers. Tell people it's used only for aggregate monitoring.
- After stage 1 scoring, compute for each group: share of submissions, share of the shortlist, and share of accepted talks.
- Don't adjust scores to hit a target. Use the data to evaluate the process and to decide next year's outreach.
- Publish aggregate numbers after the program is announced, whatever they show.

## Measures that move the number

Anonymization alone is unlikely to be enough. Pair it with:

- **Targeted outreach:** reach out to communities and meetups for underrepresented speakers, and reopen or extend the CFP for a week if the mix is badly skewed.
- **Invited or mentored slots:** reserve a small number of slots for invited speakers, and offer mentoring and rehearsal support to first-time submitters.
- **Committee composition:** diversify the reviewers.
- **Speaker support:** travel and accommodation support, and clear childcare and accessibility information, which affect who submits next year.

Check with your legal contact before using any explicit gender targets or quotas in selection. The rules differ by country.

## Announcement wording

Avoid "diversity measure" as the headline. It over-promises, and critics can check it. Something like:

> This year, Kestrel Summit's first-round review is anonymous. Reviewers score proposals on content alone; speaker bios are shared only at the shortlist stage. We're also tracking submission and acceptance data by group and will publish the results after the program is announced. Last year's speaker line-up was not balanced, and this is one of several steps we're taking, alongside targeted outreach and speaker support.

## Points for the two board members

- You are changing rules after the call was published, so tell submitters first.
- You can't promise a gender outcome from review changes alone. You can promise a fairer process and published data.
- The decision belongs to the board, but the chair should confirm the Monday date is feasible once Step 3 is done.
