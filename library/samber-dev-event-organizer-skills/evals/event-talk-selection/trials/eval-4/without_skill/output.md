## 1. Reviewer math

I assumed 15 minutes per review and a cap of 20 to 35 reviews per volunteer. Adjust if yours differ.

- 2,100 submissions × 2 reviews = 4,200 reviews. Across 32 reviewers that is 131 each, and 3 reviews each would be 197 each.
- A realistic volunteer cap is 20 to 35 short reviews. 32 reviewers can cover about 640 to 1,100 reviews, which is 0.3 to 0.5 reviews per submission.
- Your structure cannot work at this volume, however well you assign the work. Last year's failure was probably the same math landing on two people.

## 2. Structure

1. **Recruit to about 100 reviewers.** Cap each at 35 reviews. Sources: past speakers, accepted speakers from earlier years, track chairs' networks. A further option is reciprocal reviewing, where submitters review 3 to 5 others. That adds load but carries conflict and quality risk.
2. **Name 4 track chairs from your strongest reviewers.** Each track chair owns assignment, score calibration, the shortlist and the track's slot quota. You and your co-chair review nothing.
3. **Your roles as chairs:**
   - Set quotas.
   - Calibrate across tracks.
   - Handle appeals and conflicts of interest.
   - Build the schedule.
4. **Use platform tooling.** HotCRP or OpenReview with reviewer bidding, auto-assignment, conflict-of-interest declaration and a hard per-reviewer cap. Nobody assigns by hand.
5. **Review in two rounds:**
   - Round 1: every submission gets 1 review. Track chairs confirm clear rejects with a quick second look.
   - Round 2: roughly the top third (about 700) get 2 more reviews.
   - Total is about 3,900 short reviews, which fits 100 reviewers at 35 each.
6. **Decide in three steps:**
   - Normalize scores per reviewer.
   - Auto-accept the clear top of each track.
   - Discuss only the borderline band, about ±30% around each track quota.

Allocate the 96 slots by track in proportion to submissions, so acceptance rates are equal across tracks. For example, a track with 600 of 2,100 submissions gets about 27 slots.

## 3. The 25% acceptance floor: don't adopt it

- 96 of 2,100 is 4.6%. A 25% floor means about 525 talks, 5.5 times your capacity.
- CHI's 26.4% is the outcome of its capacity and its archival-paper model. It is not a target other conferences should copy.
- Acceptance rate follows from slots and quality. Setting a rate as a goal forces you to either lower the bar or add days and rooms you don't have.
- The board's real concern is wasting good submissions. These measures address that:
  1. Keep a waitlist of about 10 to 15 alternates for dropouts.
  2. Offer an overflow format (lightning talks or posters) only if you can staff it.
  3. Send every submitter their anonymized reviews.
  4. Give rejected submitters a clear pointer to other venues.
