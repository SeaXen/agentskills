# Reviewer conflict-of-interest policy, and what to do if someone refuses

**Do first:** send all seven reviewers the policy below and collect a written conflict declaration from each before Thursday. About 30 minutes of work.

## Short answer

| Case | Verdict | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Marisol and the submitter on her team | ❌ Note is not enough. Conflict. | Recuse from that proposal only. |
| Reviewer 90% sure he knows an author from style | ℹ️ A guess is not a conflict. | Depends on whether he has a relationship with the guessed author (see below). |
| Reviewer at a direct competitor of three submitters' employers | ❌ Conflict. | Recuse from those three proposals only. |

Recusal is per proposal. Nobody leaves the panel for declaring a conflict.

## Why Marisol's note fails

1. **The score still counts.** Disclosure doesn't remove the bias. It only labels it.
2. **The note breaks anonymity for the other six reviewers.** "I know him" reveals that this proposal comes from someone near Marisol, and that can anchor their scoring.
3. **Her working relationship is a standard conflict.** They share an employer and a team, and she sits next to him. Academic and large-conference review policies treat that as a conflict.

**Fix:** you, the organizer, remove that proposal from her queue. If your tool allows it, do this silently so she never sees which one it was. She scores the other proposals as normal.

## The "90% sure from the writing style" reviewer

Ask him one private question: "Do you have a relationship or rivalry with the person you think wrote it?"

- **Yes (colleague, friend, co-author, rival):** treat it as a conflict. Remove the proposal from his queue. He tells only you who he suspects.
- **No (he just recognises the voice):** he scores as normal.

Either way, three rules apply:
- He doesn't say his guess to other reviewers. A 90% guess can be wrong, and a wrong name would taint the proposal.
- He doesn't try to confirm it (no searching the abstract, no asking around).
- He doesn't let the guess raise or lower his score.

## The competitor reviewer

1. Remove the three proposals from his queue. You hold the identity key, so he doesn't need to self-identify.
2. This covers two risks. He could mark a rival down, and he would see unpublished technical content from a competitor.
3. If his employer competes with other submitters' employers, extend the same rule to those proposals. Check the full submitter list against all seven employers.

## Keeping scores fair

- **Remove proposals before scoring.** Don't let a reviewer score and then drop the score. That invites hindsight bias.
- **Use the mean of available scores, not the sum.** A proposal with five reviews would otherwise lose to one with seven.
- **Set a minimum of four reviews per proposal.** With seven reviewers and about four recusals in total, every proposal should still have at least five. Check this after you remove the conflicts.
- **Recused reviewers stay out of discussion** of those proposals and don't see others' scores on them.
- **Log every conflict and removal** in a private organizer file.

## Policy text to paste

> Before scoring, each reviewer declares conflicts to the organizer privately. Conflicts are: same employer or team as an author, close personal or professional relationship, co-authorship, or employment at a direct competitor of an author's employer. A reviewer who suspects an author's identity from style reports it only if a relationship exists, and only to the organizer. Conflicted reviewers do not score, discuss, or view scores for that proposal. Disclosure notes do not substitute for recusal. The organizer decides edge cases and may remove a reviewer who breaks these rules.

## If someone refuses to step back

1. **Check the case type first.** Many "refusals" are just a guess-from-style case that never needed recusal.
2. **Explain that it protects them.** "This protects you from accusations and protects the submitter's fair shot. It's one proposal, not the panel."
3. **Enforce it technically, with no consent needed.** Remove the proposal from their queue. If they already have access, exclude their score on it and log that you did.
4. **Give a clear choice if they still insist on scoring:** "Recuse on this proposal, or step down from the panel." With seven reviewers you can absorb losing one.
5. **Remove them from the panel for any of these:** scoring after being told not to, leaking authors' identities, or lobbying others on a conflicted proposal. Record the reason in your log.
6. **Cover your own conflicts.** If you know a submitter, declare it and have a co-organizer or a non-reviewer handle that proposal. If you're the only organizer, state your conflict openly on the review page.

## Assumptions to check

- The review is blind: reviewers can't see author names. If it isn't, the policy is stricter, and every reviewer needs the full submitter list for their declaration.
- You can hide individual proposals per reviewer in your tool. If not, use a spreadsheet assignment and give each conflicted reviewer a list that omits their proposals.
