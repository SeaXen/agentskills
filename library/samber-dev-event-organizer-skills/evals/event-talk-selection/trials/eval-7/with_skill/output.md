# Millbrook Devfest: how to send the 56 rejections

**Recommendation:** send per-proposal feedback built from the reviewers' real comments, edited for tone. Don't send one identical note, and don't soften anything into "scheduling constraints".

I haven't seen your call text, announcement date or blind-review promise. Where my answer depends on those, I say so.

## 1. Why feedback wins at your size

The skill ranks decline communication on three rungs: per-proposal feedback, decline plus criteria recap, and form decline. Feedback costs the most per email. Three conditions promote it, and you meet all three:

| Promotion condition | Your situation |
| --- | --- |
| Pool small enough to write every one | 74 proposals, about 56 notes |
| First-time-heavy pool where growing the speaker base is the point | 41 of 74 (55%) are first-timers |
| Rubric already mandates written comments | The text exists. Only editing is left. |

The constraint is editorial time, not mechanism. The skill reports no data that feedback brings declined submitters back. Treat that as a bet and measure it (section 6).

## 2. Why the identical two-liner loses here

- You'd throw away the one asset you already paid for, a comment on every score.
- Most of the 41 first-timers have no other source of feedback. A form note tells them nothing about whether to try again.
- It's cheap and honest, but the skill says it "buys nothing beyond not being silent". For a community event that wants a bigger speaker base, that's the wrong trade.

## 3. Why "scheduling constraints" is out

The decline template for this rung sets three rules:

1. **Report what reviewers actually wrote.** Invented softer reasons "produce feedback a speaker cannot act on". They also contradict the decision if the speaker asks a follow-up.
2. **Strip attribution and reviewer names.** The feedback comes from the committee.
3. **Say plainly that the feedback explains this decision, not a general assessment of the speaker.**

A made-up reason also causes concrete problems:

- A first-timer told "scheduling" will resubmit the same weak abstract next year.
- A submitter who asks "which slot clashed?" gets an answer nobody can give.
- It breaks any criteria your call published, because you'd be declining on a reason that isn't your selection basis.

Your co-organiser is right about one thing: tone matters. Soften the wording and keep the substance. For example, "the abstract doesn't say what attendees will be able to do afterwards" is kind enough and still true.

## 4. How to keep the cost down

Not all 56 need the same depth. The tiers below are my own design, not a published pattern.

| Tier | Who | What they get | Rough effort (my estimate, not sourced) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Shortlisted but cut, plus waitlisted | Full edited feedback | 10–15 min each |
| B | Mid-scoring | Two to four sentences synthesized from the reviewer comments | 5–8 min each |
| C | Bottom, or comments too thin to use | The default rung: criteria recap plus the 74:18 ratio, with one concrete line if a comment supports it | 2–3 min each |

At that pace the total is roughly 5–8 hours across two organisers. Check it against your announcement deadline, since I don't know it.

Editing rules:

- Merge several reviewers into one voice. Drop any line that could identify a reviewer.
- Cut comments that are off-topic, snide or about the person rather than the proposal. Keep the substantive point.
- If reviewers disagreed, say so. "Reviewers differed on X" is honest and useful.
- Don't send raw scores. They invite arguments the rubric can't settle.
- Decide before you start whether to promise feedback in the email at all. Don't promise it to anyone you might not get to.

## 5. Order of sending and draft copy

Per the skill's notification sequence:

1. Send acceptances, each with a confirmation deadline.
2. Send waitlist notices at the same time as acceptances.
3. Send declines only once acceptances are confirmed.
4. Announce the program.

If your call promised a notification date, hit it. Missing it costs more trust than any wording recovers.

**Decline with feedback (tiers A and B):**

> Thank you for submitting **"{title}"** to Millbrook Devfest. We're not able to include it this year.
>
> We received 74 proposals for 18 slots, so we declined many talks we'd have liked to program. Selection followed the criteria we published with the call: {criteria in one line}.
>
> Here is what the reviewers said about your proposal: {edited, merged comments}.
>
> This feedback explains this decision on this pool, on one day. It isn't a general assessment of you as a speaker.
>
> We'd genuinely like to see you submit again for {next edition}.

**Waitlist:**

> Your proposal **"{title}"** is on our waitlist for Millbrook Devfest. It made our final shortlist and would be programmed if a slot opens. You'll hear from us either way by **{date}**. Please don't hold travel on our behalf before then.

Promise the date, never the odds. No data exists on how often waitlisted talks get promoted. Size the waitlist before sending anything. The documented example is about five, for a single-track event.

## 6. Two checks that remain

- **Who sees declined names?** If the call promised blind review, the skill's cheapest fairness commitment applies. Decide which of your people ever sees a declined submitter's name, and write it down. Edit the comments in a way that doesn't require unmasking.
- **Record the return rate.** Count how many declined submitters, and especially first-timers, resubmit next edition. That's the only real test of whether this effort was worth it. Write down now what result would make you drop back to the cheaper rung.

## 7. What to tell your co-organiser

You and they want the same thing: fewer hours and no angry replies. The tiering gets most of the speed. The honest comments get the trust. A made-up reason saves ten minutes today and costs a follow-up exchange the first time someone asks.
