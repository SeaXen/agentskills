# Cadence 2026: invited vs. open lanes, and the sponsor ask

**Recommendation: do neither.** Keep two lanes. The 14 open slots stay a real, blind-reviewed contest. The 20 invited sessions stay outside it. If the platinum sponsors speak, they take labeled slots outside the 14.

This is a vendor/user conference, so mostly invited content is legitimate. The 14-slot community slice is where the rules below apply.

## Assumptions to confirm first

I couldn't ask these, so the answer rests on the defaults below.

1. **What the call published.** I assume it advertised 14 open slots, or at least didn't promise all 34. Check this against the call text before anything else.
   - If the call said "34 sessions, all open", the invited lane is a published-promise problem. Disclose the split to all 160 submitters now, before decisions go out.
2. **Blind review.** I assume the call promised anonymous review of the open pool.
3. **Sponsors in the pool.** I assume neither platinum sponsor submitted. Say so if they did.
4. **Reading capacity and structure.** I don't know how many people can read 160 proposals, or whether Cadence is single-track.

## Head of content: don't pool the 20 invited into blind review

Reject it for four reasons.

- **Blind review of invited talks can't work.** Staff and named industry people are invited because of who they are. Anonymizing them is impossible, and pooling them corrupts the review of the open pool too (the skill lists this as a failure mode). Reviewers would score on recognizable identity and style.
- **It breaks commitments.** You invited those speakers months ago. Putting them through a contest they might lose rescinds the invitation.
- **It shrinks the open lane.** Pooled, 160 + 20 compete for 34 slots. The invited 20 likely win on name recognition, and the 14 external slots could fall below 14. Submitters were told one thing and get another.
- **The fairness goal is real, and blind review is the wrong tool.** Meet it this way:
  - Publish the structure: "20 curated sessions, 14 selected from open call".
  - Have a named owner review the invited lane against the same quality bar, openly rather than blind.
  - Apply the lesson-transfer test to any invited vendor talk.
  - Give invited staff slots a written cap, with disclosure and abstention.

## VP of Sales: don't hand sponsors 2 of the 14 open slots

The community-pole rule is that no slot is bought. A vendor conference may curate its own content, so this is a lane question more than an ethics one. Giving sponsors slots carved out of the open pool fails on three counts.

- **It breaks the call's promise.** The 160 submitters competed for 14 slots. Quietly making that 12 changes the odds after the fact. If the sponsor swap is announced later, it reads as paid access.
- **Sponsor talks tend to be product talks.** Run the lesson-transfer test: if the audience forgot the product name tomorrow, would they keep the lesson?
- **It can't be defended as editorial independence.** Review exists so that a slot can be explained to a declined submitter.

Offer the VP a defensible alternative instead:

1. **Option A: same review.** Platinum sponsor staff submit to the open pool (closed now, so ask whether they did). They are scored blind like everyone else, under a self-set cap on sponsor-affiliated speakers.
2. **Option B: a labeled sponsor lane.** If sponsorship includes a session, place it in the curated 20 side, never the 14. Label it "sponsored session" in the program. Both options carry these conditions:
   - No commercial demo or sales pitch.
   - Lesson-transfer test applied.
   - Same acceptance-email commercial-content line as every other speaker.
3. **Put it in the contract.** Add the editorial-independence wording and the cap to the sponsor agreement (`event-sponsor-agreement`), so it is a signed term and not a refusal.
4. **Hold the 14 open slots.** If the VP needs "2 sessions", find them in the 20-slot lane, within the commitments already made.

## Plan for the 14 open slots

Show this section by section for validation before review opens. Once scoring starts, the rules freeze.

1. **Pool math.** 160 proposals for 14 slots is about 8.75%, or 11.4:1 oversubscribed. That is healthy and in the range of PyCon US 2025 (about 15%) and KubeCon EU 2024 (9%). Record it.
2. **Committee.**
   - Default: a small consensus committee with a non-voting anonymizer plus four to six graders. Name a deadlock arbiter.
   - Capacity check: 160 proposals across five graders is heavy if each reads all of them. Assign each proposal to three or more reviewers (the only documented distributed-pool threshold), or add a first-pass cut.
   - If Cadence is multi-track and no room can read 160, use a two-tier cascade. That sizing is my interpolation, not a documented point.
3. **Rubric, written before scoring.**
   - A committee that will meet uses the letter-grade advocate scale.
   - A: will advocate. B: likes it. C: neutral. D: will advocate against. X: abstain, suspected identity.
   - Aggregate as a best-worst pair, never an average. At least one A is the floor.
   - Written comment on every score.
4. **Anonymization depth.**
   - If the call promised blind review, hire a dedicated anonymizer (platform hiding alone leaves title and abstract text exposed).
   - Name the person. Publish the step where identity re-enters: after the cut, for accepted speakers only.
   - Declined submitters are never de-anonymized.
   - No evidence shows blind review changes demographic outcomes, so call it a process commitment and not an equity result.
5. **Conflict of interest.**
   - Brief reviewers before scoring: abstain on colleagues and competitors, and on suspected identity.
   - Add organizer and staff submissions: disclose, abstain on your own and your employer's talk, and cap frequency.
   - Name the arbiter for a reviewer who won't recuse. Name the fallback for a proposal that loses too many reviewers (my own design, since no published escalation path exists).
6. **Cut rule, stated before ranking.** For example: shortlist the top about 30% (roughly 48) with at least one A, then the program-fit pass picks 14. This number is mine, not sourced.
7. **Program-fit pass, on the shortlist only.**
   - Check topic spread against the 20 invited sessions, employer spread, local vs. out-of-town, first-time speakers, topic freshness and audience depth.
   - Write down every trade. A higher-scored talk dropped for spread needs a recorded reason.
   - Consider holding one or two slots open for late invitation if the field moves fast.
8. **Waitlist.** About five ordered backups, built before anything is sent. Promise a date, never odds.
9. **Notification order.**
   - Acceptances first, with a deadline and the commercial-content line.
   - Declines once acceptances are confirmed: a warm template, the published criteria, and the 8.75% ratio.
   - Waitlist notices say plainly that a slot may never open.
   - Decide which of your people sees a declined submitter's name (ideally none).
10. **Metrics to record for next edition.**
    - Acceptance ratio.
    - Balance outcome against stated goals.
    - Reviewer coverage and recusals.
    - Declined-submitter return rate.
