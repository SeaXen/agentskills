# Riverbend Summit: selection plan

**Do not add the 20% "diversity" column.** Count first-time and end-user status as facts, and apply them as pre-declared gates at the shortlist stage. Log every swap. That setup also answers last January's argument.

I read the skill and its four references. Four items below are my own design, because the references say nobody publishes the mechanics. They are the cascade margin, the extension rule, the swap rule and the waitlist size, and I label each.

Items 1–8 follow the skill's expected output. Then come the gate-versus-goal decision, the panels, and the questions that could change this plan.

## Why not the column

- ⚠️ **A weight cannot guarantee a floor.** The board said at least 19 first-time speakers (50% of 38) and at least 10 end-user speakers (25% of 38, rounded up from 9.5). A 20% column may land above or below those numbers, so you would need a shortlist count check anyway. A gate hits the number. A weight only nudges it.
- ⚠️ **First-time and end-user status are facts, not opinions.** Read them off the form and count them. Scoring them asks reviewers to guess.
- ⚠️ **"Diversity" scored by reviewers means inferring identity.** Under blind review they can't see the speaker. Under named review they would be guessing from names, which is unreliable and unfair.
- ⚠️ **A hidden 20% feeds the ranking silently.** That is the "silent balance" failure mode, and it is what caused January. A score-rank list that quietly mixes in spread can't be explained afterwards.
- ℹ️ **Precedent.** The documented event-level targets of "at least 50% new speakers" and "at least 25% end-user companies" are **not** line items in the rubric reviewers see. The only hard composition rule documented is a gate, not a score input.
- ℹ️ **Limit of the evidence.** No published evidence says gates beat weights in outcomes. This is mechanism reasoning, not a measured result.

## 1. Committee model and size

Pool: 420 for 38 slots, which is about 11:1 or 9.0% accepted. That is close to KubeCon EU 2024 (9%).

With three tracks and a pool no single room can read, I recommend the **multi-tier cascade**, assuming the effort ceiling allows it.

- **Anonymizer(s):** one or two people who see raw submissions and never score.
- **Track committees:** each reads its own track's proposals.
- **Track chairs:** one per track.
- **Co-chairs:** make the final call. Pick an arbiter for deadlocks now.

The documented percentages don't fit your slot count. Top 30% of 420 is 126. Chairs pass about 30% of those, which is about 38, exactly your slot count. That leaves nothing to trade in the fit pass.

**My own extension:** chairs pass about 1.5× the slots, roughly 55–60 proposals, as the shortlist for the fit pass. If the committee can't read 420 in the calendar, fall back to a smaller consensus model per track. Tell me the effort ceiling and I'll re-rank.

## 2. Rubric

Use the multi-criteria star scale from the cascade model. Reviewers never meet, and an aggregated cut needs comparable scores.

- **Scale:** five stars in half-star steps.
- **Criteria:** content, originality, relevance and speaker fit.
- **Comments:** a written comment on every rating.
- **No diversity criterion.**
- **Brief on speaker fit:** "No track record is not a negative. Judge fit from the proposal." The skill's worked example shows how a first-time-speaker penalty enters through "not sure about the speaker".

Write the rubric down before anyone scores.

## 3. Anonymization and where identity re-enters

I don't know what your call promised, and the process must execute what was published.

- **If the call promised blind review:**
  - Use a dedicated anonymizer. They also hold two tags, first-time and end-user, in a sheet scorers can't see.
  - The anonymizer applies the panel gate before review, as a compliance check rather than a score.
  - Identity and tags re-enter at one published step: the shortlist, for chairs and co-chairs only.
  - State the limit: no evidence shows blind review changes outcomes by demographic. It is a process commitment, not a proven equity result.
- **If the call published named review:** anonymization is off the menu this edition. Retrofitting it now would break the promise.

If the form also carries travel-grant requests, anonymization enforces "asking for money won't hurt your proposal".

## 4. Conflict-of-interest brief and arbitration

Brief reviewers **before** scoring opens.

- **Skip the question.** Abstain on colleagues and on competing organizations. Review wearing the event hat.
- **Suspected identity.** Abstain if you think you've identified an anonymized speaker.
- **Lesser conflicts.** If you continue despite a lesser conflict, disclose it in the review text.
- **Consultancy panel.** Anyone at that consultancy abstains.
- **Organizers who submit.**
  - Disclose before scoring.
  - Abstain on your own talk and your employer's.
  - Set a written cap on organizer talks.
  - Cap sponsor-affiliated speakers too.

All of this is honour-system. The arbiter and the too-many-recusals fallback below are your own design, and the brief should say so.

- **Arbiter:** name one now.
- **Fallback:** a proposal that loses most of its reviewers goes to chairs for a direct read.

## 5. Rounds and cut rule (state before seeing any ranking)

1. Committees score, with comments on every rating.
2. The top 30% (126) advance to chairs.
3. Chairs shortlist about 55–60, with comments.
4. Co-chairs run the fit pass.

**Pre-declared extension rule (my design):** if the shortlist holds fewer than 19 first-timers or 10 end-users, chairs add the next-highest-scored proposals meeting the missing tag until the count is feasible. Log each addition. Check feasibility on the tags right after round one. If the pool can't supply the numbers, you learn it before the cut, not after.

## 6. Program-fit pass and trade log

The pass runs on the shortlist only. It covers first-time and end-user gates, track spread, employer spread, local versus out-of-town mix, and the panel gate. The field heuristics (lesson-transfer test, freshness, depth gradient) apply too, and they are heuristics, not measured findings.

**Swap rule (my design, so the log is mechanical):**

1. Take the highest-scored shortlisted proposal meeting the unmet gate.
2. It displaces the lowest-scored selected talk that isn't needed to satisfy any gate.
3. Repeat until the gates are met.

**Log every swap.** Never log "spread". One row per swap:

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| In | Talk, score, rank |
| Out | Talk, score, rank |
| Rule triggered | First-time, end-user, panel, track cap or employer cap |
| Score gap | Points between In and Out |
| Decided by | Name |
| Date | |

Keep this in the decision record. Next year's committee, and any submitter who asks, get an answer.

## 7. Waitlist and notifications

- **Waitlist:** build it before sending anything, as an ordered list. Size is an **extrapolation**, since the only documented size is about five for a single track. Use roughly 8–12 for three tracks, scaled to your decline risk.
- **Gate-preserving replacement:** if a gate-satisfying speaker withdraws, promote the next waitlisted proposal that restores the gate. Decide this now.
- **Sequence:**
  1. Acceptances go first, with a confirmation deadline.
  2. Waitlist notices go out at the same time, giving the date they'll know and no odds.
  3. Declines go out once acceptances are confirmed.
- **Decline depth:** default is a decline plus criteria recap with the ratio ("420 proposals for 38 slots"). Per-proposal feedback is better if editorial time allows, since your rubric already mandates comments.
- **Declined names:** decide which of your own people ever sees a declined submitter's name.

Draft copy, adapted from the skill's templates:

> **Accept.** Your proposal "{title}" has been accepted for Riverbend Summit, {date}. Please confirm by {deadline}, or we'll offer the slot to another speaker. {Perks as the call stated them.} What we need next: {bio, headshot, slide deadline, travel/A-V}. {If several accepted: which would you rather give? We can schedule one.} One rule for every speaker: no commercial demo or sales pitch. The audience should leave with the lesson, not the product.

> **Decline.** Thank you for submitting "{title}" to Riverbend Summit. We can't include it this year. We received 420 proposals for 38 slots, so we declined many talks we'd have liked to program. Selection followed the criteria published with the call: {one line}. We'd like to see you submit again next year.

> **Waitlist.** Your proposal "{title}" is on our waitlist for Riverbend Summit. It made our final shortlist and would be programmed if a slot opens, which sometimes happens and sometimes doesn't. You'll hear from us either way by {date}. Please don't hold travel before then.

Duplicate acceptances: let the submitter choose which talk to give.

## 8. Metrics to record for next edition

- **Acceptance ratio:** 38/420 = 9.0%. A hard swing next year usually means the call changed.
- **Balance outcome:** first-time count against 19, end-user count against 10, employer spread, local share. Compare to what was promised.
- **Reviewer coverage (self-set):** proposals per reviewer and recusal losses.
- **Declined-submitter return rate (self-set).**

For each, write the revision it would trigger before the event. Also record the rubric, the cut rule, the swap log and the specific complaint each would need to hear.

## Decide before review: gate or goal

The board wrote "must" in your account. Ask them: is 50%/25% a hard gate, or "if possible"? The published precedent says "if possible". Pin down three more things in writing:

- **Unit:** do you count speakers or sessions? Panels make this matter.
- **First-time:** first time at Riverbend, or ever at any conference?
- **End-user:** which employers qualify? Consultancies and vendors don't. Collect it as a self-declared form field, then spot-check.

If you treat them as gates, say so to reviewers and in the decline recap. If you treat them as goals, record when you missed them and why.

## Your two panels

Published precedent has a hard panel rule: any session with three or more speakers needs at least one speaker who does not identify as a man, and panelists must not all share one organization. It is the only documented hard composition rule. Adopting it is your call.

- **Four-speaker consultancy panel:** fails the shared-organization ban.
- **Three-speaker all-men panel:** fails the gender gate.

⚠️ Check how you know it's "all men". Use self-declared form data, never inference from names or looks. The consultancy panel needs the same check for the gender gate.

⚠️ If the call didn't publish this rule, rejecting outright applies a criterion nobody saw. My own design: give both panel organizers a short revision window before scoring opens, then gate-check again. Panels that don't fix it are declined with the rule stated. Send each organizer a short note naming the rule and the deadline.

Under this plan reviewers see no diversity column, the board's targets become checkable gates, and every swap leaves a row in the log.
