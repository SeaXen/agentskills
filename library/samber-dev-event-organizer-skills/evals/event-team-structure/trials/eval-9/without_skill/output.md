# Keeping all three leads for GoNorth's seventh edition

You have two separate problems. Two leads have lost the enjoyment, and one lead is caught between Sift's interests and yours. Handle them in separate conversations, and don't link them in anyone's mind.

## Next actions, in order

1. **This week:** talk to the marketing lead. Her situation has a deadline, because Sift's expectations grow the longer nobody answers them.
2. **Next 7 days:** hold a follow-up with each of the two unhappy leads.
3. **Within 2 weeks:** write a one-page independence policy for the whole committee.
4. **After that:** make one move toward Sift, through your sponsorship lead.

## 1. Marketing lead and Sift

**Diagnosis.** She has probably done nothing wrong. Sift's expectations are Sift's, and the fix is structural. Making it personal would cost you her trust.

1. **Ask before you assume.** Start with: "What exactly has Sift said to you? Is it a direct ask, or a hint?" "Implying" could mean anything from one joke to a real request. Find out which.
2. **Give her cover.** Tell her: "You never have to carry Sift's position into program discussions. I'm making that a rule for everyone, so you can say 'the chair doesn't allow it' and the pressure lands on me, not you."
3. **Write the rule for everyone, not for her.** One page, sent to the whole committee:
   - Keynotes and program slots are chosen by the program committee against published criteria. They are never part of a sponsorship deal.
   - Everyone discloses their employer ties once, at the start of the edition.
   - Anyone with an employer tie to a topic or speaker recuses from that decision.
   - Sponsors buy visibility (booth, logo, labeled sponsored session). They do not buy program influence.
   - Committee members speak for GoNorth in meetings, not for their employers.
4. **Narrow her role if needed.** She keeps marketing. She stays out of program and keynote discussions, at least for anything touching Sift. That costs her little, since marketing is her job.
5. **Give Sift a legitimate way to be recognised.** They are donating paid staff hours, and that has real value. Offer a "supporting organization" credit, or a normal sponsorship tier if they want one. Route it through your sponsorship lead, not through her.
6. **Give Sift a fair route to a talk.** Their speakers can submit to the CFP like anyone else, and are reviewed blind if you can manage it. Keep the keynote off the table. If you ever grant it, a quiet deal is the thing that damages trust with attendees and speakers.
7. **Write to her manager or your Sift contact, warmly.** Something like: "Thank you for supporting [Name]'s time on GoNorth. So that she is never put in a awkward position, here is how our program decisions work, and here is how Sift can be recognised." Do this only after step 1 tells you where the pressure really comes from.

**Don't** quietly sideline her, reveal what she told you to Sift, or make a one-off exception for the keynote.

## 2. Sponsorship lead and A/V lead

**Diagnosis.** "Not enjoying it" is a symptom. The cause is usually one of: too much load, a lonely role, no recognition, the work no longer matches what they signed up for, or something outside GoNorth. Guessing the cause is the mistake to avoid.

**The follow-up conversation (30 to 45 minutes, one on one):**

1. Thank them for answering honestly. Say that you asked because you want to fix it, and that nothing negative follows from the answer.
2. Ask open questions and listen:
   - "What part of the role drains you most? What part do you still like?"
   - "If you could drop one thing, what would it be?"
   - "What would make the next edition feel worth it?"
   - "What would you need to stay for the seventh edition? And if you couldn't, what would a good handover look like?"
3. Offer concrete changes only after you have heard the cause.
4. Agree on one or two changes and a check-in date, 4 to 6 weeks out.

**Likely fixes by role:**

| Role | Common cause | Options |
|---|---|---|
| Sponsorship | Unpaid selling is thankless and rejection-heavy | Add a deputy or a second closer. Cut the target list. Take over the 3 hardest accounts yourself. Reuse last year's packages and contracts. Recognise their share of revenue raised openly. |
| A/V | Show-day stress, equipment hauling, no backup | Hire a professional production vendor for the heavy parts. Make them producer, not operator. Recruit and train a second person. Cap their on-site hours. |

For both:
- **Cut scope before you cut people.** Ask what a lighter version of the role looks like.
- **Make them less irreplaceable.** Document their processes and name a deputy. This lowers their pressure, and it protects you if they leave anyway.
- **Recognise them in public.** Give them a stage mention, or a credit on the site.
- **Offer a way to step back with dignity.** "Lead this edition lightly, hand over the next" often keeps someone who would otherwise quit. Don't pressure them into it.

## 3. About you

Two of five leads saying no is a signal worth reading, not only a retention problem. Check whether you are the bottleneck, whether too many decisions route through you, and whether the committee gets enough thanks. Ask in your follow-ups: "What should I do differently as chair?"

## 4. Realistic outcome

You may not keep all three at full strength. Aim to keep each person involved in a way they can sustain, and to make sure no single departure can sink the event. The Sift issue is the most solvable, because a clear policy removes the tension.

## Two questions that would change this advice

1. Is Sift already a paying sponsor, and does the marketing lead have any say over sponsorship terms?
2. How long until the event? Under 4 months means lighter scope changes. Over 6 months leaves room for a real handover.
