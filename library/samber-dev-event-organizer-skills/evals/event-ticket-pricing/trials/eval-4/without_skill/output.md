## Short answer

Don't apply an industry-standard policy to this person, because you never published one. Refund them, and publish a policy for future sales.

## 1. Industry standard

There's no law-like standard, but community and conference events usually follow this pattern.

| Time before event | Typical refund |
|---|---|
| 30+ days | 100%, sometimes minus processing fees |
| 14 to 30 days | 50%, or 100% as credit or transfer |
| Under 14 days (some use 7) | No refund, but transfers to another person are allowed |
| Event cancelled or rescheduled by you | 100%, always |

Two rules show up almost everywhere:
- Ticket **transfers or name changes** are free and allowed until the event.
- Payment processing fees are often non-refundable. Stripe, for example, usually keeps its fee on a refund.

Small meetups and community events tend to use the short version: full refund up to 7 days before, none after.

## 2. This person

⚠️ **Their request sits in the refund window under every common policy.** They wrote on 27 September, which was 21 days before the 18 October event. Under a 30/14/7 policy they'd get 100% or 50%.

Today is 15 October and the event is in 3 days. If nobody has replied in the 18 days since, the delay is the bigger problem.

Reasons to refund and not apply a policy retroactively:
- **A policy buyers never saw isn't enforceable.** Terms must be shown before purchase. You can't bind 190 existing buyers to a rule they never saw.
- 🔴 **Legal exposure, if you sell in France or the EU.** The 14-day right of withdrawal normally doesn't apply to dated leisure events. But you must tell buyers that before they pay, and you didn't. Without that notice, a consumer can argue the withdrawal right still applies, and the period can run up to 12 months. I'm not a lawyer and I don't know your jurisdiction or whether your buyers are consumers or companies. Treat this as a reason not to fight the refund, not as legal advice.
- **Refusing costs more than refunding.** A chargeback typically costs the ticket price plus a dispute fee, plus the reputation hit in a community you run yourself.
- **Refunding costs one seat out of 190.** You can reallocate the seat, for example to a hardship waitlist.

What to do:
1. **Reply today** with an apology for the delay.
2. **Refund in full.** Absorb the processing fee. It's small, and it avoids an argument.
3. **Offer a transfer first only if they'd welcome it.** For example: "You can also pass the ticket to a colleague." Don't make the refund conditional on it.
4. **Allow 5 to 10 days for the refund to reach them.** Say so in your reply.

Draft reply:

> Hi [name], sorry for the slow reply to your 27 September email. That's on us. We're refunding your ticket in full today. It should reach your account within [5-10] business days. If you'd rather give the ticket to someone else, tell me and I'll transfer it. Thank you for registering, and I hope to see you at a future edition.

## 3. The 11 hardship tickets

- **Check whether this person holds one of the 11.** If the ticket was free or discounted, there may be little or nothing to refund. Cancel it and free the seat. Don't treat hardship ticketholders worse than paying ones.
- **Don't build the policy around the 11.** They're a separate allocation, so give them the same cancel-and-release rule: if someone can't come, they say so, and the ticket goes to the next person in need.

## 4. Publish a policy now

Add it to the registration page and the confirmation email template:

> **Refunds.** Full refund until 30 days before the event, 50% from 14 to 30 days, none afterwards. Tickets can be transferred to another person at any time, free of charge. If we cancel or reschedule the event, you get a full refund.

- This applies to purchases made after you publish it.
- **Don't mass-email the 190 existing buyers.** It invites 190 refund requests and still doesn't bind them. Handle existing requests case by case, as above.
- Add a line telling buyers there's no right of withdrawal for dated events, if you sell in the EU. Have someone check the exact wording.
