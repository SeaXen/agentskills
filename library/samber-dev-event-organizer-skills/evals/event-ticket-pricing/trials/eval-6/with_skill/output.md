## Recommendation

Run the 10 EUR refundable deposit as a one-edition experiment. Don't start charging 15 EUR flat. The evidence behind the deposit is thin, and I'll say how thin below.

## What the data says

- **Your baseline:** 112 of 180 turned up. That is a 38% no-show rate (68 seats), and it applies to a seat nobody paid for.
- **Deposit evidence:** There is one data point, and it isn't from developer events. Eventtia's 2024 managed-conference data shows a $3 refundable deposit cutting a previously free event's no-show rate from 38% to 14%. Your baseline matches that 38% exactly, which makes the example tempting. Don't lean on it. It is general-conference evidence, and the skill treats deposits as untested for developer events.
- **Deposit size:** Nothing I have says a 10 EUR deposit works better or worse than 3. I have no dose-response data, so I can't tell you 10 is the right amount.
- **Flat fee evidence:** I found no sourced data on flat fees reducing hackathon no-shows. The skill's only sourced fee rule (devopsdays) is to charge 10-20% of a typical commercial conference ticket. It describes that fee as risk-spreading rather than funding.
- **Your 15 EUR figure:** It is not derived from anything in the sources. You would be inventing it.

## Why the deposit beats the flat fee

1. **Your goal is attendance, not revenue.** Sponsors cover 100% of costs, so you have no ticket-revenue line. The skill says that for a free hackathon you delete the priced postures rather than rank them. A flat fee puts you back into a priced posture you don't need.
2. **A flat fee costs more than it looks.** The skill says any non-zero price pulls in accounting and tax handling. That is a sourced claim about charging. A deposit also handles money, but because you refund it at check-in, you never book it as revenue. That second part is my reasoning, not a sourced rule. Check it with whoever does your accounting.
3. **A flat fee is irreversible once public.** A published price can't be withdrawn mid-sale without refunding the people who already paid. A deposit is a policy you can drop next edition.
4. **A deposit is tied to showing up.** A flat fee is a sunk cost whether people come or not, and it also excludes people who would have shown. The no-show rate you are trying to fix is the quantity a deposit is built around.

## Caveats

- **Day-one only.** Returning the deposit at check-in fixes day-one no-shows. It doesn't stop people leaving partway through the 48 hours. If your lunches are spread across both days, you may still see waste. That is my inference, not sourced.
- **Exclusion risk.** A 10 EUR hold is a bigger barrier for students and anyone short of cash. Decide upfront how to waive it, for example by email request, with a named person reviewing. That is a judgment call, not sourced.
- **A cheaper lever exists.** You can order catering against expected attendance instead of registrations. At 112 of 180, that is about 62%, and you could add a margin. The no-show skill owns this lever (`event-no-show-management`). I couldn't open that skill, so I'm pointing at it without having read it.

## Before you open registration

The skill says to write the refund policy before the first ticket sells. Fix these points in writing:

1. **Deadline:** One calendar date after which the deposit rules stop. All four sourced events use a single dated cutoff, not a sliding scale.
2. **Refund before the deadline:** Say whether someone who cancels early gets the full deposit back. Say whether you keep an admin fee. The sourced events differ on this.
3. **Check-in refund:** How and when it happens. Say what happens to people who arrive without registering.
4. **Forfeited deposits:** What you do with deposits from people who never show. Fund lunches, donate them, or something else. This is my addition. State the choice publicly, because keeping the money quietly reads badly.
5. **Transfers:** Allowed or not. If not, write that they are not offered.
6. **Hardship waiver:** Who gets the deposit waived, and who decides.
7. **Publish the policy** on the registration page, beside the price.

## How to tell whether it worked

Self-set measures, written down before registration opens:

- **No-show rate against the 38% baseline.** The Eventtia result of 14% is a possible reference, not a target.
- **Registrations against last edition's 180.** A deposit may cut sign-ups. Lower registrations with the same turnout is a fine outcome, but you should know about it.
- **Deposit waiver requests.** Count how many you received and granted.

If the no-show rate doesn't move, drop the deposit next year.
