# Verdict: ❌ Not as proposed. ✅ Yes to a revised structure (below).

## 1. $1,200 flat price: don't sign off

- ⚠️ **The benchmark is wrong.** Large cloud-native conferences charge that much because they have thousands of attendees, many tracks, and an established brand. Your first user conference has none of that.
- ⚠️ **One price for two audiences.**
  - Customers (about 300): the employer pays, so they barely notice the price.
  - Practitioners (about 300): many pay out of pocket or need manager approval, and they are the people you're trying to win. $1,200 filters out your target.
- ⚠️ **Risk:** the room fills with customers and the conversion half stays empty. The conference then turns into a pure retention event while you report it as acquisition.
- ℹ️ Marketing owns the budget line. First decide whether tickets must **cover cost** or the event is a **pipeline and retention investment**. At 600 seats, $1,200 tops out at $720k only if every seat sells, and that won't happen.

## 2. Supporter at $2,200: sign off with changes

The idea is sound. Corporate budgets can absorb it, and it funds the room. Fix these:

- 🔴 **Cap it at 20–30.** A dinner with the CTO only works for a small group. Scarcity is the value.
- 🔴 **Sell it to customers, not practitioners.** Make it an add-on to a base pass, or invite-only for strategic accounts. A practitioner paying $2,200 to meet your CTO is unlikely, and a dinner of strangers wastes the CTO's time.
- 🟠 **Give the dinner a purpose.** Run it as a product-roadmap roundtable with feedback sessions, not just access. That gives buyers something to justify to their finance team.
- 🟠 **Check the jacket.**
  - Landed cost, including sizing and shipping.
  - Collect sizes at purchase and ship after the event.
  - Check customer gift-policy limits. Some employers cap gift value.
- 🟢 Front-row reserved seating costs you almost nothing. Keep it.
