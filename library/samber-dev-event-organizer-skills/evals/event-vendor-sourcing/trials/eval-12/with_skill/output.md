# Public liability cover for DataForge: which policy, and who sells it

**Short answer:** Buy an event public liability policy (called general liability in the US) for the single day. Get the exact wording from your venue contract first, because that wording decides the policy. The cousin can be the seller, but only if he passes the checks in step 3. Get at least one other quote as well.

I've followed the event-vendor-sourcing skill. It says the *amount* and *type* of cover is a risk decision, and that I must not invent prices or assert local legal rules. So some items below are things you collect, not things I can tell you.

## What I don't know yet

- ⚠️ **Country or state.** Insurance licensing, compulsory cover and policy names all change by jurisdiction. Tell me where the event is and I'll tighten this.
- ⚠️ **Exact contract wording.** "Public liability cover" is only a label. The contract will say the real requirements.
- ℹ️ **Price.** I won't quote one. No public benchmark exists for a 500-person tech event. One US insurer advertises a floor of "as low as $75" per event, but that floor moves with coverage and state. Don't budget against it or quote it to a broker. Your own quotes are the only real number.

## Step 1: pull three things from the DataForge contract (this week, ~30 min)

Copy these verbatim into one document:

1. **Minimum limit.** The required amount per occurrence and in aggregate.
2. **Additional insured wording.** Is the venue named as an additional insured? Under what exact name?
3. **Certificate deadline.** Is the certificate of insurance due before load-in, or before the deposit? Is a policy "in place" enough, or do they want the certificate in hand?

Also ask the venue in writing:

- Do they need anything beyond public liability? Examples are property damage to their building, liquor or host liability, and cover for your suppliers.
- Do they have a preferred or approved insurer or broker?
- Do they require insurers with a minimum rating?

A broker who gets the contract clause starts from a fact. One who gets "we need event insurance" will decide the cover for you.

## Step 2: find sellers

Use two or three sources:

- **The venue.** They've seen certificates before and often know who produces them quickly.
- **Peer organizers in your city.** Ask who insured their last event and whether the certificate arrived on time.
- **Event-liability specialists.** Examples named in the skill (a shortlist to request quotes from, not endorsements): K&K Insurance, Direct Event Insurance (US), Hiscox (UK). These are US/UK names, so they may not suit your country. You can buy direct from an insurer or through a broker.
- **The cousin.**

## Step 3: vet the cousin like any other broker

Referral is a reasonable default. It tells you something a quote can't: whether he turns up. But a family tie gives you no extra protection if the paperwork is wrong. Check each item:

| Check | How |
|---|---|
| Licensed where the event happens | Look him up on the regulator's public register. Don't rely on his word. |
| Has placed **event** cover before | Ask for events of similar size and scope. Home and auto lines don't count. |
| Places with well-rated insurers | Ask which insurer, then check its financial-strength rating (AM Best or your country's equivalent) and complaint record. |
| Has his own errors-and-omissions cover | Ask. It protects you if he gets the placement wrong. |
| Can meet the contract wording | Send him the Step 1 clauses and see whether he understands them. |

He passes if all five check out. If he's a general-lines agent with no event experience, thank him and use a specialist. A mistake here shows up on load-in day, not at purchase.

Even if he passes, get **a second quote**. A single quote tells you nothing about price or terms. It also gives you a way to refuse politely if the cousin's offer is weak.

## Step 4: send every seller the same brief

Same text to the cousin and to each other seller, so the quotes can be compared:

> **Event:** DataForge, [date], [exact venue address]
> **Attendance:** ~500 (firm number locks [date]; likely to move [up/down])
> **Format:** [conference / hackathon / etc.], [indoor, hours], food served: [yes/no], alcohol: [yes/no]
> **Contract requirements (verbatim):** [paste the clauses]
> **Additional insured:** [exact venue legal name]
> **Certificate needed by:** [date from contract]
> **Please quote separately:** premium, taxes and fees, broker fee, excess/deductible, exclusions, and any cover the venue asks for beyond public liability.
> **Please state:** insurer name and rating, cancellation terms, whether the event can move date, and how a claim is made.
> **Answer by:** [date]. **We decide on:** [date].

## Step 5: compare on total cost and terms, not headline premium

Put each quote on one row. A cheap premium with a large excess, narrow exclusions, or a limit below the contract minimum is not cheaper. Read these before paying:

- Does the limit match the contract?
- Is the venue named as additional insured with the exact wording?
- What are the exclusions (alcohol, crowd size, suppliers, outdoor areas)?
- What happens if you cancel or move the date?
- Is there a cancellation or postponement cover option? Treat it as a separate decision, not a bundled extra.
- Who issues the certificate, and how fast?

Don't pay until the draft certificate matches the venue's wording. Send it to the venue for approval before the policy is final.

## Step 6: timeline for 11 weeks

Work backward from the date the venue wants the certificate. Fill in the real dates once Step 1 is done.

| When | Action |
|---|---|
| Week 1 | Extract contract clauses; email venue questions; ask peers; send brief to cousin plus 1–2 others |
| Week 2 | Vet the cousin; chase quotes |
| Week 3 | Compare; ask the venue to approve the draft wording; choose |
| Weeks 4–5 | Bind the policy; receive the certificate; send it to the venue and confirm they accept it |
| By week 6 | Cover confirmed in writing, **ahead of any date the venue sets** |

Binding early leaves slack. Some cover must be bought well ahead of an event, and some can't be bought once a known problem exists, such as a cancellation risk. If the contract sets an earlier certificate date, it overrides this table.

## Related items for a 500-person event

- **Suppliers.** Your caterer, AV crew and security may be asked for their own certificates. Scale that ask to the supplier's size; a two-person business doesn't need an enterprise-grade policy.
- **Sponsors and exhibitors.** If you take any, align what you ask of them with what the venue asks of you.
- **Whether you need more than public liability** (for example property damage, cancellation, or accident cover for attendees) is a risk-register decision. Take it to whoever owns your risk planning, or ask the broker to explain the options.
