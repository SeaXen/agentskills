# StackSummit lunch: you are missing something

**Short answer:** the three headline prices can't be compared yet. Northgate may still win, but you can't know that from these quotes. Before you decide, do these three things:

1. Get the venue's catering answer in writing.
2. Send all three the same brief and get all-in totals.
3. Read the failure terms, meaning the late-delivery remedy, headcount lock and cancellation.

## Why the headline price misleads

- **Unit prices may cover different things.** A per-head price can leave out delivery, serving staff, a service charge (sometimes on the whole invoice), equipment rental, teardown, overtime and tax.
- **A one-page quote isn't cheaper, just less specific.** Every cost it doesn't state is a question you haven't asked yet. Northgate's low price could be genuinely low, or it could just exclude things.
- **A six-page quote isn't a better quote.** The extra pages are probably where the terms live, so read them for those terms and not for polish.
- **"All inclusive" is a claim, not a total.** Ask Fenwick to list what it includes line by line: staff, hours, equipment, service charge, teardown and tax.
- **Normalising often reorders the ranking.** The supplier with the lowest headline price can end up with the highest total.

## Your headcount range is a pricing risk

You expect 210 to 260, and registration is still open. Each supplier's terms handle that range differently:

- **Headcount lock date.** This can be anywhere from 5 to 14 days before the event, and it decides when you must commit to a number.
- **Downward moves.** Some caterers don't credit them at all.
- **Minimum order.** At 210 it may bite.
- **Upward moves.** Check whether they can supply 260 and at what price.

The cheapest supplier at 240 may not be the cheapest at 210 or 260. Price each quote at 210, 240 and 260, and ask where each supplier's flexibility ends.

Also say where the 240 comes from: registrations, or actuals from a previous edition. Registrations are not attendance, so the lock date matters even more.

## Step 1: the venue's answer, in writing

"Happy for us to bring anyone in" is a verbal answer. Before you spend more time comparing, get written answers on:

- Whether outside caterers are permitted, and whether they charge a fee.
- Whether open flame or chafing dishes are allowed. If not, a family-style, hot-held service plan may not work.
- Kitchen or prep access, the loading route, and the load-in window.
- Whether outside deliveries can be received, and where.
- Who may serve. Some venues require their own staff, and you'd need to know that before planning a volunteer rota.

## Step 2: one brief, all three suppliers

Send the same brief to all three, with each cost line priced separately. The brief should contain:

1. Event name, date (8 June), exact address and the venue's access hours.
2. Headcount: 240 planned, a 210 to 260 range, the date you can lock it, and which direction it is likely to move.
3. Service style (plated, boxed or family-style) and the serving time. Ask them to work back to a delivery time.
4. Dietary counts as numbers, covering organizers, speakers and volunteers as well as attendees: vegetarian, vegan, gluten-free, allergies, halal and kosher. The number of dietary accommodations the event commits to is a separate decision for whoever owns accessibility and inclusion.
5. Who serves, who sets up, and who collects what.
6. Separate lines for delivery, staff, service charge, equipment, setup and teardown, overtime, and tax.
7. One named contact on your side, with a backup phone number.
8. A deadline for answers and the date you will commit.

## What to read in Fenwick's six pages

- **Late or short delivery.** What exactly do they owe you? For a single lunch, this is the failure that lands on the day. Most small-event contracts say nothing here, and the missing remedy is the finding.
- **Substitution rights.** Can they swap dishes without asking?
- **Cancellation and force majeure.** Compare the schedule and what happens to your deposit.
- **The headcount lock and downward credit.** This matters most because of your range.
- **Liability and indemnity.** Mutual is a legitimate ask if the draft is one-sided.
- **Access, timing and venue rules.** The supplier needs to know the venue's restrictions, or they will breach them on your account.

## Northgate's single page

Don't treat the short quote as a red flag, and don't assume it's fine. Email these questions and ask for answers in writing. Fold the missing terms into your grid:

- Is delivery included?
- How many staff, and for how long?
- Is there a service charge?
- Is equipment included, and is it returned or collected?
- Who sets up and tears down?
- Is tax included?
- What is the headcount lock date, and how are downward moves treated?
- What do you owe if lunch is an hour late?
- Can you name an event you served recently that I can call?

## Insurance, scaled to your event

Ask each caterer for evidence of their own liability cover, and ask for it in proportion. A two-person catering firm may not hold an enterprise-shaped certificate, and demanding one can cost you the supplier. Check what the venue's contract requires of you first, and keep the ask consistent with what you require from sponsors. What the event itself needs to insure is a separate decision for whoever owns your risk register.

## Volunteers

You're community-run, so volunteers can help. They can lay out delivered food, keep a drinks station, and run a restricted-diet table. They can't replace a caterer where hot food is prepared on site, because food-safety obligations don't transfer to volunteers. Settle who serves before you plan the rota.

## What this gives you for negotiation

With three like-for-like all-in quotes in hand, you have a real alternative. Name a target number and ask the supplier what trade-offs reach it, rather than asking for a blanket discount. For example: "Is there a change to the menu that would bring this to [target]?" Ask for non-monetary concessions too, such as a later headcount lock, waived delivery, or teardown included. Don't use this approach on a supplier the venue mandates, because you'd have no alternative to lean on.

## Timeline

Today is 15 Oct 2026 and the event is 8 June 2027, so you have plenty of time. The binding dates are not the order date:

- Typical catering orders are placed 2 to 4 weeks before the event. A venue-mandated caterer usually needs more notice.
- The headcount lock can fall about 5 to 14 days before.
- Plan delivery 30 to 60 minutes before serving for plated or boxed meals, and 60 to 90 minutes before for family-style.
- Give every supplier an exact delivery location, a named contact and a backup number.
