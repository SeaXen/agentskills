# Hackathon vendor check: volunteer cooking and volunteer overnight doors

**Short answer:** neither idea survives as stated. Both can be partly kept.

- **Volunteer-cooked hot dinner:** drop it.
- **Four volunteers as overnight security:** drop it as a replacement for a licensed firm. Keep volunteers as front-of-house.

This follows the `event-vendor-sourcing` skill, which deletes volunteer substitution for any category carrying a food-safety, licensing or crowd-safety obligation. It does so because goodwill doesn't transfer a legal obligation, and a volunteer can't be indemnified the way a supplier can.

## Step 0: get the venue's answers in writing

Do this before anything else. Everything below depends on it. Ask the venue, in writing, for each category:

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Does the venue supply catering, mandate its own caterer, permit an outside one, or charge a fee for one? | Universities especially mandate their own. Booking a caterer the venue forbids is the costliest ordering mistake. |
| May we cook on site in the kitchen? Who may use it? | "Venue's kitchen" is not the same as "our right to cook in it". |
| Does the venue require food-handling certification or an inspected operator for hot food? | This varies by country and city. I won't assert one. Ask the venue and your local authority. |
| Are chafing dishes allowed? Is there an overnight fire-code or occupancy limit? | Both are invisible until asked. |
| Does the venue provide security, mandate a firm, or permit an outside one? | If it mandates one, the category closes. |
| What does the building require overnight: guard posts, hours, a separate paid fire marshal? | The venue sets the coverage level. You don't. |
| Does the venue's insurance cover volunteer-run food or door staffing? | Route the answer to `event-risk-management`. |

## Idea 1: volunteers cook Saturday dinner

**Verdict:** removed from the options, not just ranked low. Hot food prepared on site for 220 people carries a food-safety obligation.

What goes wrong with it:
- **No recourse.** There is no contract and no supplier insurance behind it. If 40 people get ill at hour 26, the exposure lands on the organizing entity. It can't be undone afterwards.
- **Venue permission is unknown.** The kitchen may not be yours to use.
- **Dietary counts.** You need vegetarian, vegan, gluten-free, allergy, kosher and halal counts for everyone fed, including mentors, volunteers and organizers. One good cook doing this for 220 is a large allergen-control job.
- **Capacity.** Your cook is also a person you need awake for 36 hours.

**What to do instead:**
1. **Referred single-source first, multi-quote for dinner.** Food is a hackathon's biggest budget line, so this is where the skill says to promote to three quotes on one identical written brief. Compare all-in totals only: delivery, serving staff, service charge, equipment rental, teardown, overtime and tax. Headline per-plate prices mislead. Read the failure terms too: late-delivery remedy, headcount lock date, cancellation schedule and substitutions.
2. **Split the meal grid by slot.** Different suppliers for different meals is a legitimate way to cut cost. Use a cheap, simple delivered dinner. Use volunteers where substitution is allowed: snacks, drinks, laying out delivered food and running the serving line.
3. **Use your cook for something that is allowed.** They can write the menu brief, taste-test quotes, check suppliers' kitchens or references, or run the serving line and allergen-table check-off. That uses the skill without taking on the liability.
4. **Check who may serve.** Some caterers require their own staff. Settle this before planning a volunteer rota.
5. **Serving plan:** several stations, not one table. Release tables a few at a time. Keep restricted-diet meals on a separate table, with names checked off a printed list. Post the menu with allergen detail.

**Timing:**
- Place the order 2–4 weeks out.
- A venue-mandated caterer needs it sooner.
- Plated meals: delivery 30–60 minutes before serving.
- Family-style: delivery 60–90 minutes before serving.
- Give the supplier an exact delivery location, a named contact and a backup phone number.
- Agree the pickup time for reusable equipment when you sign.

**Price:** I'm not giving a per-plate figure, because no public benchmark applies to your city, size and year. The only real number is three quotes on one brief. The published per-person figures are US student-hackathon data and shouldn't be used as a budget target.

## Idea 2: four volunteers on the doors overnight instead of a security firm

**Verdict:** a licensed firm is not substitutable by volunteers. The skill says plainly that volunteers can staff a door, direct people and watch a room, but they can't carry the obligation a licensed guard carries, and no briefing transfers it.

Security is also the thinnest category in this skill. Whether a licence is required, and at what coverage level, turns on local knowledge. Weight the venue's and the firm's own answers accordingly.

**What to do:**
1. **Ask the venue first.** It may include security, mandate a firm, or require specific posts overnight. That settles most of the question.
2. **Ask the venue and peer organizers in your city for firms that have worked that building.** Referred single-source is close to the only responsible path here.
3. **Ask the firm what licences and insurance it and its guards hold.** Also ask whether the guards are its own employees or subcontracted, because that can change whether a licence is required.
4. **Require a written post order before the event:** each post, the guards per post, and their hours. A firm without one has priced a headcount, not a deployment.
5. **Check guard qualifications:** trained for crowd management, de-escalation and emergency direction, and identifiable by uniform or credential. Ask about their incident-report practice.
6. **Use the budget you save elsewhere.** You may be able to hire fewer guard-hours rather than none. Let the venue and firm set the minimum. Don't set it yourself.

**Where your 30 volunteers fit:**
- Greeters, wristband and badge check, directing people, and watching quiet rooms.
- Serving line, drinks, snacks, and runners.
- Check-in and a welcome desk.
- They are not a replacement for the licensed security post.

Route the staffing itself to `event-volunteers`. Key points for a 36-hour event:
- Plan shifts so nobody is working while exhausted.
- Fatigue is itself a risk overnight.
- 30 keen volunteers across 36 hours is a thinner rota than it sounds.

## Two items for other skills

- **Insurance:** whether volunteers are covered, and what the event buys, belongs to `event-risk-management`. Ask it, and ask the venue what it requires. I'm not deciding that here.
- **Overnight stay:** the fire-code or occupancy limit for sleeping or staying overnight is a venue constraint. Get it in writing alongside the security answer.
