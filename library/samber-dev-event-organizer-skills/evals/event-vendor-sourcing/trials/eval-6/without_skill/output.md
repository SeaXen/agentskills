**Short answer:** both ideas can work. Each has one hidden blocker you should check this week, before you commit.

I'm assuming France, since you're in Nantes. If the event is elsewhere, the principles hold but the legal details change.

## Do first (about 1 hour, 3 emails)

1. Ask the venue for the kitchen rules in writing. Can outsiders cook? Is a licensed caterer required? What does their insurance cover?
2. Ask your insurer whether your association's liability policy covers serving meals to 220 people and volunteer-run overnight security.
3. Ask the venue whether the fire-safety rules require a trained fire-safety person (SSIAP) on site overnight. Also ask whether sleeping at the venue is allowed.

⚠️ If the venue says "licensed caterer only" or "SSIAP required", the savings plan changes. It's better to learn that now than on the day.

## Saturday dinner

The real risk is food poisoning at 220 people. One good cook is not the same as a safe kitchen for 220.

**Watch for:**
- **Capacity.** 220 hot portions needs several cooks, large equipment and a timeline. Check the venue's ovens, fridges and dishwashing. A good home cook can underestimate this by a factor of 5.
- **Legal status.** Serving meals to the public, even free or at cost, is still food service. In France, associations usually need to declare to the local food-safety authority (DDPP) and apply basic hygiene rules. Check this with them. Don't assume the "volunteer" label exempts you.
- **Allergens.** You must be able to tell any guest exactly what's in each dish. Label the 14 major allergens. Plan for vegetarian, vegan and halal or no-pork guests, and collect dietary needs at registration.
- **Temperature control.** Hot food stays above 63°C and cold food below 4°C. Don't cool and reheat large batches.
- **Hygiene.** Volunteers who are sick or have had stomach trouble in the last 48 hours stay out of the kitchen. Provide handwashing and gloves.
- **Traceability.** Keep a sample of each dish for several days, and keep your receipts.
- **Insurance.** See the check above.

**Make it safer and still cheap:**
- Use a low-risk menu. Pasta or a curry served straight from the pot is safer than rice salads, raw egg, creamy sauces or large batches that sit for hours.
- Split the work. Volunteers cook, but a cheap supplier delivers the high-risk parts such as meat, or you buy a fully catered main and cook only the sides.
- Name one person responsible for food safety on the day, who isn't also cooking.
- Get a quote from a caterer or food truck as a fallback. For 220 people, some quotes are closer than you'd expect.

## Overnight doors

The risks are legal, physical and about fatigue.

**Watch for:**
- **Legal scope.** Unpaid volunteers from your own association are generally not "private security" under French law. But they have no powers. They can't search bags or frisk anyone without consent, and they can't use force beyond what any citizen can. Brief them clearly. Ask the venue and your insurer to confirm.
- **Role.** Volunteers watch, greet and call for help. They never confront drunk, aggressive or unwelcome people. The escalation path is: ask, then call 112, then wait.
- **Safety in numbers.** Never leave one person alone on a door. Use pairs, give each pair a phone or radio, and agree a code word for "I need help".
- **Fatigue.** 36 hours is long. Keep shifts to 2–3 hours and rotate. Check that volunteers aren't also working the day, and give them a real rest area and food. Tired volunteers make bad calls at 3 a.m.
- **Fire exits.** They must never be locked or blocked, even to control access. Controlling entry means badges, wristbands and a guest list, not locks.
- **Fire safety.** See the venue question above.
- **Alcohol.** Decide your policy now. If you serve any, security gets harder.
- **Medical.** Have a first-aid kit, at least 2 first-aiders (PSC1) on the team, and the local emergency address written on a card.
- **Code of conduct.** Publish one, with a named person to report to. That person should be reachable overnight and should not be one of the door volunteers.

**Cheaper than a full security firm:**
- Ask the venue whether it has its own night staff, since that may already be in the contract.
- Hire a firm only for the highest-risk window, such as 11 p.m. to 6 a.m., instead of the whole event.

## Using the 30 volunteers well

- Write a shift grid with a named lead per area: kitchen, doors, first aid, floor.
- Cap every volunteer at about 8 hours of work, with proper breaks.
- Feed the volunteers too. People forget this, and it affects the kitchen workload.
- Hold a 20-minute briefing before the event. Cover the escalation rules, the emergency contacts and who has authority.
- Keep one organizer unassigned each shift. Someone needs to be free to handle surprises.

## Decision

Don't cut either line item yet. First get the three answers from the "Do first" list. Then:

- If the venue and your insurer both say yes, do both ideas with the safeguards above.
- If either says no, keep the volunteer cooks but buy the high-risk food, and hire security for the overnight window only.
