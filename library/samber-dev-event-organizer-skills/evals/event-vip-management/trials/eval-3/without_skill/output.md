## Why

- **The shout-out is a mention.** She asked for no mention in any post, and a closing announcement is a mention. It would also introduce her to 400 people at once, which is the opposite of "don't introduce me to anyone."
- **The reserved seat doesn't work either.** It's invisible only if nobody asks why one seat is empty. A volunteer will ask, and then someone will find out who she is. It also gives her special treatment, which contradicts the no-hierarchy rule you're loud about.
- **Honoring the request is the nice thing.** For someone who is well known and wants to be an ordinary attendee, the gift is being treated as one. Your no-hierarchy principle covers a maintainer who wants to be left alone as much as it covers a speaker.
- **Doing something "nice" anyway overrides her stated wish.** However kind the intent, it puts your co-organisers' idea of a good gesture ahead of her explicit request.

## What to do instead

1. **Reply to her once.** Thank her, confirm you'll respect all three requests, and stop there. Don't offer a menu of perks, because that makes her do the work of declining them. One line is enough: "If you ever want anything, just say so."
2. **Keep it need-to-know.** Tell the people who handle badges, photos, and social posts, and nobody else. Say "do not approach, introduce, tag, or photograph this attendee." Tell the photographers her name and what she looks like.
3. **Check the other places her name could appear.** That includes the public attendee list, badge name, session tags, and any "who's here" posts. Take her off anything public.
4. **Treat the photo request as best effort.** In a crowd of 400 you can't guarantee she never appears in a frame. Brief the photographers, and delete any shot she's in on request. Tell her that rather than promise perfection.
5. **Skip the follow-up.** Don't mention her afterwards in a recap or thank-you post, and don't mention her to attendees who ask whether she came.
