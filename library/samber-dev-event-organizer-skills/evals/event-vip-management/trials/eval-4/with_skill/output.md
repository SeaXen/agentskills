# Kestrel Summit guest brief: the nine "executive guests"

Pole assumed: vendor/corporate (marketing-funded). Under the skill, naming guests from the mainstage is only allowed when a guest's office has asked for it in as many words. Nobody has been asked yet, so the plan is to ask first and then build what the answers support.

⚠️ The skill's menus and orderings are its own construction, not benchmarked against event data. I treated them as defaults.

## 0. Bottom line

1. Don't commit to "all nine named from stage plus a CEO photo" yet. Nobody has stated a wish or an obligation, so right now no one qualifies.
2. Ask each guest's own office what they want. Offer opt-in choices and make "nothing special" an easy answer.
3. Build only what the replies support. Expect a mixed result: some named from stage, some seated near the front without an announcement, some doing a private photo, some doing nothing.
4. Treat the analysts differently from the start (see section 2).

## 2. Qualification: obligation, not title

Test: can you state, in the guest's own terms and in one sentence, what their presence imposes that the door and help desk can't absorb? Today the answer is "unknown" for all nine.

| Guest | Route / status | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 3 customer CIOs | Attendees unless they speak. | If any is on a customer-story session, that CIO belongs to `event-speaker-experience`. If any is covered by a signed agreement, they belong to `event-sponsor-fulfillment`. Otherwise, unknown obligation. |
| 2 board members | Unknown. Possible dependency exposure. | They are insiders and hosts. They probably need a seat and a contact, not an escort. |
| Lead investor | Unknown. Possible dependency exposure or discretion. | Ask before naming. Investors sometimes prefer not to be publicly tied to the company at a given moment. |
| 3 industry analysts | Route to `event-press-relations` for briefings and accreditation. | This is my judgment, not the skill's text. Analyst firms often restrict public association with a vendor and what hospitality they accept. A stage name-check or a photo with your CEO can compromise their independence. Default to not naming and not photographing them unless their own firm's contact says yes. |

What counts as a qualifying statement:
- External protocol.
- Security.
- Discretion.
- Dependency exposure. This is the softest type and needs the relationship to recur (Q7 says it does).

Prominence alone never qualifies.

## 3. Identification breadth (Menu D) and roster

- Start with the **obligation-only list**.
- Promote to **obligation + relationship** for the investor and board members only if the visit is itself a deliverable to that relationship. Ask the CEO or whoever owns the relationship. "We should be nice to them" doesn't count.
- Don't use the broad notability list. That would be a list of nine "notable" people nobody has assessed.
- The current nine-name list is a **candidate list** until replies arrive. Don't treat it as a roster.

Roster fields, and nothing beyond them:
- Name, as the guest's own office writes it.
- Obligation type (one of the four).
- Stated requirements, quoted, with the date and who stated them.
- What was refused, in writing, with the reason.
- Named organizer.
- Visibility answer, quoted. "Not asked" is a valid entry and a prompt to go ask.

Rules for the roster:
- **Holder:** one named person in field marketing.
- **Deletion date:** set today, in the same pass. Default is the day post-event follow-ups go out.
- **No judgments:** no "VIP", "key" or "important" notes.
- **Circulation by need:** the door lead gets name and arrival window only. Room leads get the name plus the instruction. Nobody else sees the requirements.

## 4. Program posture (Menu A)

Nine names and a yearly recurrence move you from ad hoc to the **defined criteria + protocol runbook** rung. Concretely:
- Documented qualifying triggers (the four above).
- A named owner.
- A repeatable sequence (the ask, then the greeter brief, then the day-of decisions).
- A roster with a deletion date.

The formal named program with dedicated staff is only warranted if a guest arrives with a protective detail and that recurs. Revisit after the replies.

## 5. Visibility posture (Menu B): the core decision

Vendor-pole defaults:

| Rung | Use when |
|---|---|
| Public marker + recognition moment (named from stage) | The guest's office asked for it in as many words. The rung is *not* the default: it costs the most effort, can't be unsaid, and loses the ratio round to the visible marker. |
| Visible marker (reserved seat near the front, ribbon) | A cheap, reversible way to deliver most of the acknowledgement. Print run is open, so it's available. |
| Staff-only signal | Guest says "don't single me out", or hasn't replied. |
| Invisible handling | Same surface as staff-only, with no briefing. Fallback only. |

How to apply this to the nine:
1. **Opt-in only.** Name from stage only guests who said yes in writing, with the wording they want (name, title, company, pronunciation).
2. **Reject the nine-name roll call.** It is dead air in a 1,200-person opening, and any who declined would look conspicuously absent. Use one 30–45 second acknowledgement segment by the CEO or emcee, covering only opted-in guests. Alternative: a categorical line ("our customers, board and partners") with no names.
3. **Analysts are not named from stage** unless their firm's contact confirms in writing that it's acceptable.
4. **Reserved seats** near the front are the low-regret substitute for anyone who wants to be seen but not announced.
5. **Don't publish movements.** Don't list names in a public run of show or social post before the day. A guest's security or investor-disclosure posture may forbid advance publication.
6. **Hand the stage slot to `event-run-of-show`.** The opening segment has to fit without pushing a talk. That call isn't mine.

Check Menu D first if this menu pushes you to mark many guests: a visible tier that nobody chose is usually caused by a list that's too long.

## 6. The ask: sequence and script

Sequence:
1. **Now (mid-October):** the CEO's office or the relationship owner sends each ask. The owner is the person who already has the relationship, not field marketing cold. That means the CEO for the investor and board members, the account executive or customer-success owner for each CIO, and your analyst relations lead for each analyst.
2. **Answers due about 8 weeks before doors** (early January, proposed), and at least 2 weeks before the badge print freeze and the run-of-show lock.
3. **Chase once**, through the same owner. No reply means default to staff-only: no stage name, no photo, no publication.
4. **Close the roster** after the replies and set it against the run of show.

Script (adapt; one message per guest, sent by the relationship owner):

> We're hosting Kestrel Summit in Austin on [dates]. We'd be glad to have you, and we want to match how you'd like to be treated. Whichever you prefer is fine, including none:
> 1. Welcomed by name from the stage at Tuesday's opening.
> 2. A reserved seat near the front, no announcement.
> 3. A private photo with [CEO name] on Tuesday afternoon. We would not publish it without your sign-off.
> 4. Nothing special: attend like any other guest.
>
> Is there anything we should know in advance, for example a contact who travels with you, timing limits, or things you'd rather we not publish? Please reply to [named organizer] by [date].

The default when someone replies "4" or doesn't reply is staff-only handling: no marker, no name from stage, no photo.

## 7. Escort depth (Menu C), greeter brief and liaison

- **Default:** a named greeter for arrival only, meeting the guest at the door, walking them in and handing over a contact card. That costs one person for about 20 minutes per arrival.
- **Fall back to a printed contact card** if nobody can be spared.
- **Escort + liaison** only if a guest's office says they bring a protective detail, a protocol officer or an advance team. If so, run the intake:
  1. Name one counterpart with a number answered from the day before.
  2. Write down their requirements verbatim.
  3. Answer each in writing (yes, no, or substitute) before the day.
  4. Walk the route if they ask.
  5. State that the code of conduct applies to their staff too.
  6. Tell your own team only what changes.
- **Don't escort anyone all day** without a stated need. A person shadowing a guest becomes a visible marker.

Greeter brief (one page, written by the named organizer, never improvised on the morning):
- Who you're meeting, with a photo only if the guest's office supplied one.
- When and where, with a late fallback and a phone number that gets answered.
- The route: door, coffee, the room, toilets, exit. Walk it once before doors.
- What they asked for, quoted, and what was refused, quoted.
- The visibility instruction in one line.
- Your handoff person, and what to do if they're missing.

What not to say:
- Never say "VIP", including on the radio.
- Never introduce the guest to anyone who hasn't been told to expect them.
- Never explain the arrangements to a curious attendee. "I'm just showing someone where the room is" is complete and true.
- Never volunteer a departure time.

## 8. The CEO photo op

- **Opt-in per guest**, as in the ask above.
- **Don't do one group shot of all nine.** A single frame with analysts, customers, board and investor mixes audiences, and any one of them may object to appearing with the others. Run separate short slots (one per opted-in group, such as customers together and the investor with the board).
- **Analysts:** no photo by default. A private photo, with the firm's approval, is the most you'd offer.
- **Publication rule:** a photo is never published, and no guest is quoted or tagged, without that guest's written approval of the specific image. Consent isn't implied by attendance and isn't yours to give.
- **Logistics:** a fixed room away from the main flow, one named person bringing guests to it on schedule, and a photographer given a shot list of who has opted in.

## 9. The two named day-of decisions

Write a name against each before the day. If you leave them blank, whoever is standing there decides under time pressure.

1. **Who decides on the spot when someone requests a photo of a guest:** the named organizer for that guest. Proposed fallback: the field marketing event lead.
2. **Who says no on the guest's behalf:** the same named organizer, using the two lines below. Only the guest or their office can say yes to an exception.
   - To an attendee: "Not right now, sorry, we're on a tight schedule."
   - To a photographer: "No photos of this group, please." Address the frame, not the person.

If a photo has already been taken against a guest's wishes, the ask goes to whoever took it, in private, immediately.

## 11. Measurement (set targets before the event)

- **Obligation met, per guest:** every stated requirement was met or refused in writing in advance. Zero requirements first discovered at the door.
- **Image consent:** zero published images a guest didn't approve.
- **Roster deletion:** the roster is gone by its stated date, confirmed by the named holder.
- **Tier visibility:** at a vendor-pole event, a visible tier isn't a defect. Still ask two nearby attendees what they noticed, and record it for next edition.
