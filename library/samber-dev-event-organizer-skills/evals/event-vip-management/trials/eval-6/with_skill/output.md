# Guest brief: is the gold lanyard the right call?

**No.** At a flat, anti-hierarchy community event, a gold lanyard for 44 people is a visible tier, and nothing on the spreadsheet justifies one. Your ops lead's goal is fine: guests should be looked after and nobody should arrive unrecognised. This method fails it on three counts.

- **It qualifies people on status.** The skill qualifies a guest on an *obligation*, never on a title or prominence. The spreadsheet records "notable" and "nice to have on side", and neither is an obligation.
- **It is the tier-by-logistics failure.** A visible marker chosen because it is the cheapest way to brief an 18-person door team is a known failure mode. The fix is to shorten the list, not to mark it.
- **It contradicts your register.** You are loud about being flat. Attendees will read a gold lanyard as a hierarchy on sight, and someone will post a photo of it.

Pole assumed throughout: **community**. If I have that wrong, tell me, because it flips the visibility section.

## 1. Qualification per group

Nobody on the list qualifies yet. The test is one sentence naming what the guest's presence imposes on you that the door, queue and help desk can't absorb. If you have to invent the sentence for them, they don't qualify.

| Group | Result | Route |
|---|---|---|
| Well-known maintainers | No obligation stated. Attendees with a known name. | General attendee flow. Anyone on the program belongs to `event-speaker-experience`. |
| Four journalists | Here to file copy. | `event-press-relations` owns accreditation and any press badge. |
| Large followings | Status only. | General flow. |
| Two ex-employees of the headline sponsor | Not sponsor personnel and not covered by any agreement. | General flow, with the caution below. |
| About twelve "nice to have on side" | A relationship wish, not an obligation. | Ask which obligation each one carries. Expect the list to collapse. |

**Caution on the two ex-employees.** A gold lanyard visible to the sponsor's own staff could embarrass them, or the sponsor, depending on how they left. Do not tell the sponsor about them. Do not assume either way. Ask them.

A guest qualifies only on one of four obligation types:

- **External protocol:** their institution has a procedure you must receive.
- **Security:** a protective detail or a stated security requirement.
- **Discretion:** they asked not to be identified, photographed or listed.
- **Dependency exposure:** the event depends on the relationship and it recurs across editions.

For the "nice to have" dozen, the only plausible route is dependency exposure. That needs a funder, grant officer or board member whose visit is itself a moment in a recurring relationship. "We should be nice to them" does not count.

**Realistic outcome:** zero to a handful of people in scope. Zero is a correct result, not a failure.

## 2. Identification breadth and roster

- **Breadth:** the obligation-only list. The broad notability list is deleted outright at a flat-register event, because a written roster of "notable" attendees is the tier in document form. The obligation + relationship list needs a recurring relationship and someone whose visit is a deliverable to it.
- **The spreadsheet as it stands is the problem.** Strip the "notable" and "nice to have" judgments. A roster never records a judgment about a person. Keep it only for guests who pass the test.
- **Fields:**
  - name, as the guest's own office writes it
  - obligation type
  - stated requirements, quoted, with date and source
  - what you refused, in writing
  - one named organizer
  - visibility answer, quoted ("not asked" is a valid entry and a prompt to ask)
- **Holder:** your ops lead, or someone you name.
- **Viewers:** by need, never by seniority within the team. The door lead gets name and arrival window only, not requirements and not reasons.
- **Deletion date:** set now, in the same pass. The default is the day post-event follow-ups go out.
- **Today:** the 44-name file with judgments attached is the artifact hardest to justify. Check where it is shared and who has access.

## 3. Program posture

- **Default:** ad hoc invisible accommodation, with one organizer paying attention. This is right for one or two guests a year.
- **Promote to a runbook** if more than a handful qualify and the relationship recurs.
- **No formal named program.** It is deleted for a flat register with no external obligation.
- **If nobody qualifies:** promote "no posture" to first. Anything above it manufactures a tier for nothing.

## 4. Visibility posture

- **Recommendation: staff-only signal.** The public surface is identical to every other attendee: same badge, same door, no reserved row, no announcement.
- **Internally, three groups know before doors, and nobody else:**
  - the door lead: name and arrival window
  - the room leads: name, plus "no announcement, no reserved seat, route questions to the named organizer"
  - code-of-conduct responders: name
- **Brief the 18 volunteers on none of this.** The briefing line is: *"She has asked not to be singled out. If you see her, she is an attendee."*
- **A visible marker is promoted only if both are true:** the guest's office explicitly asked to be identifiable, and the badge print run is still open. Neither is known. Seniority and the size of a cheque never promote it.
- **If the print run is closed, both marker rungs are deleted.** A hand-written addition to a printed badge reads as a tier more clearly than a designed one.
- **Reversibility:** a lanyard colour can be dropped next edition. A published photo of a gold-lanyard cohort cannot be unpublished.

## 5. Escort depth

- **Default:** a named greeter for arrival only, about twenty minutes of one person's time, plus a printed contact card. The card is the fallback when nobody can be spared.
- **The dedicated full-visit escort is deleted** for guests who want to move unremarked at a flat event. A person shadowing one attendee all day is a status marker whatever the badges say.
- **Escort plus protocol/security liaison** is promoted only if a guest arrives with a protective detail or advance team. If so:
  - name one counterpart
  - take their requirements verbatim
  - answer each in writing (yes, no, or a substitute) before the day
  - walk the route if they ask
  - state in the same message that the code of conduct applies to them

The greeter brief is one page: who, when and where, the route, what they asked for and what was refused (both quoted), the visibility instruction in one line, and the handoff. The greeter never says "VIP", never introduces the guest to anyone who has not been told, and never volunteers a departure time.

## 6. Two day-of decisions, named in advance

- **Who decides on the spot when someone asks for a photograph of a guest:** [name to fill in].
- **Who says no on the guest's behalf:** [name to fill in]. Suggested lines:
  - to an attendee: "Not right now, sorry, we're on a tight schedule."
  - to a camera: "No photos of this group, please."

## 7. Handoffs

- Journalists, accreditation and any press badge: `event-press-relations`.
- Speakers among the 44: `event-speaker-experience`.
- Sponsor personnel and anything a signed agreement covers: `event-sponsor-fulfillment`.
- Check-in desk changes: `event-attendee-experience`.
- Seating or program placement: `event-schedule-design` and `event-run-of-show`.
- Any access need: `event-accessibility-inclusion`.
- Crowd, medical or credible-threat issues: `event-risk-management`.
- Food, drink and the evening programme: `event-hospitality`.

## What to do instead of the lanyard

1. **Sort the 44 first.** Mark each as speaker, sponsor personnel, press or general attendee. Most will leave the list at this step.
2. **Ask the remainder one line at invite time.** For example: "Anything we should know to make your day easier: arrival time, discretion, a point of contact?" This answers the visibility question from the guest instead of guessing. The "nice to have" people get a personal note from an organizer who actually knows them, which costs nothing and creates no tier.
3. **Give the door team one job.** Every attendee gets the same treatment. Only the door lead and room leads hold the few names that qualify.
4. **Delete the notability column and set a deletion date** for whatever remains of the roster.

## How I would measure it

These signals are this skill's own construction, not benchmarks. Fix the targets before the event.

- **Obligation met, per guest:** zero requirements first discovered at the door.
- **Tier visibility:** ask two attendees near a guest whether they noticed anyone being treated differently. At a flat event, any "yes" is a finding.
- **Image consent:** zero published images a guest did not agree to.
- **Roster deletion:** confirmed gone by its date, by the named holder.

## Caveats and open questions

- **The menu orderings are argued, not measured.** Three of the four value and effort orderings are clean by construction rather than derived from event data. The lanyard verdict rests on the qualification test and the deletion rules for a flat register, not on a numeric ranking.
- **I could not run the interview.** These answers would sharpen or change parts of this brief:
  1. Has any of the 44, or their office, stated a requirement: arrival window, security, discretion or a point of contact?
  2. Is the badge print run still open?
  3. Is this a recurring relationship for any of the "nice to have" dozen, such as a funder or board member?
  4. Is anyone arriving with a protective detail?
  5. Who holds the spreadsheet, who else can see it, and where is it stored?

Per the skill's own rule, I would present sections 1 to 5 for your approval one at a time before treating them as final.
