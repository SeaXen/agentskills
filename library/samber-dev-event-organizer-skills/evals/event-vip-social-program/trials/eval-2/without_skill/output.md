## Short answer

Drop the uniform two-seat cap. Aurelio's signed agreement entitles it to 4 seats, so capping it at 2 would be a breach, not a fairness measure. What protects you from favouritism accusations is a written rule applied the same way to everyone, not an identical number for everyone.

## Why the cap fails

- ❌ A cap of 2 for Aurelio breaches a signed Platinum benefit. It also makes you the party who broke the contract, which is worse than any favouritism claim.
- ⚠️ The same cap on Kestrel and Bowline is arbitrary unless it matches their tiers. If either one has a seat benefit in its package, that number applies.
- ✅ Fairness means the same rule for everyone: entitlement first, then discretionary seats by a published criterion.

## Steps

1. **Read all three agreements.** Check each one's tier and whether it lists lunch seats. Check the wording for conditions, such as a names-by-date deadline, a seat transfer clause, or "up to".
2. **Count the seats.** Take the lunch capacity and subtract these, to get the discretionary pool:
   - contractual seats across all sponsors
   - speakers
   - your own staff
   - hosts and VIPs
3. **Set a written seat rule.** For example:
   - Seats listed in the agreement are honored.
   - A sponsor whose package lists no seats gets the standard tier allotment, or buys extras at a stated price.
   - Discretionary seats go first-come, within the pool, until the names deadline.
4. **Decide Kestrel and Bowline against that rule.**
   - **Kestrel (2 seats):** if its package lists 2 or more, confirm. If its package is silent, 2 is fine as the standard allotment, not as a favour.
   - **Bowline ("a couple of people"):** don't read this as 2. Reply with the number its package allows, and ask for names, titles and a deadline. Vague asks get a precise answer.
   - **Aurelio (4 seats):** confirm 4, cite the agreement, and ask for names by the deadline. Don't frame this as an exception. It's an entitlement.
5. **Hold the speaker's request.** Don't answer yes or no until steps 1–3 are done.

## The speaker's former colleague

She has no contractual claim. The lunch is a paid sponsor benefit, so a free seat for a non-partner cheapens it. Before deciding, find out:

- Where does the colleague work? A competitor of a sponsor, or a prospect, changes the answer.
- What does the speaker agreement promise? Check for a guest or plus-one clause.

Default answer: no lunch seat. Offer a conference pass or reception access instead, since that costs you less and is easy to defend.

Make an exception only if a seat is still free after the names deadline and the colleague has no conflict with a sponsor. Treat it as a one-off and don't promise it now.
