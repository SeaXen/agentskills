# Foundry Conf roundtable: does "publish criteria and let them do the arguing" hold?

**No, not as stated.** Two facts you gave me break it, and a third question decides how bad it is. This uses the `event-vip-social-program` skill's logic. I haven't run its full interview. Several answers below are assumptions, and I've marked them.

## Why it fails

1. ❌ **The venue sets the capacity and won't move.** The skill deletes "open nomination with published criteria" when the seat count comes from the venue or budget, not from the conversation you want to have. Published criteria against a number you don't control are a promise you can't keep. If more people meet the criteria than the Blue Room holds, you've turned a capacity limit into a broken commitment. Each refused person will also have a better grievance than "we ran out of seats," because they met the published bar.
2. ❌ **Criteria select on who applied, not on who needed to be there.** The skill's own menu ranks this rung last for putting the right people in the room. Capacity is a constraint, never a criterion. If the room is smaller than the purpose, shrink the purpose deliberately instead of letting the fire limit decide who mattered.
3. ⚠️ **The prospectus already published the roundtable as a Gold benefit.** That changes the question before the allocation rule does. Seats the agreement sold are a deliverable, not an allocation problem. The skill is blunt about it: applying your allocation rule to sold seats is "a breach dressed as fairness." Those seats belong to `event-sponsor-fulfillment`, against what each signed agreement says.

## The question that decides everything

I haven't seen your signed Gold agreements. Please check three things:

- **Is the roundtable in the agreements, or only in the March prospectus?** Prospectus-only wording may still bind you, depending on whether the agreements incorporate it. Check this with whoever signs your contracts.
- **What does it promise?** "An invited-guests roundtable" can be read as "we invite whom we choose." It can also be read as "Gold gets a seat." Marketing's wording is ambiguous, and the contract text settles it.
- **Which of the four asking sponsors are Gold?** Non-Gold sponsors are plain requests. Gold sponsors may be owed something.

## What I'd do instead

1. **Get the Blue Room number from the venue in writing.** That figure is the ceiling for everything below.
2. **Take sold seats off the top.** Route the Gold sponsors to `event-sponsor-fulfillment` and honour what the agreements say. If the agreements promise a seat to each Gold sponsor and there are more Gold sponsors than the room holds, you have a prospectus problem. Don't ration your way out of it. Your options are a larger room (Menu C), a second session, or an honest conversation with the affected sponsors.
3. **Allocate the remainder with a cap per asking party.** Q5 shows several parties asking at once: non-Gold sponsors, speakers, and probably your own team. Under the skill's rule, that means "host list + capped nominations per asking party." Settle each party's allowance as a number before anyone else is promised anything. "You have N seats" is a different conversation than "who would you like to invite." You build the list. A nomination is a request, not a claim.
4. **Write the refusal rule now and cite the same rule to every party.** Defensibility comes from one rule applied identically, not from a published rubric. It is cheap only if it exists before the next ask. Pair every refusal with something real: an introduction during a break, a slot with a committee member, or the general evening.
5. **Don't publish criteria.** The prospectus has already published the room. Add no further public promise about how selection works. Keep the rule private and cite it individually.
6. **One named owner** holds the list, sends every invitation, works the door and answers every refusal. Make sure nobody else, marketing included, tells anyone yes. Withdrawing an accepted invitation costs more than the seat did.
7. **Handle speakers separately.** Don't extend the speakers' dinner budget logic to them. They go through the same capped-nominations rule unless their speaker agreement says otherwise.
