**Short answer: no, criteria won't do the arguing for you.** The problem is a promise, not a selection method. Criteria help only after you have settled what you owe.

## Why it doesn't hold

1. **Paying sponsors have a claim, and criteria don't remove it.** The prospectus lists the roundtable as a Gold benefit. If you publish "apply and we'll pick," a Gold sponsor reads it as "the benefit I paid for is now a lottery I can lose." Merit criteria applied to paying customers look like a bait-and-switch, however fair they are.
2. **Speakers and sponsors have different claims.** Sponsors hold a (possibly contractual) promise. Speakers hold none, only goodwill. One shared pool compares unlike things, and any ranking will offend one group.
3. **An application process creates losers.** "Invited guests" means you invite. "Apply" invites everyone, including people who hadn't asked, and turns a quiet no into a visible rejection. You have 6 askers now. Publishing could give you 30.
4. **Criteria will look reverse-engineered.** If they happen to select the people you already had in mind, the people who lose will notice. Then you have the original problem plus a credibility problem.
5. **It leaves the real question open.** Do the entitlements exceed the seats? Criteria can't fix that.

## What to do instead

1. **Get the facts first.**
   - Read the exact Gold wording in the signed contracts, not only the prospectus. "Invited-guests roundtable with the programme committee" can mean the sponsor attends, or the sponsor nominates a guest, or you invite whomever you choose.
   - Get the Blue Room's written fire capacity. Subtract the programme committee members and any staff.
   - Count how many sponsors hold the Gold benefit, and how many seats each is owed.
2. **Allocate in two layers.**
   - **Entitlements first.** Whatever you promised in writing gets seats (for example, one per Gold sponsor). This is a debt, not a competition.
   - **Discretionary seats second.** Speakers and any non-Gold askers share what's left. You decide, as the organiser. Don't invite applications.
3. **If entitlements exceed capacity, that is your mistake to own.** Marketing's wording is an internal problem, and the sponsors don't care whose wording it was. Options, in order of preference:
   - Move the roundtable to a bigger room.
   - Run two sittings.
   - Cap guests at one per sponsor.
   - Offer a remedy: a different benefit, a private session with the committee, or a fee credit.
4. **Use the fire capacity as your honest, neutral constraint.** "The room legally holds N, and the venue won't change it" is a fact nobody can argue with. A criteria rubric is a judgment call people can argue with. Lead with the number.
5. **Tell the six askers separately, without publishing anything.**
   - **Sponsors:** confirm what the benefit includes, give the seat count and how it's allocated, and name any remedy.
   - **Speakers:** thank them and say seats are limited by the room. Offer an alternative, such as a speakers' dinner or a committee Q&A slot.

## When criteria are useful

If you have discretionary seats left, set a short internal rule for yourself, such as topic fit for the roundtable or balance across sponsors and speakers. Write it down so you can explain a decision, but keep it private. If someone asks, you can say "the committee chose for topic fit within the room's limit."

## Draft line for sponsors

> "The roundtable takes place in the Blue Room, which holds N people under venue fire rules. Each Gold sponsor has [X] seat(s); I'll confirm yours by [date]."
