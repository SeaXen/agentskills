# DunkelFlex Conf 2027: volunteer plan

## Diagnosis

- A 3-hour shift on a 10-hour day looks light. But the last shift of the day doesn't fit (3+3+3 leaves 1 hour), and setup, handoff and breakdown pad each shift. Most people ended up on site, on duty or on call, all day.
- Nobody guaranteed them any attendee time, so the grid was built around coverage and gave them whatever was left.
- Two people bought tickets and gave them up. That means the sign-up flow let a ticket-holder trade their conference for a shift without anyone noticing.

## Principles

1. **Build the grid around attendee time first, then fill coverage.** Each volunteer's must-see talks are fixed before any shift is placed.
2. **Cap duty hours per person.** Proposed: 6 hours on duty across the whole event, out of about 20 event hours. That leaves roughly 70% free.
3. **Protect the shared moments.** Opening keynote, closing keynote and the evening social are duty-free for everyone. The social is where volunteers meet the community, which is often why they signed up.
4. **A volunteer never gives up a ticket.** Every volunteer gets a ticket with the same access as a paying attendee.
5. **Let volunteers leave a shift for a must-see talk.** A small float pool covers them.

## Capacity check (do this first, in the spreadsheet)

1. List every position (registration, room hosts, speaker care, info desk, AV runner, and so on) with its open hours per day.
2. Sum position-hours across both days. That is demand D.
3. Volunteers needed = D ÷ 6.
4. Example with made-up numbers: 8 positions × 10 h × 2 days = 160 h. At 6 h each, that needs about 27 volunteers, so 34 leaves a buffer. If your real D is above 204 h (34 × 6), cut position hours, not the cap.

Two ways to cut D:
- Shape demand. Registration is busy 08:00–10:00 and thin after that. Close the info desk during talks and keep it open at breaks.
- Use in-room roles. A room host sits inside the talk. Count those hours at half toward the cap (2 h in-room = 1 h against 6). They don't need to be an exception to the cap, because the volunteer sees the talk.

## Shift design

- Use **2-hour shifts**, not 3. Two hours also lines up with the program: shift edges go on talk-slot boundaries, not on the clock.
- Put a **15-minute overlap** at each handoff, so nobody has to arrive early or stay late.
- Maximum **2 shifts per day** and **3 across the event**. No back-to-back shifts without a 90-minute gap.
- Keep **3–4 floaters** on the grid at all times. They cover sick calls, lunch breaks and "I need to leave for my talk" requests. Floaters count in the 6 h cap like anyone else.
- Everyone gets a **free lunch slot of 45 minutes or more**, written in the grid and not left to chance.

## Spreadsheet changes

Add these columns and checks to your grid:

| Column | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Must-see talks (3–5) | Collected from each volunteer before you place shifts |
| Total duty hours | Flag red above 6 |
| Longest unbroken duty stretch | Flag red above 2.5 h |
| Talks fully attended | Count of must-see talks with no shift overlap; target is all of them |
| Free time before and after keynotes | Confirm both keynotes are duty-free |
| Ticket type | Confirms every volunteer holds a ticket |

Rule of thumb for moving blocks: you can move a shift freely unless it overlaps someone's must-see talk or pushes them over a cap. A conflict means the swap fails.

## Timeline to June 2027

1. **Now to end of October.**
   - Contact the two people who gave up their tickets. Offer a free ticket to next year's conference, or a refund if they prefer. Say that the plan below exists because of their feedback.
   - Reply to the exit-chat group with the headline changes: duty cap, must-see talks first, tickets guaranteed.
2. **November.** Pull last year's grid and compute each person's actual duty hours and talks missed. This gives you a baseline to beat, and you can state it back to volunteers.
3. **January to February.** Open volunteer sign-up with the promise stated up front: "6 hours on duty, a ticket, and the keynotes off."
4. **After the program is published (about 6–8 weeks out).** Collect must-see talks from each volunteer.
5. **About 4 weeks out.** Build the grid in the spreadsheet using the capacity check. Run the red-flag columns.
6. **About 2 weeks out.** Publish individual schedules. Allow swaps through a single shared sheet, and keep a minimum of 3 floaters even after swaps.
7. **Briefing, 1 week out.** Give everyone their shift, their free blocks and their float contact. Say plainly: "If you need to leave for a talk, tell a floater."
8. **Event day.** One lead per day checks that volunteers actually take their free blocks and lunch. Leads set the example by taking theirs.
9. **Within 48 hours after.** Send a short exit survey with a measured question: "How many of your must-see talks did you attend?" Compare it to the baseline from November.

## What to measure

- Average and maximum duty hours per volunteer (target: ≤ 6 and ≤ 6).
- Share of must-see talks attended (target: 90% or more).
- Volunteers who held a ticket before and during the event (target: 100%).
- Volunteer retention year on year. This is the number that tells you whether the fix worked.

## Risks

- **Not enough volunteers for the capped hours.** Recruit more before you raise the cap, or cut open hours on low-traffic positions. Raising the cap brings back last year's problem.
- **Floaters become the new "worked all day" group.** Rotate the float role and keep their hours capped like everyone else.
- **Volunteers skip free blocks to help.** Leads have to model taking breaks, and the briefing has to give explicit permission.

## What I assumed

You didn't say how many shifts each person worked, which positions you staff, or whether the 34 volunteers were spread over both days. The 6-hour cap and the 8-position example are placeholders. Your real position list is what sets the right numbers.
