# Verdict

The goals are good, but I'd change two of the three moves. Keep the veteran marker, change the Discord, and don't keep the spreadsheet as it is.

## 1. Keeping the full spreadsheet: change this first ⚠️

You're in Portugal, so GDPR applies.

- **Emergency contacts** are data about third parties who never agreed to anything. You needed them for the event, and that purpose has ended.
- **Dietary answers** can reveal health or religion, which are special-category data. They are the riskiest column.
- **Phone numbers and t-shirt sizes** aren't needed to track who helped.
- **The legal basis** was running the event. Keeping everything "just in case" for a year or more has no stated purpose and breaks the storage-limitation principle.
- **Breach exposure.** One leaked sheet containing everything is much worse than a leaked list of names.

What to do:

1. Copy a minimal roster into a new file with these columns: name, email, editions helped, roles held, and an opt-in flag with the date it was given.
2. Delete dietary answers, emergency contacts, phone numbers and t-shirt sizes from the old sheet. Keep them only until the event is closed out, and set a deletion date now.
3. Email this year's volunteers and ask them to opt in: "Want to stay on the crew list and be invited to next year? Yes/No." Keep only the yes answers.
4. Collect the sensitive fields again each year, and delete them again each year.

The minimal roster gives you the retention memory you want. It also answers "who helped?" without the risk.

## 2. A permanent volunteer Discord: use the one you already have

A separate 40-person server tends to go quiet by February. It also gives your community person a second thing to maintain.

- **Preferred:** a private `#crew` category in the existing attendee Discord, gated by a "Crew" role.
- **Why:** one server to moderate, and one place for volunteers to see the community they're serving.
- **Consent:** invite people by email, only if they opted in. Don't add anyone automatically.
- **Check with your community person first.** They're the owner, so ask before you commit them.
- **Give it a rhythm.** "Year-round" works only with a few fixed moments:
  1. A volunteer call in January.
  2. A mid-year social or video call.
  3. A debrief right after the event, while memories are fresh.
  4. Role handover notes posted before sign-ups open.

## 3. Veteran crew marker: keep it, but make it do something

It's cheap and it's real recognition. Two tweaks:

- **Mark by edition, not just "veteran".** You have no records from editions 1–3, so ask volunteers to self-declare past years. Add a small "Founding crew" mark for edition 1 if you can verify it.
- **Give it a light function.** Same jobs, but veterans are the people first-timers and attendees can ask. Pair each first-timer with a veteran buddy.
- **Watch for staff confusion.** Attendees may read the badge as authority, so keep it visually modest.

## 4. The part you haven't mentioned: retention of knowledge

Starting from zero is as much about missing runbooks as missing people. Keep one page per role with the shift schedule, common problems and contacts. Volunteers who return get faster, and first-timers get a head start even when veterans don't come back.

## 5. Measure it

You can't compute last year's return rate, but this year's roster is your baseline. Next spring, track:

- the share of this year's volunteers who return
- the share of the crew who are first-timers
- why people declined, from a one-question exit survey
