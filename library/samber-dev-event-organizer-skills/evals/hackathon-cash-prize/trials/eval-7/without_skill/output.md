# Torvik Open prize pool: sizing and pitch

I don't know last edition's prize pool, the total budget, or what Torvik Open's audience is, so the numbers below are assumptions. Replace them where I guessed wrong.

## 1. Decisions to make first

1. **Name the target.** "Way more" than 40 isn't a number finance can budget against. I suggest 120–150 submissions, which is 3–4x.
2. **Don't present $50k as the lever for that target.** Prize size is a weak driver of submission count. These matter more:
   - reach (how many relevant developers see it),
   - friction (how hard it is to submit),
   - runway (weeks between announcement and deadline),
   - trust (known judges, clear rules, last edition's winners paid on time).
3. **Treat $50k as a sensible headline, not a magic one.** In 2026 it is respectable but won't make press on its own. Coverage comes from the angle, the judges, and the theme.

## 2. Sizing: what to tell finance

Give her a cash figure and a promotion figure, not just "prizes."

| Line | Amount | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Headline prize pool | $50,000 | Committed, paid only on award |
| Payout overhead | $5,000–7,500 | About 10–15%: payment fees, FX, tax forms, winner verification |
| Promotion to reach 3–4x | $10,000–15,000 | Community partners, newsletter slots, creator or judge fees |
| **Total ask** | **$65,000–72,500** | |

- **Cash timing.** Prizes are a contingent liability until awarded. Ask her to reserve the full $50k but book the cash out at award time. That makes the request easier to approve.
- **Cost per submission.** At 150 submissions, $50k is about $333 per submission, or about $450 with promotion. Last edition was 40 submissions. Check your prior total spend divided by 40 against this.
- **Scenarios.** Give her three, so she can choose:
  - **Lean:** $25k pool, with the same promotion spend.
  - **Base:** $50k pool.
  - **Stretch:** $50k plus a sponsor-funded top-up, which costs you nothing.
- **Sponsors.** If any sponsor can fund part of the pool, take it. Then your cash ask drops, for example to $35k cash plus $15k sponsor-funded. Label the split honestly.
- **Rule against over-spending.** Check at the midpoint. If submissions are behind pace, add promotion money. Don't raise the prize. A bigger number late rarely moves submissions.

## 3. Structure: more winners beat one big prize

Developers decide on their odds of winning, not only the top figure. With 40 entries, a top-three prize already had decent odds. With 150, odds worsen, so more winners keeps entering worthwhile.

Example split of $50k:

| Prize | Count | Each | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grand prize | 1 | $15,000 | $15,000 |
| Runner-up | 2 | $5,000 | $10,000 |
| Category prizes (your 4–5 themes) | 5 | $3,000 | $15,000 |
| Community choice | 1 | $2,500 | $2,500 |
| Honorable mentions | 15 | $500 | $7,500 |
| **Total** | **24** | | **$50,000** |

This gives 24 winners, so roughly 1 in 6 entries wins something at 150 submissions. Entry is also easier to justify for small teams.

Include a **quality clause** in the rules: prizes are awarded only to entries that meet a minimum bar, and unawarded money is not guaranteed to be spent. This protects finance if submissions disappoint.

## 4. The public pitch

- **Lead with specificity.** "$50,000 in cash prizes, 24 winners" beats "$50,000 pool." A concrete number reads as real.
- **Say what is cash.** Never inflate the headline with credits, swag, or in-kind items without labeling them. Developers notice, and it damages trust. Show cash and in-kind separately.
- **Show the payout.** Say how and when winners are paid, for example within 30 days of results, and that last edition's winners were paid. Name past winners if they agree.
- **Lower the cost of entering.** Allow existing projects, a short submission form, and a 5-minute README-based entry. Most entry drop-off is effort, not motivation.
- **Give an 8+ week runway**, with an early-submission milestone and reminders.
- **Name judges early.** Known judges add credibility and give partners something to share.
- **Press angle.** Pitch a story, not the number: who won last time and what they built, the theme, who is judging. The prize is supporting detail.

## 5. Check before committing to $50k

- **Legal.** Contest rules, eligibility by country, sanctions screening, and tax withholding on foreign winners (US payers can withhold up to 30%). Have counsel check this. It affects the overhead line above.
- **Funnel.** Online hackathons commonly see only a small share of registrants submit, often 10–20% (a rule of thumb, not a measured figure for your audience). For 150 submissions, plan for roughly 800–1,500 registrations and size the promotion to deliver that.
- **Past participants.** Ask last edition's 40 teams to refer others. It's the cheapest reach you have.

## 6. Questions for you

1. What did last edition cost in total, and what was its pool?
2. Who is the audience, and how many people could you reach through existing channels today?
3. Can any sponsor fund part of the pool?
4. Is a hard deadline fixed, or can you extend the runway to 8+ weeks?
