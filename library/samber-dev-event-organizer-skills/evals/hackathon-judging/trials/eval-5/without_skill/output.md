# Tie at 4.20: what to do in the next 40 minutes

**Recommendation:** don't re-watch the demos, and don't open with a coin flip. Check the unrounded scores first. If they're truly tied, have the four judges cast independent secret ballots. If the ballots split 2-2, flip the coin, with both teams' consent. Meanwhile, ask the partner for a second ticket.

## Do now

1. **Check the unrounded totals (3 min).**
   - 4.20 may be a rounded display.
   - Recompute from raw judge sheets at full precision, and check the weighted arithmetic for errors.
   - Using the same rubric at higher precision is not a new rule, and it often breaks the tie.
   - Do this before anything else.

2. **Fix the tiebreak procedure before anyone looks at who it favors (5 min).**
   - Write the order down first, with the two chairs and one more organizer agreeing.
   - Don't pick a tiebreaker after seeing which team it helps. That is the main fairness risk, more than the method itself.

3. **If still tied, run a secret ballot (10 min).**
   - Ask each of the four judges, separately and in writing, which team should win.
   - Don't hold a discussion first, and don't show them each other's ballots.
   - A majority wins.
   - Judges with a conflict (mentored a team, employed by one) abstain.

4. **If the ballots split 2-2, flip the coin.**
   - Flip it in front of both team leads, either backstage or on stage.
   - Get both leads' consent to a stage flip beforehand. A stage flip gets the laugh, but it makes the loser the punchline of a prize they earned.
   - Backstage is safer. Announce the result on stage.

5. **Ask the partner for a second ticket now (2 min).**
   - Ask for a second conference pass, or a runner-up perk.
   - Partners usually say yes, because a co-winner story is good PR for them.
   - If they agree, the tie stops being zero-sum and you may not need a tiebreak at all. Even if you still pick one trip winner, the other team leaves with something.

6. **Announce the tie openly.**
   - Say something like: "Two teams tied at 4.20. Our rules were silent, so the organizers set a tiebreak: [X]."
   - Hiding the tie looks worse later.

## Why not the two proposals as stated

- **Judges re-watch both demos and revote:**
  - It takes 15 minutes or more, with no slack before ceremony.
  - Judges already know the totals, so they're no longer scoring independently.
  - Fatigue and lobbying can sway them.
  - It also re-judges the event instead of breaking a tie.
- **Coin flip first:**
  - It's fair in the narrow sense.
  - It throws away information you already have: the raw scores and the judges' preferences.
  - The ballot step uses that information and takes about 10 minutes.

## What to write down for next time

Add a "Ties and scoring" section to the published rules. Draft text:

> **Scoring precision.** Totals are calculated to [3] decimal places. Rounded scores are not used for ranking.
>
> **Ties.** If two or more teams tie for any prize, the following apply in order:
> 1. The team with the higher score on [the highest-weighted criterion] wins.
> 2. If still tied, each judge submits a private ranked ballot of the tied teams. The majority wins.
> 3. If still tied, the organizers decide by coin flip, witnessed by a representative of each tied team.
>
> **Judges' conflicts.** A judge with a connection to a team abstains on that team. [An odd number of judges or one tiebreak judge] ensures a decisive ballot.
>
> **Final decision.** The organizers' ruling is final. Results are announced with any tiebreak used.

Also decide and publish these:
- **Judge count:** use an odd number, or name a tiebreak judge. Four judges can split 2-2.
- **Single-item prizes:** say whether the prize can be split or duplicated. Here, say whether the partner can add a second ticket.
- **Prize conditions:** name the travel requirements, the age limit, what happens if the winner can't attend, and whether the ticket can be transferred.
- **Disputes:** state a window for score appeals, and say who hears them.
- **Score publication:** say whether per-criterion scores are released and when.

After the event, add a short note on what happened this time, so the next tie follows the precedent.
