# Verdict: don't cancel at 14. Re-base the target on expected attendance.

The 65 comes from applying a ratio to the wrong number. I'd put the real target at 14 to 20 mentors. The numbers below use my assumptions, which I've marked.

## Why 65 is the wrong target

1. **Signups aren't attendees.** Free events usually lose a large share of signups. My rule of thumb is that 50 to 65% show up, but that figure is an assumption, not data. If you have past Coastline Hack numbers, use those instead. At 50 to 65%, 260 signups means about 130 to 170 people.
2. **Participants aren't all non-experts.** The "one expert per four non-experts" line fits beginner or learning-focused events. Most hackathon participants are working developers, designers, or students who can already build. If your crowd is mostly beginners, the ratio is fair. If it's mixed, it overstates the need.
3. **Mentors serve teams, not individuals.** At about 4 per team, 130 to 170 people is roughly 33 to 43 teams. A mentor handles a team in short, bursty visits. Nobody sits with one person for hours.
4. **Mentors work in shifts.** A hackathon runs many hours, so 65 mentors present at once would be an enormous staffing level. Real coverage is mentors present at any one moment, not total headcount.

## What 14 actually gives you

| Show rate | Attendees | Teams (~4) | Attendees per mentor | Teams per mentor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50% | 130 | ~33 | ~9 | ~2.3 |
| 65% | 170 | ~43 | ~12 | ~3.1 |

Even if only half the mentors are on the floor at once, that's about 1 mentor per 5 to 6 teams. That's workable but thin at peak. 14 is not a crisis, and it isn't comfortable either.

## Where your co-organizer is right

Your event is open-ended, so no mentor can cover every topic. The mentor pool needs breadth, not just headcount. Cover these:

- Backend and infra
- Frontend and UX
- AI/ML
- Design
- Pitching and product

Check your 14 against that list. A gap there matters more than the raw count.

## Proposal to bring to him

1. **Replace 65 with a ratio on confirmed attendees.** Target 1 mentor per 8 to 10 attendees. That's 13 to 21 mentors for 130 to 170 people. It's his idea, applied to the right number.
2. **Reconfirm attendance 7 days out.** Send a "confirm or release your spot" email. This gives a real headcount and cuts no-shows. Re-run the math then.
3. **Fill the gap to about 20.** Sources, in order of yield:
   - Sponsor engineers (ask for 1 to 2 each).
   - Past participants and winners.
   - Local meetup organizers.
   - Your own network.
4. **Cover the skill gaps first,** using the list above.
5. **Make mentoring cheap.** Offer 2-hour shifts, a bookable office-hours board, and a shared channel so teams can flag for help. This stretches 14 to 20 people further than a fixed-station model.

## Decision rules

| Situation at T-7 days | Action |
|---|---|
| Reconfirmed attendees ≤ 170 and mentors ≥ 14 with skills covered | Go ahead |
| Reconfirmed attendees > 200 and mentors < 20 | Cap admissions and waitlist the rest. Don't cancel |
| Mentors < 10, or a major skill area has none | Escalate: delay, or shrink the event |
| Attendees are mostly beginners | Tighten the target to 1 per 6, about 22 to 28 mentors |

Cancel only if you can't staff mentors for the confirmed headcount at the ratio. Even then, shrinking beats cancelling.

## Two things to check before you talk to him

- **What type of event does the guide describe?** If it's a beginner or education hackathon, its ratio may not transfer to yours.
- **What's your real show rate?** Do you have past data? That one number settles most of the argument.

Both are quick to check and would change the target.
