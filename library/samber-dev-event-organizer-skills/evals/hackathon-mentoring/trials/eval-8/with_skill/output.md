## Handout

# Mentoring at Sable Street Hack

You are here so teams learn by building. The prizes are $12,000 in cash, half of it funded by our sponsor. Judges will read each team's repository and decide who wins from what the team built. That is why this page spends most of its words on one line: **the difference between helping a team and building for it.**

### 1. Your slot

- Your slot is a commitment: `[day, hours]`, for the `[track tag]` track.
- Staying longer is a courtesy we can't schedule around. Please don't leave your slot until your replacement arrives.
- If you can't make it, tell `[contact]` as early as you can. We will find cover.
- Don't stack on one team. If two mentors are already with a team, go to another.

### 2. How teams reach you

Teams read this in the brief and hear it at the opening:
- **Recognise a mentor by** `[marker, e.g. badge colour, @mentor role]`.
- **To ask for one:** `[channel / desk / walk up]`. It is normal to ask early.
- **Someone will come.** If no mentor covers the skill, the team is told so plainly, early enough to change approach.

A request is anything in that channel. Treat it as a request even when it's phrased as a complaint.

### 3. The boundary

> **Mentors explain, diagnose, unblock and demonstrate. Mentors do not write code that ships in a submission.**
>
> If you need to show something in code, write it outside the team's repository (a scratch file, a message, a whiteboard). The team types the version that lands.
>
> If you have already committed to a team's repository, tell the team to declare it in their submission, and tell `[contact]`.

Why this matters:
- A project a mentor carried teaches the team nothing. It also competes against teams who were not carried.
- Submitted code is public, so the commit history shows whose work it is.
- A hidden mentor commit becomes a dispute at the ceremony. A declared one is just a fact the judges can weigh.

If a team asks you to take over, say no kindly. Offer the next best thing: a smaller step, a pointer to the right file, or a question that gets them there.

### 4. Scenarios

**A. Opaque deployment error, hour 20.**
- Right: read the log with them, name the class of failure, ask what changed since it last worked, and point at the config file. They find the line themselves.
- Wrong: "let me drive". You fix it in two minutes, they can't reproduce the fix, and your commit is in their repo.

**B. Team says "just push the fix, we're out of time."**
- Right: shrink the problem with them, show the pattern in a scratch file, and stay while they type it. Tell them honestly what a smaller scope would look like.
- Wrong: pushing it. "They asked me to" doesn't change what judges will read.

**C. You can see a bug in their code, they haven't hit it yet.**
- Right: ask a question that exposes it ("what happens if this list is empty?").
- Wrong: fixing it silently, or telling them the architecture is wrong.

**D. You don't know the answer.**
- Right: say "I haven't used this." Then send them to the escalation route (section 5). A confident guess can cost a team hours.
- Wrong: improvising.

**E. A team's idea looks weak to you.**
- Right: help with the problem they brought. Keep your verdict to yourself.
- Wrong: giving an opinion. From a mentor it reads as a judgement, and you are not a judge.

**F. You'd love to just fix it on their laptop.**
- Right: ask before touching the laptop, keyboard or repo. If typing is truly unavoidable, get a yes first and explain what you're doing as you do it.
- Wrong: reaching over.

### 5. When nobody on the floor covers the skill

1. Ask a mentor who has read the docs. Tell the team plainly that this is the level of help they're getting.
2. Ask `[sponsor channel]` if it's the sponsor's technology.
3. Suggest the team ask the room. Another team may hold the skill. Make it a request, never an assignment.
4. If none of those works, tell the team there is no help for this, early. A team told at hour 4 can pivot. A team told at hour 30 cannot.

Log every gap with the track and the hour. We use it to plan next year.

### 6. Working with exhausted people

Teams are tired and often junior, and they ask in front of teammates. Your authority is real, so use it gently.
- Ask before you touch anything.
- Leave the idea unevaluated.
- Say what you don't know.
- Don't use this time to advertise your employer, jobs or yourself.

**If you are a sponsor employee:** you are helping the team with the problem they brought. This is not a sales, recruiting or demo conversation. If asked about your company, pricing or jobs, answer briefly and point to `[booth / channel]`. The mentor marker makes it hard for a participant to decline a conversation, which is why this is not the place for one.

### 7. If you are also a judge `[keep or delete]`

We keep a log of which mentors spent meaningful time with which teams. A mentor who helped a team recuses from judging that team. Tell `[contact]` after any substantial session so the log is right before judging.

### 8. Anything that isn't a technical question

A distressed participant, a dispute inside a team, or a conduct or safety concern:
- Contact `[escalation contact, channel]`.
- Conduct reports follow the event's code of conduct: `[link, contact]`. You may be the first person a report reaches. Pass it on and don't resolve it yourself.
