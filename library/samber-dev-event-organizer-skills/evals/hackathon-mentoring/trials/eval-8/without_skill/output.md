# Sable Street Hack: Mentor Guide

Thank you for mentoring. Eleven of you are confirmed, and most of you are doing this for the first time. This guide is short, so please read it before you arrive.

## Your job in one sentence

Help teams get unstuck and make better decisions, **without doing their work for them.**

Teams are competing for a $12,000 prize pool. Everything they submit has to be theirs, because the judges and the other teams are relying on that.

## The line: explain, don't build

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask questions: "What have you tried? What did you expect to happen?" | Take the keyboard |
| Explain a concept, a pattern or a trade-off | Write, rewrite or restructure their code |
| Sketch an approach on a whiteboard or in pseudo-code | Commit to their repo, or paste in large blocks of code |
| Point to docs, examples and the right search term | Fix the bug and hand them the fix |
| Show a tiny illustrative snippet (a few lines, in your own scratch file) | Build a component, endpoint or schema for them |
| Tell them what to cut so the demo works | Decide their idea, stack or scope for them |

**The test:** after you leave, could every team member explain every line of what changed and why? If not, you did too much.

Some cases are less clear:

- **The team is hopelessly stuck and time is running out.** Narrow the problem with them. Find the failing line together, and let them type the fix. If they truly cannot move, get an organizer. Do not quietly fix it.
- **Their architecture is poor.** Name the risk once, give them your reasoning, and let them decide. A rough design they understand beats a good one they don't.
- **A teammate asks you to "just do it, we're out of time."** Decline kindly. Offer a smaller scope instead.

A mentor who writes large parts of a team's project damages the event. It makes the result unfair to other teams, and it undermines the team's own learning. Our feedback form last year showed this has happened before. We know it comes from wanting to help. This year the line is explicit.

## How to run a session

1. **Ask first.** "What are you building, and where are you stuck?" Listen for two minutes before saying anything.
2. **Keep it to about 15 minutes.** Teams have a lot to do, and so do you.
3. **Leave with a next step.** End by asking: "What are you doing in the next 30 minutes?" The team should say it, not you.
4. **Spread your attention.** Check in on quiet teams, because they often don't ask. Don't camp with one team all day.
5. **Say "I don't know" freely.** Then help them find out. That's good mentoring.

## Sponsor and prize conflicts

Half of the prize pool is funded by our sponsor, so please follow these rules:

- **Don't steer teams toward sponsor products or APIs.** Answer questions about them if asked. Never pitch.
- **Don't discuss judging, scores, or who is "winning."** You don't know, and speculation affects teams.
- **If you work for the sponsor, tell the organizers and the teams you advise.** You are welcome here. Disclosure is the only requirement.
- **If you have any other stake** (a team member is your colleague, friend or relative, or you invest in or advise a participating company), tell an organizer. We may reassign you.

## Conduct

- Treat every participant with respect, whatever their experience level.
- Don't take ideas, code or contacts from teams for your own use.
- Don't share a team's work or ideas outside the event.
- Follow the event code of conduct **[link]**. Report problems to an organizer immediately.

## If something feels off

Contact an organizer, not the team, if:

- you realize you've gone past the line yourself. Say so, and we'll sort it out together without blame.
- you see another mentor doing a team's work.
- a team appears to have submitted work that isn't theirs.
- anyone is being harassed or is unsafe.
