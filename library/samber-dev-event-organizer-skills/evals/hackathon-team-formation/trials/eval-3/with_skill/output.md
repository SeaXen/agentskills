# Kestrel Hackday: team-formation decision set

**Short answer: not as planned.** The 40-minute mixer fails on three counts. The skill's alternative is a pitch round plus a stocked project backlog. Details below, section by section.

## Why the mixer plan fails

1. **No second space.** The skill promotes a mixer only when the cohort is mostly first-timers **and** a second space exists. You meet the first condition (70%) but not the second. A mixer in the only hall displaces the hacking, and 90 people talking at once in one room is loud.
2. **"Everybody meets everybody" doesn't fit the time.** 40 minutes ÷ 89 people ≈ 27 seconds each, with no time to move between people. In practice people meet 5–10 others, and those groups form by proximity and social comfort, not by skills.
3. **Nothing to build.** Only 6 of 90 people are bringing a project. A mixer forms social groups that then have to invent a project. The skill's failure mode is groups that gel, find no project, and dissolve by lunch. With 6 projects in the room, that is the likely outcome for most of your 84 people.

Roughly 70% first-timers also means many people will find a mixer harder, not easier. The "first-time hackers" cohort is the one most likely to end up unteamed.

## 1. Team-size rule (decide first)

**Recommend: "Teams can be 1-4 people."**

- The `1` answers the solo question in the same sentence as the cap.
- The cap of 4 is one organizer network's practitioner observation, not a study. Present it that way.
- At 90 people and a cap of 4, you need at least 23 teams. Pass that number to `hackathon-judging` as its input.
- Check the cap against your prize medium (`hackathon-cash-prize`) before you publish.
- **Composition rule:** none unless you run a beginners track. If you do, say how many team members must be first-timers and publish it with the brief. "First hackathon" is a cheap, low-review proxy. If it is keyed to age, student status, gender or disability, route it to review first.

## 2. Mechanic: pitch-and-join round, with a backlog behind it

The pitch round needs a project for people to join, and you have only 6 pitchers. That is the real constraint.

**Run sheet (the shape is from the skill; the minute figures are my own estimates, not published):**

1. **Welcome.** Include the code of conduct, "who has never been to a hackathon?", and logistics.
2. **Say out loud:** "A project of one is okay too." Also say that pitching an idea you won't build is not allowed, except for the organizer-stocked ideas below.
3. **Six participant pitches, 1 minute each.** Each covers name, problem, solution, and the skills or help needed. A named person enforces the 1-minute cut-off and says so in advance.
4. **Backlog read-out.** Organizers present the stocked projects as one-line pitches. The skill allows idea pitches nobody will lead only when there aren't enough ideas to go around, and here there aren't. Label them clearly as organizer-stocked.
5. **Join time.** Pitchers stand at a numbered marker in the hall. People walk to the project they want. Keep it inside your own time budget, and measure the real duration.

Round budget estimate: about 15–25 minutes in total, including the join time. Nothing published gives a limit for pitch count or join time, so these figures are mine. Hacking starts when the round ends.

**Backlog sizing, using your numbers:**

- Six pitched projects at cap 4 hold at most 24 people. That leaves roughly 66 people needing about 17 more projects.
- If the event is themed, the published ratio is one expert plus a workable project per about 4 non-experts. That is about 21 for 84 non-experts. It applies to themed events only, and the expert is embedded in one project.
- If the event is open-theme, the count is yours to choose. Plan about 15–20 as a starting point.
- A listed project counts only if it has ready-to-go tasks for newcomers at varied skill levels and a build environment that starts in under 20 minutes. Anything else is just a line of text.
- Meet the 6 pitchers about 10 days out. Get each to that bar and ask what newcomers can do on their project.

**Crowd aids (my suggestions, not published practice):** a numbered marker per project readable across the hall, and a skill-coded wristband at check-in so the room balances by discipline.

**Accessibility:** offer a non-verbal route into the same mechanic, such as a written project entry read out by the facilitator and a joinable list captured during the round. Take the design to `event-accessibility-inclusion`.

**Optional, if you still want a mixer feel:** use the hall's own time before the round, such as check-in and the welcome. Don't use a separate 40-minute slot. Only run a mixer with a stocked backlog behind it.

## 3. Unteamed-participant protocol (decide before the day)

With 70% first-timers, assume a real number of people will end the round without a team.

- **Backstops, in order:**
  1. Solo said out loud at the opening.
  2. The stocked backlog above.
  3. A **floor sweep by one named person**, covering the window. The offer, in this order: a short-handed team, then a stocked project, then solo as a choice. Never lead with solo.
- **Roster capture.** Write down team rosters when the round ends. The sweeper needs to know which teams have room, for example a team of 2 under a cap of 4.
- **Escalation, promoted.** First-timer-heavy cohorts and any minors meet its promotion condition.
  - It needs a named owner who can decide.
  - The ask goes privately to a team about a specific person and contribution, never as a room-wide appeal.
  - It ends with the participant choosing among real options, never with them being placed.
  - **Time-box:** the skill gives no number of minutes, so don't invent one. Anchor it to a clock your day already has: the end of the pitch round, first meal service, or first workshop break. Record what it actually took.
- **If Q9 shows nobody is free in the window,** the sweep and the escalation drop out entirely. Say that plainly. Don't pretend a rota exists.
- **Safeguarding:** if minors take part, a sweep is a safeguarding interaction, so use your event's existing posture. Never keep a named list of who was alone. Keep counts only.
- **Misconduct.** If someone is unteamed because of another participant's behavior, route it to `event-code-of-conduct`. Don't solve it by finding them another team.

## 4. Change policy

**Recommend: open until a published cut-off, then frozen.**

- Set the cut-off **after** the sweep and the escalation have run, and **before** judging opens. Freezing at the pitch round would stop the sweep from placing anyone.
- Express it as a wall-clock time. I need Q2 (the hacking window) to fill it in.
- If you publish a composition fraction, state what happens to a team that stops meeting it after someone leaves.
- Prizes are awarded against a submission (confirm via Q7), so "no stated policy" is off the table.

## 5. Handoffs

- **Brief (`hackathon-brief-design`):** the size rule with its solo answer, any composition fraction, and the change cut-off. Publish before registration closes, and get your explicit approval first.
- **Run of show (`event-run-of-show`):** the round's duration estimate, and the constraint that it runs immediately after the welcome and before the first line of code.
- **Volunteers (`event-volunteers`):** the named sweeper and the named cut-off enforcer.
- **Mentoring (`hackathon-mentoring`):** any stuck team needing technical help, not this skill.

## 7. Record for next edition

These are self-set gates, not industry standards, and only your own second edition gives you a comparison.

- How many registered alone versus as a team.
- How many were unteamed when the sweep ran. If nobody can state this number right after the round, the sweep has no target.
- What each of them did next.
- How long the pitch round actually took against its budget.
- How many teams changed membership after the cut-off.
