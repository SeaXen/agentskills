# Poking holes: whiteboard instead of pitch round (Harbourlight Hack, 140 confirmed)

**Verdict:** ⚠️ Keep the whiteboard. Don't drop the pitch step entirely, and don't open the board to ideas nobody will build. As written, the plan fails at team formation.

## 🔴 Holes that can sink the day

1. **No forcing moment for teams.**
   - Browsing "whenever they like" means nobody commits at a shared time.
   - Teams form at 10:00, 12:30 and 15:00, so late teams lose hours of build time.
   - Early teams are already full, and the latecomers are the quiet people.
   - **Fix:** set a hard team-lock time, for example 60 to 75 minutes after kickoff. After that, no new teams without organizer approval.

2. **The board has no way to join.**
   - A marker on a whiteboard records an idea. It doesn't record who is interested, who has joined, or whether the team is full.
   - Interested people can't signal interest, and idea owners can't see demand.
   - **Fix:** give each idea a template with three fields: one-line pitch, roles needed, and team size. Add a "joined" column, or use sticky notes and dots. Put the owner's name and contact on it.

3. **One whiteboard cannot serve 140 people.**
   - About 8 to 10 people can read a board at once, and the coffee area is already a traffic chokepoint.
   - Writing is serial. A marker queue forms, and handwriting is hard to read from more than 1 m.
   - 30 to 50 ideas won't fit on one board. The ones written last end up squeezed into the corners.
   - **Fix:** use several boards or wall panels, one idea per sheet or card, placed away from the coffee. Mirror them in a shared digital list for remote and late arrivals.

4. **Removing the pitch hurts the people who most need it.**
   - Newcomers, introverts and people without a team don't walk up to strangers to ask whether they can join.
   - A 60-second pitch gives the owner a warm-up and gives listeners a low-risk way to say "me."
   - Without pitches, well-known people attract teams, and unknown owners and solo attendees get left out.

## 🟠 Weak premise

5. **You may be misreading the "30 people" rule.**
   - The rule says one-by-one introductions don't scale to a single room. It doesn't say pitching can't scale.
   - Alternatives: cap pitches at 15 to 20 volunteers at 60 seconds each (about 20 minutes), run 4 parallel pitch rooms of about 35, or have pitchers stand at their sheet while people circulate.
   - Check whether the guide says what to do above 30. One guide is thin evidence.

6. **140 confirmed won't be 140 present.**
   - Free events often see 60 to 80% show up, which would be roughly 85 to 110 people, or about 20 to 28 teams of 4.
   - Size the number of boards and ideas for the expected attendance, then check against actual check-ins.

7. **Position and visibility bias.**
   - Ideas written first, at eye level, or near the entrance get the traffic. Late and low ideas are ignored.
   - There's no skill matching, so a team can form with five front-end developers and no backend.

## 🟠 "Write up ideas you won't build" is the weakest part

8. **It floods the board with orphans.**
   - If 140 people write 60 ideas and only about 25 teams form, 35 ideas are decoys.
   - The buildable ideas get buried and attention is wasted.
   - An idea with no builder in it has no champion. People who join it have nobody to lead, and the team stalls.

9. **Credit and fairness.**
   - Who owns an idea the writer won't build? Who gets credit when it wins?
   - Judges may ask whether a team simply took someone else's idea.
   - Decide this upfront and print it on the board.

10. **Low-quality ideas.**
    - With no cost to posting, vague ideas ("AI for health") will appear. They need clarification, which is slow.

**Fix:** keep the idea, but separate it.
- **Main board:** a project needs a named owner who will be on the team, or at least a named sponsor who commits to mentoring or building with it.
- **"Wishlist" section:** for ideas from people who won't build. Label it as a request for a champion, cap it at about 10 to 15 sheets, and have an organizer close any that don't find an owner by team-lock.
- **Credit rule:** the building team owns the result. Idea contributors are acknowledged by name.

## 🟡 Smaller risks

11. **You still need a plenary.** Kickoff has to cover rules, schedule, judging and sponsors. Add 5 to 10 minutes explaining how the board works.
12. **Judges and sponsors lose context.** A pitch round told them what was being built. Replace it with a one-line project sheet that teams fill in at team-lock.
13. **Accessibility.** Check board height, readability from a wheelchair, and low-vision users. Offer a digital list as an equal alternative.
14. **Physical fragility.** Markers dry out, sheets fall off, and someone erases the wrong thing. Take a photo or backup of the board on a schedule.
15. **Coffee area noise and spills.** Move the board a few steps away from the drinks and keep the path clear.

## ✅ Recommended shape

1. **Before the event:** collect ideas in a form with the template above, and print them as sheets.
2. **Kickoff (10 minutes):** explain the board and the rules. Hold an optional 60-second pitch for owners, capped at 15 to 20 slots, so people can opt in without being forced.
3. **Gallery walk (45 to 60 minutes):** multiple boards, dots or "I'm in" stickers, and organizers circulating to match skills.
4. **Hard team-lock:** each team posts its name, members and one-line project.
5. **Wishlist:** closed at team-lock.

## Questions for you

1. How long is the hack (one day, a weekend)? That sets how much time team formation can cost.
2. Are ideas collected in advance, or do people arrive with none?
3. What is the team size cap?
4. Is there a venue layout where you can place 3 to 4 boards away from the coffee?
5. Do sponsors or judges need a project overview before demos?
