# PortQuay Conf remote plan: 14 March, Bristol

I followed the `hybrid-event-design` skill. Hybrid stays settled, and I don't re-argue the cost.

The skill's interview is one question at a time, and I couldn't ask them here. So this plan runs on defaults, with the assumptions marked. Your answers to the four questions in the last section can change the plan.

## 1. Do this first

1. Write down what the remote audience was promised, in one sentence, and say who it was promised to. Examples are a paid virtual ticket, a sponsor deliverable that names the stream, an access commitment, or nothing written down. Every choice below is measured against that sentence.
2. Choose the streamed room before the grid is published. Removing a session from the remote programme after publication is a public retraction.
3. Ask your production lead for a quote on one streamed room that includes a return path. Give them the requirement below and let them name the kit.

## 2. The promise

Draft sentence, assuming nothing was sold or written down: "Remote attendees can watch one room live all day and get questions put to the speaker by a named person."

If a paid virtual ticket or a sponsor line exists, replace the sentence. That moves the eligibility and staffing rungs up (see the last section).

## 3. Eligibility: one designated streamed room

- Make one room the remote room for the whole day. Pick the room that holds the opening and the closing, plus one full track.
- Add a column to the published grid, not a separate remote schedule. For each session it holds three entries:
  - eligible or not
  - the return path
  - the named remote-side person, or an explicit "closed"
- Mark every session in the other room "closed". That marks it as a deliberate choice, not an unchecked row.
- Don't stream both rooms with this budget. Two streamed rooms with live Q&A is a staffing problem before it is a technical one.
- ⚠️ Trade-off: a Go and Rust two-track day means remote gets one language's talks.
  - If tracks split by language, choose the room on schedule coherence, not cabling.
  - If a promise exists, switch to a curated programme that mixes both languages. That needs both rooms fed and costs more.
- Don't offer "recordings published afterwards" as the remote programme. That is a recorded event, not a hybrid. Post-event publishing is separate work.
- Consent comes before marking. For each speaker in the streamed room, confirm their consent covers a live feed, not only a recording.
  - Decide whether in-room Q&A questioners are on camera, audio only, or off the feed.
  - Put a visible "this session is live" sign where people see it before they speak.
  - Name who can cut the feed if a speaker withdraws on the day.
  - Once the feed is live you can't recall it.

## 4. Parity: live relay by a named person

- Remote questions go into one channel, and one named person reads them aloud during Q&A. This is the cheapest rung where the speaker answers a remote question live.
- Minute by minute:
  1. Before the session, the relay tells the speaker that remote is watching and that remote questions come through them.
  2. During the talk, the relay selects the best questions. Reading everything aloud is dictation, not relay.
  3. At Q&A, the relay says who is asking and from where, then reads the question.
  4. After the answer, the relay confirms in the channel that the question was asked.
- Moderator rule: remote takes the first question of every Q&A. A sequence rule survives a short Q&A, and a proportion doesn't.
- Put that rule on the cue sheet row, not only in a briefing.
- Publish one line where remote attendees look: which room is streamed, how questions work, and what happens in the closed room.
- Fallback: any streamed session with no relay person available drops to feed-only. Close its question channel and pin a line saying so. An open channel nobody reads is worse than no channel.
- Don't put remote voices or faces into the room. It's the most expensive rung and needs consent and a return audio path. Revisit it only if the promise is about participation.

## 5. Staffing: an advocate in the streamed room, plus a watcher if you can

- Advocate: one named person in the streamed room for every session. They relay questions and tell the speaker remote is there. Name them per session, not as a function.
- Don't make the room lead the advocate. The room lead owns the clock and cuts Q&A, and that job collides with relaying a question at the same moment. The remote side always loses.
- Watcher: one person in no room, watching the outgoing feed as a remote attendee sees it.
  - An advocate beside the stage is the last person to notice the stream died, so the watcher catches what the advocate can't.
  - If you can find a second person, add the watcher. If not, the advocate alone is the default. Say plainly that a dead feed will be caught later in that case.
- Give remote a named live-chat contact. Don't point them at an inbox that someone checks between sessions.
- I haven't set a staffing ratio, because none has been published. Your ceiling is how many people can work the remote side without also covering the room.

## 6. The cue track: one extra column in the run of show

Hand this to your run-of-show owner as an addition. Don't author a second document.

- The new column holds three things per row:
  - what the stream shows: the stage, the slides, or a holding card
  - who owns that, by name
  - what the remote audience is told
- The room's transition and the stream's transition are the same row.
- Every gap gets a row, with at least a holding card showing the next talk title and start time.
  - Add a line posted in the remote channel at the start of each gap.
  - A remote attendee can't tell a break from a dead feed, and a holding card removes that ambiguity.
- Flag every row where the room is told something out loud, such as a delay, a room change, or a swapped speaker. At the briefing, walk the flagged rows and check each has a stream entry.
- Disruption rules. These are defaults from other hybrid conferences:
  - Keep the room going and fix a dead feed in parallel.
  - The call to give up on a feed sits in the technical chain, escalated to a senior organizer, not left to a volunteer.
  - When something breaks, say what broke, what still works, and where to watch.
- Two points have no documented answer. Decide them in writing before the day, name who decided them, and don't present them as inherited practice:
  1. If the remote-side person and the room lead need the same technician at once, who gets them?
  2. If a session is moved to fix an in-room problem, does the remote programme move with it?

## 7. What to tell the budget owner

- The feed survives every tier.
- The return path and the people watching it are what a lean tier cuts first. Protect those first.
- The staffing cost is the advocate for the streamed room, plus the watcher if you add one.
- I won't split the £6,000 into figures, because I have no quote. Whether it covers one streamed room with a return path is the production lead's answer. Don't spend any of it on a second room's kit.
- ❌ Don't put the money into the highest-rung options (both rooms, remote voices on stage) while delivering the lowest parity. That is the failure the skill names.

## 8. Success measures

These are metrics I'm suggesting, not industry standards. Pick two or three, decide what change each would trigger, and record them before the event:

- Remote questions that reached the stage, against remote questions asked.
- Announcements made in the room that never reached the remote channel.
- Time from a feed problem starting to the remote audience being told.

Completeness gates to check before the first session:

- Every eligible session has a named remote-side person, or its return path is explicitly closed.
- Every cue row that changes what the room sees also says what the stream shows.
- The disruption order says in writing what happens when a remote-side failure and an in-room one compete.

## 9. Questions that would change this plan

1. Was the remote audience sold a ticket, or named in a sponsor deliverable? If yes, move to a curated programme, add a host on the feed for the gaps, and consider remote voices in the room if the promise is participation.
2. Can both rooms physically carry a feed, and are they already wired? If yes, the "stream everything" rung gets cheaper.
3. Is the remote audience mostly in UK time, or spread across time zones? A wide spread makes the live relay matter less, and the written return path better.
4. When is the last date a session can join or leave the remote programme? Settle consent before that date.

If this is a recurring event, record per edition what was promised, how many remote questions reached the stage, and every time the remote side was left unattended. Start the next edition from that record.
