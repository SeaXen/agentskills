# Fabricoil Conf: deploying two volunteers on the remote side

**Next action:** don't put both volunteers in the two busiest rooms. Put one volunteer in one room as the remote advocate, and make the other the event-wide stream watcher. Run the other two rooms as feed only, and say so publicly. Q1 below can flip this.

I took the format decision as closed and didn't revisit it. I haven't seen your grid or the promise made to remote attendees, so the one-sentence promise below is a placeholder.

## 1. The promise (Q1 decides the layout)

I assumed nothing was written down beyond "all three rooms are streamed". Confirm or correct this one sentence:

> "Remote attendees get a live stream of all three rooms. Live questions are taken in one named room."

If the promise is different, the layout changes (see "What flips this" at the end).

## 2. Eligibility: feed vs. return path

- All three rooms carry the feed. The feed is the cheap part, and the production sibling builds it.
- Eligibility is not the same as return path. A room can be streamed with its return path closed.
- Mark the grid with a column (never a second document). Per session it holds eligible or not, the return path, and a named person or an explicit "closed".
- The advocate's room is your "remote room". Pick it for how well its sessions hold together as a remote day, ideally the room with the keynote or plenary.
- Don't pick it for in-room crowd size. In-room busyness says nothing about where remote attendees will ask questions.

## 3. Parity depth (what remote gets, per room)

| Room | Return path | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Remote room (advocate present) | **Live relay by a named person** | The advocate briefs the speaker that remote is watching. They pick questions, name the asker and their city, read it aloud, then confirm in the channel that it was asked. |
| Other two rooms | **Feed only**, closed | A pinned line says what the feed carries and that questions aren't taken live here. Add where recordings will appear, if they will. |

- Do not leave a question channel open in the two feed-only rooms. A channel nobody reads invites a question and drops it in front of the person who asked. That is the worst remote experience.
- An asynchronous written path is allowed in those rooms only if someone other than your two volunteers commits to a named answerer, a deadline and a place the answer appears. Otherwise leave it closed.
- Optional: a volunteer may float to a second room for a specific session. They must be named on that row, and its Q&A can't overlap the first room's. Every other session stays "closed".

## 4. Staffing: who goes where

**Volunteer A is the event-wide watcher.** They sit in no room and watch all three outgoing feeds as a remote attendee receives them.
- They notice a dead, silent or frozen feed. An advocate beside a stage is the last person to notice the stream died.
- They own the remote channel and are its named live-chat contact. They post every gap line and every room announcement, such as delays, room changes and swapped speakers.
- They tell remote attendees what broke, what still works and where to watch.

**Volunteer B is the advocate in the remote room.** They do the relay mechanic in section 3 for each session in that room.

**The AV contractor** stays on equipment and the technical chain. Don't count them as a remote advocate or a watcher. Ask them to commit to a feed-down escalation path, but that is their call.

Why this beats your instinct (an advocate in each of two rooms):
- It leaves nobody watching the output, and the third room is unwatched too.
- The documented failures happen when staff look only at the room. Remote attendees aren't told about a slip, and remote check-in lags.
- A fixed pair of advocates guards two rooms' Q&A. The watcher guards all three rooms' attention.

What you give up: live remote Q&A in two of the three rooms. That is the real price of having exactly two people. No staffing ratio is published, so I'm not inventing one. A full setup (a watcher plus an advocate per room) would need four people.

Don't let a room lead double as the remote advocate. That role owns the room's clock, and it competes with relaying a question at exactly the moment both come due.

Use names per session on the grid, not functions. Any session with no name gets "closed".

## 5. The cue track (add a column, no new document)

Add one column to the run of show, with three things per row:
- what the stream shows (stage, slides or holding card),
- who owns it, by name,
- what remote is told.

- **Gaps.** Every gap in every room gets a holding card with the next talk's title and start time. Volunteer A posts the same line in the channel. Without it, remote can't tell a break from a failure.
- **Spoken announcements.** Flag every row where the room is told something out loud. At the briefing, walk those rows and confirm each has a stream entry.
- **Same row.** The room's transition and the stream's transition live on the same row.

## 6. Disruption rules to decide in writing before the day

Name who decided each rule. I suggest you decide them, as the organizer.

- **Dead feed:** the room keeps going and the feed is fixed in parallel. Pausing is a stated exception.
- **Giving up on a feed:** the authority sits in the technical chain, with a senior organizer as the escalation point. A volunteer is never left alone with that call.
- **Same technician needed by the watcher and a room lead:** no published rule exists, so you decide. I'd pick one order in advance (for example, room first) and write it down.
- **A session moved to fix an in-room problem:** does the remote programme follow it? No rule exists, so decide this too.

## 7. Budget consequence

- The feed survives every tier.
- Lean tiers cut the return path and the people watching it first. You've already taken that cut: live relay in one room, feed only in two.
- Report this split to the budget owner.

## Gates and measures (record before the event)

Completeness gates:
- Every session has a named remote person or a "closed" return path.
- Every cue row that changes what the room sees also says what the stream shows.
- The disruption order says, in writing, what happens when a remote-side failure and an in-room one compete.

Pick two or three self-set metrics (these are not industry standards):
- remote questions that reached a stage, against questions asked,
- room announcements that never reached the remote channel,
- time from a feed problem starting to remote being told.

## What flips this (Q1, Q3, Q7)

1. **A paid virtual ticket, or a sponsor line naming remote interaction.** Then live Q&A in two rooms is owed. Use two advocates, but get someone else to own the watcher role in writing (the contractor, an organizer, or a third body for the gaps). Otherwise the feed-down failure goes unnoticed.
2. **A remote audience mostly in a distant time zone.** Then the remote audience is small during part of the day. Put the advocate on the sessions they'll be awake for.
3. **A recurring event.** Write the advocate briefing and the cue template once, and keep them for the next edition.

Answer Q1 first, because it decides which layout you use.
