**No. Don't open a call you've already decided not to run.** There are two honest ways to get the slate you want.

## Why the quiet version fails

- **The site made a promise.** "Open to any early-stage company" is a published route. The skill treats a published route as a promise, not a starting position. Filling the slate from your own list after saying that is the curated rung run in secret. The skill calls that "the single most contestable thing" in contest design, and it deletes the curated rung from the menu once a public route exists.
- **"The bar was high this year" is a false statement.** There would be no bar, because there would be no review. It is also the line most likely to be disproved.
  - Your six all come from your network, and founders talk.
  - A rejected applicant will see who pitched and ask which criteria they lost on. You would have none to show them.
  - Every statement you make afterwards depends on that first one.
- **Applicants hand you custody.** Traction facts and team history are material about private companies. Collecting it for a decision already made takes on that obligation for nothing. A deck cannot be un-circulated.
- **There is no recovery path.** Published criteria, weights and a named reviewer are what let you answer a challenge. A fake review has none of them.

## What you can do instead

Your six companies are not the problem. Choose one of these.

1. **Run the call for real, and let your six apply.**
   - Publish the route as one sentence: who may apply, how the slate is chosen, who chooses, and what an accepted company commits to.
   - Publish the criteria and their weights in the call. "Undisclosed weights score a competition nobody entered."
   - Judge your six against the same criteria as everyone else.
   - If you or your team know them, say so and recuse yourselves from scoring those applications.
   - If your six are good, they should survive a single review pass. If one doesn't, you learned something.
2. **Change the route publicly, now.** If you want reserved slots, rewrite the page so it matches what you will do. For example, say that some slots are by invitation and the rest by open application. Say it before anyone applies, and publish a changelog line so the edit isn't silent. This costs some reputation, but it is a true statement.
3. **Narrow what "open" means.** Perhaps "any early-stage company" is wider than you meant. If so, publish a tighter stage definition, such as live product plus first customers. Do it before the call closes, and tell people who already applied.

Option 1 is the default. It is also the only route that gives you a read on whether the funnel found anyone you didn't know. The skill's measurement signal is "applications from companies nobody on the team already knew."

## The timeline check

An accepted company needs at least 4-6 weeks' notice to arrange travel or time off (DevOpsDays organizing guide). Work backwards from the stage date. If the notification date would land inside that floor, the open call no longer works. Then the honest route is option 2: say plainly that the slate is by invitation.

## Before you publish anything

Three questions decide the rest of the design:

1. Who judges? Is anyone an active investor, and was anyone invited because of who would pitch?
2. What does the winner receive, and has each party committed to it in writing?
3. Does any award carry capital, a convertible instrument, or equity?

The third question matters most. If any award carries capital, it goes to counsel before it is announced. The skill states no thresholds.
