# What you should have written down

The brief's one line covered the wrong interest. "Recuse yourself from scoring any company you are invested in" handles an interest in the result. A fund partner's interest is in access, which means an early, free look at companies he would not otherwise have seen. Recusal removes the vote and leaves that advantage intact.

Nobody recused, which may simply mean nobody held a position. The partner's two calls with the second-place company broke no rule, because no rule covered them. That is why the founder's email reads as a grievance. The judges' interest was never disclosed to the pitchers, so they had no chance to enter the contest under it.

## What the brief and the call should have said

The skill's default for an investor panel is recusal plus disclosure to the pitching companies, before they pitch. A fuller brief, delivered in writing before judges see any application, would have asked three things:

1. **Positions.** Does the judge or their fund hold a position in any applicant, or in anything that competes with one? The competing case matters because the interest can run backwards: a judge may want a rival applicant to look ordinary.
2. **Permission.** Do the judge's own firm's rules allow them to sit on the panel and see this material? That is for the judge and their institution to answer, not for you to interpret. Ask in writing before confirming the invitation.
3. **Intent.** Does the judge plan to approach companies from this stage, and will they accept a contact protocol if you publish one?

Question 3 is the one organizers skip. It is the only answer you can act on before the event instead of after it.

The brief should also have told judges: "You are judging for the event today, not sourcing for your fund." Pair that with an offer, not a ban. Tell them that if they want to talk to a company afterwards, you will make the introduction. A route is easier to follow than a prohibition, and a prohibition tends to get ignored.

For the two engineering directors, the same brief should ask about any pilot, procurement or vendor relationship with an applicant. A customer judge also gains early access to companies, even if the access is different.

## What should have been published to the pitching companies

- **A disclosure slide before the block.** It lists each judge's declared interests and their role (fund partner or customer).
- **A statement in the call itself, before applications closed.** It names who judges and what the judges may and may not do. A condition disclosed up front is a term the applicant entered under. The same facts disclosed after the result are a grievance. Only the timing changes.

## What I recommend for March

Add the contact protocol, the fourth rung of the ladder. It is the most expensive rung, so this is your decision. Its trigger is a recurring contest whose published rules promise a process, and March makes yours one. It has four published elements:

1. **Stand-off.** Judges do not approach pitching companies directly during a stated window after the block. You set the window as a date. The skill deliberately states no length for it.
2. **Route.** A named organizer makes the introduction on request, from either side.
3. **Founder control.** A company can decline an introduction with no effect on its result. The result is already final when the window opens.
4. **Carve-out.** A relationship that predates the contest is handled by the disclosure above, not by the protocol.

The protocol's purpose is not to stop investment. It makes sure the first conversation happens after the result, not around it. It is also hard to unwind, because withdrawing it next year reads as a retreat. Decide knowing that.

## Other items to write down before the call opens

The same design pass requires these, and the post-mortem suggests they may also have been missing:

- **The award list.** For each item, name the committing party, whether it is in writing, and the delivery date. If any award carries capital or equity, send it to counsel before announcing it. Contest-law classification also goes to counsel.
- **Sponsor influence.** Check whether any signed agreement gives a sponsor a say in who pitches or wins. A stage slot is a speaking slot and is not for sale.
- **Recording and press.** Say whether the room is recorded, streamed or open to press, and tell applicants before they apply. This cannot be fixed afterwards.
- **Criteria and weights.** Publish them in the call. Undisclosed weights score a competition nobody entered.
- **Pass check.** Before the call goes public, six things should exist in writing with no blanks: the published route, the criteria, the format, the conflict rule (including what it does not cover), the award list with committing parties, and the three gate answers.

## What to do now

1. **Answer the founder honestly.** Say that judges had access to all the pitching companies. Say the brief only addressed scoring, and that March will publish the disclosures and a contact route. Don't claim a rule was broken, because none was.
2. **Ask every judge the three questions retroactively.** Record which interests existed at the time. Then compare "declared at the time" against "surfaced later". That is the skill's own recommended signal.
3. **Offer the partner and the second-place company nothing to undo.** The calls happened after the result and may be harmless. Their cost to you is that the founders could not see that. Decide whether to tell the other pitching companies what happened.

## Open questions I could not answer from here

- Was the second-place company's founder told about those calls, or only the one who emailed you?
- Did the call say anything about how judges would use what they saw? A published route is a promise, so reread it.
- Were any judges or audience members invited because of who would pitch? If so, a screening call before the slate is fixed may be worth its cost in March.
