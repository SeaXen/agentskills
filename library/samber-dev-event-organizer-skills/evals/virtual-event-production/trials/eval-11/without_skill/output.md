**Do this first (about 30 minutes):** read the platform contract and your registration form. Check three things: who controls the attendee data, whether the form told people their details may go to sponsors, and the platform's concurrent-breakout limit. The sponsor list sale is your biggest risk. Fix that before anything else.

## 1. Attendee list sale (highest risk)

Selling 3,000 people's contact details only works if registrants agreed to it. Check what the form said. This isn't legal advice, so have counsel confirm, especially if any attendees are in the EU, UK, or a US state with a privacy law.

1. **Pull the registration wording and privacy notice.** Did they say "shared with sponsors"? Did they name sponsors or categories?
2. **If consent was clear:** deliver only the fields the notice promised, such as name, company, title, and email.
3. **If it was vague or silent:** don't hand over the full list.
   - Add an unticked opt-in checkbox now, naming the three sponsors.
   - Deliver only the people who ticked it, plus anyone who scans, visits, or requests something at that sponsor's booth.
   - Tell the sponsor early. Reframe the product as "consented leads and booth leads", and offer a make-good if the list shrinks.
4. **Put sponsor terms in writing:**
   - one-time use for follow-up
   - no resale and no sharing
   - honor unsubscribes
   - delete on request
   - named contact for complaints
5. **Check the platform contract.** Find out who owns the data, whether exports are allowed, and whether it has its own data terms that conflict with your sponsor deal.

Expect an opted-in list to be a fraction of 3,000. Budget for that now rather than discovering it at delivery.

## 2. Work within the platform you didn't choose

1. **Get the facts in writing:** maximum concurrent attendees, breakout room count and size limits, camera controls, recording controls, and the support SLA.
2. **Get a named customer-success contact and at least two rehearsals.** One should be a load test with 100+ people in breakouts.
3. **Pick a fallback for each critical feature.** If breakouts fail, switch to chat-based topic lounges and extend session breaks. Decide the trigger in advance.
4. **Ask before using any outside tool.** The contract may restrict it. Never move attendee data into one without checking.
5. **Run a tech-check desk** (chat or phone) open from 30 minutes before the first session.

## 3. Camera-on breakouts that work

**Camera-on should be an invitation, not a gate.** Forcing it excludes people on bad bandwidth, in shared spaces, or with safety or accessibility needs. Set it up like this:

1. **Two lanes.** Offer camera-on "meet someone" rooms and a camera-optional "text and voice" lounge. Aim for most people in the first lane through framing, not force.
2. **Small groups.** Use 3 or 4 people. Pairs feel like a blind date, and groups of 6 or more go quiet.
3. **Match on something real.** Ask 2 questions at registration, such as role and one topic, and seed rooms from the answers. Include an "assign me randomly" option.
4. **Give each room a prompt on screen.** Example: "What are you shipping next quarter that scares you?" Rotate prompts per break.
5. **Short and timed:** 2 minutes to join, 10 to talk, 3 to return. Show a countdown and auto-return.
6. **Use hosts.** Seed 10 to 20 staff or customer volunteers as roaming room starters. Brief them on the code of conduct.
7. **Safety:**
   - Publish a short code of conduct before the event.
   - Add a visible report or leave button.
   - Keep a moderator channel monitored live.
   - Do not record breakouts. Say so out loud.
   - State clearly that cameras are never recorded in these rooms.
8. **Don't schedule breakouts back to back.** Alternate talk, breakout, talk so energy stays up. Allow at least 15 minutes between sessions.
9. **Let people exchange details** (profile card or opt-in "share my contact" button) so connections survive past the room.

## 4. Make three virtual booths worth the money

Booths are quiet by default. Traffic has to be scheduled.

1. **Give each sponsor a fixed visit window.** Make booths part of the agenda rather than a side tab.
2. **Add a sponsor session slot** in the program, labeled as sponsored.
3. **Run a "booth passport":** visit all three and complete one action each to enter a prize draw. Prizes must not require handing over contact data without clear consent.
4. **Give each sponsor a live person and a live demo schedule.** An empty booth with a PDF is wasted money.
5. **Give sponsors a lead report with context:** who visited, what they clicked, and notes from conversations. Count qualified conversations, not scans.
6. **Treat sponsors equally.** Don't give the list-buying sponsor extra breakout or stage access that the others didn't pay for.

## 5. Timeline

Your event date wasn't given, so these are counted back from the start. Fill in dates once you pick them.

1. **T-8 weeks:** contract review, data-sharing decision, platform limits confirmed.
2. **T-6 weeks:** registration form and consent wording fixed, matching questions added, code of conduct drafted.
3. **T-4 weeks:** sponsor terms signed, booth schedule agreed, breakout hosts recruited.
4. **T-2 weeks:** load-test rehearsal, run-of-show locked, fallback triggers agreed.
5. **T-1 week:** attendee comms (how breakouts work, how to opt out of camera, how data is shared).
6. **Event day:** tech desk, moderators on, live metrics watched.
7. **T+24 to 48 hours:** sponsor leads delivered, attendee survey sent, recordings published.

## 6. What to measure

- **Real:** share of attendees who joined a breakout, average time in breakouts, connections shared, post-event "I met someone useful" score, and booth conversations per sponsor.
- **Track but don't celebrate:** registrations, raw booth clicks, and total session views.
- **Decisive for next year:** cost per qualified sponsor lead, and whether the same sponsors renew.
