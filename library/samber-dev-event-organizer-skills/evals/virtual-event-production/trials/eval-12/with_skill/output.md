# Tessellate Research: three online talks, autumn 2026

ℹ️ **Short answer:** yes, you're missing something. The Discord is free to start, but watching it is a standing job, and on the day you're the only person there. I'd run the talks as a one-way feed with the Discord closed during sessions, unless you get one more named person.

I read the virtual-event-production skill and its three references and applied them. I haven't interviewed you yet, so every assumption is marked and the plan flags which answers would change it.

## 1. Interaction posture (one line)

**A feed people watch, with a conversation space that opens only if someone is on duty.**

Assumptions behind it:
- Three talks, one at a time, so one session at once.
- The speakers are remote, and you and the audience are too.
- Nothing is paid and no sponsor deliverable names the stream.
- It's a recurring series, so what you build now gets reused twice.

## 2. What you're missing in the Discord plan

**An open space needs a named responder.**
- Setup takes an hour. Watching it lasts as long as the space is open.
- "Open to anyone" plus an open chat is the case where harassment risk goes up, not down. The conduct duty is the same as in a physical room.
- Nobody is on duty while you introduce the speaker and sit in the talk. That leaves a conduct duty with no responder.
- Having the speaker moderate their own chat isn't an option either. They'd be doing two jobs badly, and they can't enforce conduct against someone in their own audience.

**Nobody would notice a dead feed.**
- Virtual events fail as silence. Everyone assumes the problem is on their end.
- You'd be inside the talk, hearing the source rather than what the audience gets.
- A stream with dead audio from slide 2 goes unnoticed by everyone in the production path.

**Nobody relays questions.** The speaker can't watch chat and present. Someone has to select questions and pass them on.

**Discord has its own costs.**
- Everyone needs a Discord account, which is a barrier for a public audience.
- Check whether the free tier keeps history.
- Check whether you can export the member list before you close the server.

**Several things aren't about Discord at all:**
- recording consent
- captions
- speaker connections
- time zones
- the fallback plan

They're covered below.

## 3. Platform posture (Menu 1)

**Recommended (solo): single one-way feed.**
- Stream to a public video platform with comments closed and no accounts.
- This is the most robust option on a phone and has the lowest compliance exposure. Nothing needs moderating.
- It suits an audience that wants the talks rather than the company.

**Upgrade (needs one more person): one-way feed plus a Discord that is open only during the session window.**
- Open it about 15 minutes before the talk and close it about 30 minutes after.
- Outside those windows, announcements only. A locked-by-default server with a named moderator beats an open one with nobody on duty.

**Rejected:**
- a managed virtual-venue platform (nothing here needs booths, tracks or a directory)
- a broadcast tool with a question queue (dominated by the feed plus chat space)
- a second live platform carrying the same sessions in parallel (deleted: it halves the audience of every question)

**Questions to put in writing before you commit to the video platform:**
1. Can a viewer watch without an account?
2. Can the stream restart mid-session without changing its address?
3. What is the concurrent-viewer ceiling on your plan?
4. What does it do for viewers on a poor connection? Is there an audio-only fallback?
5. What captioning does it provide? Can you feed in an external captioner?
6. Who owns and can delete the recording?
7. Does background music in a speaker's demo risk a takedown? This is a named risk, and a takedown mid-session has no appeal that lands in time.

Reversibility is easy here. A public feed can be abandoned the week before at the cost of an announcement. You aren't signing anything.

## 4. Speaker readiness (Menu 2)

**Default: a written home-setup note**, sent with the join link the day the speaker accepts. It covers:
- a wired connection
- wired headphones
- closing background sync
- camera at eye level
- light in front of the face
- sharing one window
- no background music
- a recorded fallback of any demo

Because this is a series, the note gets written once and reused for all three.

**Move a speaker up to an individual tech check plus a local backup recording if any of these is true:**
- they've never presented on this platform
- their connection is unknown or known-bad
- their talk has a live demo
- the session can't be re-run

For a one-person crew, that individual slot is worth doing for the first talk. Doing the check this way also covers your own rehearsal.

**Rules for any check:**
- Run it on the exact platform the event uses, with the real deck, via the same join link as on the day.
- A check on a different tool is deleted from the menu. It tests nothing that matters.
- Put the speaker's local time in the first line of your message.

**Pre-recorded talk with the speaker live in chat.** Use this only for a speaker whose slot lands in the middle of their night, or one who can't hold a live slot.

**A local backup recording creates a file.** Get it covered by the recording consent record before the file exists. A recording made as insurance isn't automatically one anyone agreed to publish.

## 5. Moderation staffing (Menu 3)

- **Feed only:** skip this menu. There's nothing to moderate.
- **Feed plus Discord:** you need at least one named moderator who reads chat, relays questions and holds conduct. A name, not a function. Ideally a second named person is a technical watcher: someone who watches the output from a second device, as an audience member would. The same colleague can do both only if they aren't also speaking.

**Decision for you:** can one of the other four lab members be a moderator (and ideally a watcher) for three evenings? If yes, the Discord is viable. If no, ship the feed only.

**If you are speaking or co-presenting:** you can't also introduce, host and watch the feed. Someone else has to be the "voice on the feed" when something breaks.

## 6. Failure plan (Menu 4)

1. **Publish one fallback sentence before each talk**, where the audience already looks. For example: "If the stream drops, updates will be on [schedule page / lab site] — it's on our side, not yours." The URL must live outside the platform that might fail.
2. **Rehearse one scenario aloud:** the speaker's connection drops mid-talk. Agree the moderator's exact opening words, and who declares the failure. Without a second person this rung isn't real, because nobody is free to say it live.
3. **Skip** a warm second stream and a full outage drill. Nothing here (no paid ticket, no sponsor, no unrepeatable session) promotes them. Revisit if one of the talks is unrepeatable or a previous edition's feed dies.

Also write down what you'd do for a no-show speaker, dead audio, or a platform incident. Read the list aloud once with whoever would act on it. A contingency document nobody has read aloud is deleted from the menu.

## 7. Rehearsal

**Rehearse once on the real platform, before talk 1.** Ideally this is the first speaker's tech check, with the moderator present. Run the dropped-connection scenario in that same slot. Reuse the rehearsed crew and note for talks 2 and 3.

## 8. Other things to settle

- **Captions.** Decide the standard first (the accessibility bar is yours to set), then check what the platform can and cannot deliver, and report the gap.
- **Time zones.** "Open to anyone" means someone may be presenting or watching at 3am. Ask each speaker where they are before fixing the date. A bad slot gets moved or pre-recorded.
- **Recording and publication consent.** Get it per speaker, in writing, before the session. Decide where the record lives.
- **Public safety.** Don't show attendee faces anywhere (no open Q&A video) unless attendee consent was collected for it.
- **Announce the plan.** State on the event page whether there's live chat, how to ask questions, and where the recording will appear.

## 9. Budget consequence

- The feed survives every tier.
- The Discord, moderation and tech-check time go first.
- For a lab of five people, the real cost is hours, not money: roughly 1 setup session, 1 rehearsal, and 3 session blocks for each person on duty.

## 10. Measurement (set before talk 1; all self-set, not industry standards)

- **Speakers who joined a check before the day**, over speakers scheduled. This is the only leading indicator.
- **Time from a feed dropping to the audience being told something.** This is what the drill moves.
- **Sessions delivered without an audience-visible failure**, over sessions scheduled. A session with no audio counts as a failure.

Write down what change each would trigger. If you run the Discord, also track questions relayed against questions asked. It shows whether the moderation was real or nominal.

## Gates before the first talk

- ✅ Every open chat has a named moderator, or it's closed.
- ✅ Every speaker has joined the real platform at least once before the day.
- ✅ The fallback line is published, and the person who'll say it knows the sentence.

## Questions that change this plan (in order)

1. **Is anyone else in the lab available on the day?** This is the biggest lever. Yes means feed plus Discord. No means feed only.
2. **What does "I'm in the talk too" mean?** Are you presenting or co-presenting, or just attending? If you're presenting, you can't also host.
3. **Where are the speakers and the audience?** One time zone, or spread wide?
4. **Does any talk carry a live demo, or is any speaker new to this platform?** Those speakers get the individual check and local backup.
5. **Is anything paid or promised?** That means a paid ticket or a sponsor naming the stream. Neither is assumed.
6. **Is there a constraint on the platform?** For example employer or university procurement rules, or data-location rules.
7. **Do you already have a community chat space your audience lives in?** If so, use that rather than a new Discord.
