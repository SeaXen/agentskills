# Getting stuck participants unstuck: Marlowe Devdays hands-on sessions

**Do first (about 1 hour):** ask the instructor for her list of the places this workshop breaks, in the order they happen. Everything below builds on that list.

## Assumptions to confirm

- ℹ️ **The sessions are full hands-on.** You said "hands-on sessions", so I kept that commitment. Two cases reopen it:
  - If the exercise needs a live network service and the venue hasn't answered in writing about its network, the skill says to drop to demonstration plus published material.
  - If the exercise can be learned by reading, it doesn't need the room.
- ℹ️ **The exercise is one linear path at every seat.** This is Q6, which I couldn't ask. If it has distinct workstations or steps (for example build and deploy), see rung 3.
- ℹ️ **The solo-instructor rung is struck.** Someone will be stuck while she is mid-sentence, and with 25–30 people that is certain.
- ℹ️ **Your facts overturn the skill's default order.** The skill names "an instructor who already runs this exact workshop commercially" as one of the facts that re-ranks its defaults. Your annual cadence and one-person budget push the same way.

### 1. Structured peer pairing, scripted by the instructor (do first)

**Why it leads:**
- Its help capacity grows with the room. Fifteen pairs mean fifteen second sets of eyes, and this is the only option that scales past two answerers in a room of 30.
- It costs no budget.
- It carries over to every later edition unchanged.

**What to do:**
1. Assign roles: a driver on the keyboard and a navigator watching the screen.
2. Swap roles every 5–10 minutes.
3. Have the instructor pre-write "ask your partner first" prompts at the points where this workshop usually breaks.
4. Plan one trio for an odd headcount.
5. Check in with pairs on a fixed sweep. The failure to guard against is two stuck people, which is stable and sends no signal.

**Caveats:**
- The evidence is classroom pair-programming pedagogy, not conference workshops, and the learning-outcome results are mixed. Treat it as a structure that plausibly helps, not a proven technique.
- Plain pairing without roles is its own failure mode. The guidance says it "can often lead to one, or both, learners quickly losing focus".

**Her experience helps here.** She knows where pairs get stuck together, so she can turn that into the prompts and the sweep order.

### 2. One roaming troubleshooter, your extra hire (do second, before booking anyone)

- **Hire for skill in the material, not for logistics.** The source guidance calls this person a "backup speaker". Someone who can't debug a failed step gives you headcount, not coverage.
- **Their job is to spot quiet pairs.** The trigger is people falling behind who haven't raised a hand.
- **Keep the first sweep on the instructor's break list.** That way the roamer starts with the known failure points, not a blank page.
- **Brief them on keyboards.** Decide in advance whether a helper may take a participant's keyboard. Without a rule, they will.
- **Candidates to ask the instructor about:**
  - someone from her own commercial team;
  - a past participant who finished the workshop cleanly.

  A person she has already run this with is worth more than a stranger.
- **Year 2.** If the hire works, keep them and brief them again. This is the lever that makes later editions cheaper.

### 3. Stationed helpers per work area (conditional, probably unused)

- It has the lowest latency and the highest effort.
- It only earns a place if the exercise has distinct steps that people reach in different order.
- With one extra person you can't post a helper at every step anyway. If the exercise does split, put the one person at the step the instructor says breaks most, and revisit.
- It stays out of the plan until Q6 says otherwise.

## Sizing

**One instructor plus one roaming troubleshooter, with structured pairs, for a room of 25–30.**

⚠️ This figure is my judgment, sized from "one linear path, one extra person who knows the material". It is not an external standard. There is no valid facilitator-to-participant ratio to cite.

The three capacity ceilings all stay open. The coverage ceiling is the one this plan moves: pairing halves the number of independent screens to cover. The room and exercise-throughput ceilings depend on your room's seats and power, and on any limited kit or licences, and you haven't given me those.

## Order of work (5 months, about 3 hours of your time up front)

1. **This week, about 1 hour:** get the instructor's ordered break list.
2. **Within 2 weeks, about 1 hour:** ask her who she'd hire as the roamer. Offer the decision to her, since she knows who can debug it.
3. **By month 2:** she writes the pairing prompts and the driver/navigator swap points.
4. **Month 3:** the roamer reads the break list and shadows her once if possible. Brief them on the keyboard rule.
5. **Rehearsal:** run the pairing script with a small group before the day. Pairing and sweep timing are the parts she hasn't run in this form.

## What to measure

- **Help latency:** how long a raised hand waits. Set your own target; the skill gives no number.
- **Started versus finished:** how many reached the last exercise. A wide gap with a full room points at coverage.
- **Revision trigger, written down now:** if more than a handful of pairs go quiet unnoticed, move the roamer to a fixed route at the break points. If the exercise turns out to have distinct steps, revisit rung 3.
