# Partner scoring and outreach

## Candidate-mapping sources

Work outward from the closest calendar to the broadest, and treat every named tool as an example of its type, not a dependency - a niche outside a calendar's coverage needs a different or manual source.

1. **Your own event category's aggregator** - most event families list every scheduled edition centrally (DevOpsDays' homepage lists all its city events; the practice of checking it before picking dates is named "load balancing" in their organizing guide). Sibling events in your own family are the most pre-qualified partners possible: same audience by construction.
2. **Ecosystem season calendars** - a membership organization's shared calendar cross-lists every member event (MLH's season calendar lists each independently organized member hackathon). Being listed there _is_ the calendar cross-listing rung of the format menu. Note the confirmed gap: the calendar documents no reciprocal-promotion practice between the events it lists - the listing is passive, and any active swap on top of it is yours to broker.
3. **Cross-topic crowdsourced calendars** - community-maintained event datasets cover events outside your family and topic, which is exactly where your family's own homepage check goes blind. Two reusable examples, split by breadth: confs.tech (and its underlying open dataset) lists conferences only; dev.events spans hackathons, meetups, and conferences, so it reaches formats confs.tech never lists. Crowdsourced coverage skews toward events whose organizers know the tool - a cross-check, not a census.
4. **Local, non-category events** - an unrelated event in your city on adjacent dates competes for venues, travel budgets, and attention (DevOpsDays guide), but the same adjacency read positively is a co-location candidate: their traveling attendees are already in town.
5. **CFP listing platforms** - counting how many events already target your topic and window doubles as a saturation read and a candidate list.

## Per-criterion checks

Four screening criteria, adapted for organizer-to-organizer context:

- **Audience fit** - read the candidate's program, speaker roster, and topic tags: would your attendees genuinely want _both_ events? Then apply the complement/substitute test: if your attendees would go to theirs _instead of_ yours, it is a competitor to load-balance dates against, never a swap partner. Fit is the only criterion that can single-handedly kill a candidate.
- **Audience-size parity** - compare public attendance claims, newsletter subscriber counts where published, and social following. Parity within roughly the same order of magnitude (self-set heuristic) supports a symmetric swap; beyond that, see the parity heuristic below.
- **Brand and values alignment** - read their code of conduct (and whether past incidents were handled publicly and well), their pricing posture, their sponsor roster, and their public voice. You are attaching your event's name to theirs; misalignment here is the failure mode that outlives one edition.
- **Engagement quality** - open rates, social interaction, past swap results. Unverifiable from outside: never guess it during screening. Ask for real numbers during the deal conversation, and make sharing post-swap numbers a term of the agreement so the next edition's screen has data.

## The parity heuristic for asymmetric swaps

When audience sizes diverge, "one mention each" quietly transfers value to the smaller side, and the larger side knows it. Handle asymmetry openly:

- Balance in units, not kind: the smaller side offers more mentions, a richer placement (dedicated section vs. footer link), a longer or deeper discount window, or on-site visibility the larger side lacks.
- Name the imbalance in the outreach message itself - proposing an already-balanced deal signals you did the math and saves the awkward correction later.
- The floor case is no deal at all: a smaller event referencing a larger anchor's dates in its own positioning ("the day after [anchor] ends, same city") without any agreement. This is the event-world analog of permissionless co-marketing: it costs nothing and needs no consent, but it earns no reciprocity, and using the anchor's _name_ in your marketing beyond factual date references may need their sign-off - check before borrowing a trademark.

## Organizer-to-organizer outreach template

Write peer-to-peer, not vendor-to-prospect. Warm channel first - a shared organizer acquaintance or community space beats any template.

> Subject: [your event] × [their event] - audience swap?
>
> I organize [event], a [one-line description: format, size, city, audience]. [Specific overlap observation: "About a third of our speakers this year have also spoken at your event" / "Your attendees' stack is exactly who our workshops serve."] We'd like to propose [one concrete swap: "a newsletter mention swap in the six weeks before both editions" / "a 20% ticket code for each other's communities"]. [Clear ask: "Open to a 30-minute call next week?"]

Structure:

- One specific observed overlap, never a generic compliment.
- One concrete proposal, never "let's explore synergies."
- An ask answerable with yes/no.

Rule added for this audience: write as a fellow organizer, referencing their event's actual content. Organizers smell a mail-merge instantly, and the community is small enough that a spammy ask follows you.
