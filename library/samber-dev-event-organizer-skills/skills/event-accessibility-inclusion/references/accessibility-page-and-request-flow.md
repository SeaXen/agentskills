# The accessibility page, the request flow, and what to say on the day

## Why one page

Across the sourced events, the ones that publish accessibility content thinly scatter it across venue, logistics and registration pages; the one that publishes it well puts everything on a single page and republishes that page near-verbatim across many events (foundation template, snapshot 2025-04-21). That is evidence a well-built accessibility page is a reusable organizational asset, not something redrafted per edition. Build it once, then edit it each year against what the debrief recorded.

## Page structure

Use this order. It moves from what everyone needs to know, to what a specific person needs to ask for.

1. **Who this page is for**, in one paragraph, naming the groups the event intends to welcome and linking the code of conduct.
2. **Getting to and around the venue** - step-free routes, lifts, accessible bathrooms, what is _not_ accessible, and a map or routing tool if one exists.
3. **In the sessions** - reserved seating, captioning with its exact scope, interpretation and how to request it, hearing loops.
4. **Spaces** - quiet space (with its rationale and its rules), nursing room, all-gender restrooms.
5. **Food** - which dietary categories are covered and what happens if a supplier cannot cover one.
6. **Cost of attending** - the need-gated ticket route, childcare, any grant, each with its own eligibility and its own limits.
7. **Badges and consent** - pronoun options, interaction-consent stickers if used, and the photo and recording opt-out.
8. **Ask us** - the named owner, the address, the lead time, and what happens to what you tell us.
9. **Tell us what we got wrong** - a feedback route for after the event.

## Wording a provision honestly

State the scope inside the sentence. Two shapes, both drawn from sourced pages:

- **Do**: "Live captioning is provided in every session room on the main conference days. The tutorial and sprint days are not captioned. If you need captioning on those days, write to <named owner> by <date> and we will arrange it."
- **Don't**: "The conference is captioned."

The second sentence is not shorter, it is a different promise. A deaf attendee books travel on it and finds out on the tutorial day.

Same rule for a provision that only exists on request:

- **Do**: "We do not book sign-language interpreters by default. If you need one, tell us by <date> - we need <N> weeks to book, and we will confirm within <M> days either way."
- **Don't**: "Accommodations available on request."

The second version puts the whole burden of imagination on the person who has to ask, and it never states a lead time, which is the only thing that makes the request actionable.

When a provision does not exist, say so and name the nearest alternative if there is one. One sourced page states outright that no quiet space exists on campus and points to a public park five minutes away (FOSDEM 2026). That is more useful to a person deciding whether to attend than silence.

## Request-channel copy

Keep the field short and route it to a named person, not a role alias.

> **Do you need anything from us to attend?** Tell us here - for example a step-free route, reserved seating, captioning or interpretation, a quiet space, dietary needs, or childcare. <Named owner> reads these and will reply within <M> days. We keep what you tell us only until <date, e.g. two weeks after the event>, share it only with the organizers who need it to arrange what you asked for, and delete it after that.

Three things that sentence does, each load-bearing:

- **It gives examples.** Someone who has never seen an event provision an interpreter does not know it is askable.
- **It names a person and a turnaround.** A request with no stated reply time is indistinguishable from one that fell into a void, and the person who most needs an answer is the one least able to chase it.
- **It states retention up front.** People disclose a disability when they can see what happens to the disclosure. This is also the one element worth carrying up from the heaviest request-channel rung into the cheap one.

## The data-handling posture

Decide all five before the channel opens, and publish the first three:

1. **Retention** - a date, tied to the event, after which requests are deleted. Actually perform the deletion at the debrief.
2. **Access** - which organizers can read requests, and that they are shared only to arrange what was asked for.
3. **Turnaround** - how fast a request gets an answer, including a declined one.
4. **Where it lives** - an inbox you control empties more easily than a field inside a ticketing platform's registration records, where retention is that platform's choice. If you take the registration-field route anyway (it surfaces far more needs), know that this is the trade you made.
5. **Who decides a decline** - one named person, with the reason written down, so a decline is a decision rather than a silence.

Jurisdictional duties over health and disability data, and over what a public event must provide, are out of scope here and belong with counsel. Do not answer them from a template.

## Answering a request you have to decline

Decline early, in specifics, and offer what does exist. A late decline is worse than an early one because it has already consumed the attendee's planning window. Say what you cannot do, why, what you can do instead, and whether the answer changes for the next edition - then put it in the debrief so it does.

## On the day

A provision nobody announces is used only by the people who already knew about it, which is the opposite of the point.

- **In the opening**, name what exists and where: the quiet space, reserved seating, all-gender restrooms, the consent stickers and what the colours mean, the nursing room, who to find and how to recognize them - the marked inclusion team at a large conference, the organizers themselves at a small one. Sixty seconds.
- **At the registration desk**, brief the volunteers on every provision, including the ones whose location is deliberately unpublished - the nursing room's location withheld until check-in only works if the desk actually knows it.
- **Signpost the walk-up provisions** physically. Anything that requires asking a human filters out the people least willing to ask.
- **Name the owner per provision** for the duration of the event, and make sure the desk knows who that is.
