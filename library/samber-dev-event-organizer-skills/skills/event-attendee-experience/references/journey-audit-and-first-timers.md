# The walkthrough audit, and designing a moment

## The audit table

Run it twice: once on paper against the floor plan, before anything is designed, and once by physically walking the building before doors open.

| Touchpoint | What the attendee does | What they feel | Pain point | Opportunity |
| ---------- | ---------------------- | -------------- | ---------- | ----------- |

The stages to fill it with, in the order an attendee meets them:

1. **Arrival** - finding the building, the entrance, the right door.
2. **Check-in and badge pickup** - the first physical interaction with the event. This is the moment-of-truth analog: it sets tone the way an MC's opening does, and it is the one touchpoint every attendee passes through without exception.
3. **First wayfinding decision** - the choice made immediately after the desk, usually in the least-signed spot in the building.
4. **First session** - finding the room, finding a seat, working out whether leaving partway is acceptable here.
5. **Hallway track and networking space** - the gap between sessions, and whether there is anywhere to stand.
6. **Quiet room** - findable without asking, or not.
7. **Swag pickup** - a queue or a table.
8. **Meals** - the general line, and the restricted line.
9. **The help moment** - the first time they need to ask something, and who they ask.
10. **Departure** - the end of the day, and whether anything marks it.

## A worked row, and its negative counterpart

**Good - a row that changes the design:**

| Touchpoint                | Action                                                                              | Emotion                                               | Pain point                                                                                                                          | Opportunity                                                                                                                    |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| First wayfinding decision | Steps away from the desk holding a badge and a printed programme, looks for Track B | Mildly anxious, does not want to look lost on day one | Nothing is signed between the desk and the corridor junction eight metres away; the only fix is asking a volunteer who is mid-queue | One A3 sign at the junction naming both tracks by destination; costs one print, removes the most-asked question of the morning |

The row is useful because the pain point names a specific place, and the opportunity is something you can do before doors open.

**Bad - a row that changes nothing:**

| Touchpoint | Action             | Emotion | Pain point          | Opportunity                     |
| ---------- | ------------------ | ------- | ------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Check-in   | Attendee checks in | Neutral | Queue could be long | Improve the check-in experience |

Every cell restates the touchpoint. "Could be long" names no hour and no cause, and "improve the experience" is the question, not an answer. A row that survives without a location, a cause and a fix you could execute this week is not worth writing down.

## Reading the finished table

- Rows come back bad in clusters, not evenly. Two bad rows next to each other usually share one cause - a single unsigned junction, or a desk placed where two queues meet.
- Design against the bad rows only. A table with ten good rows and two bad ones is a signage list, not a redesign.
- The rows that come back bad on the physical walk but looked fine on paper are the valuable ones. They are the difference between the floor plan and the building.
- Keep the completed table. Next edition starts from it rather than from a blank map, which is the whole return on running it.

## Designing a memorable moment

No rigorous, validated methodology exists for this specifically, but a starting framework and one industry statistic are published in its place.

**Published practice.** PCMA's trade publication _Convene_ ("User-Experience Design and Your Meetings", Dave Lutz, CMP, 2014) argues most conference planning runs a continuous-improvement map backward - Systems & Resources > Procedures > Touchpoints > Interactions > Experiences - engineering the logistics first and treating the experience as whatever is left over. It recommends flipping that order: design the Experience first, from the attendee's own perspective, and work back to Systems & Resources last. Its stated reason moments matter at all: attendees remember the peak experiences, best and worst, more than the average ones - the same peak-focused pattern the statistic below reports independently.

Freeman's 2025 "Unpacking XLNC" experience-trends report finds attendees who experience a "peak moment" are 85% more likely to return, while only 40% of attendees report having had one against 78% of organizers who believe they are delivering them.

Read the statistic honestly before designing against it. It is a **correlation between a reported experience and a stated return intent**, measured across an entire events industry - not a causal claim, not a method, and not a target.

The genuinely useful half is the second number: the gap between 40% and 78% says organizers systematically believe they are delivering moments that attendees do not report having. That gap is the finding. The 85% is not a design instruction.

Self-set defaults, offered as worth trying:

- **Ask rather than assume.** The single cheapest correction to the perception gap is one post-event question: did anything about the day stand out to you, and what? Whoever owns the feedback survey asks it - you supply the question.
- **Design one moment, not a programme of them.** Moments that are announced as moments are received as programming. The published material describes them as experienced, never as scheduled.
- **Look for the moment in a touchpoint that already exists**, from the audit table above - the arrival, the closing, the meal - rather than adding a new one. An added activity competes with the hallway track - a well-designed existing touchpoint does not.
- **Keep it inside the register the culture work already set.** A moment that contradicts the event's own tone is memorable in the wrong direction.
- **Never design it for a subset.** A moment engineered for the people at the front of the room is one more thing the rest of the room watched.
