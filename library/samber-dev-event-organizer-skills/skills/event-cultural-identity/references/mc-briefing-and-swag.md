# MC briefing and swag posture evidence

The two touchpoints organizers most often treat as pure logistics. MC-craft guidance tends to come from professional hosts' own marketing material, and most swag-philosophy writing comes from swag vendors and planning associations - both reliable on their own practice, but self-interested on whether you should hire or buy from them. The neutral anchors below are drawn from actual event practice.

## MC and hosting

### The craft, per named practitioners (vendor-pole practice)

- **The first 90 seconds** (Will Gill, corporate emcee - Lenovo, PayPal, Salesforce, the UN): "The first 90 seconds set the tone for the entire event. An emcee who opens with hesitation, mumbled jokes, or weak energy signals to the audience that this event won't be worth their full attention." He reads attendee demographics, event purpose, and client culture in advance.
- **Script and coach** (Rosemary Ravinal, executive-speaking coach): design the flow, write "the script the celebrity emcee will read", coach the speakers - emceeing treated as an art form that sets "tone and tempo".
- **Briefing steps** (Corporate Entertainment Agency guide):
  1. Script for "the right tone for your company culture."
  2. Rehearse together beforehand.
  3. Introduce the emcee via a "Voice of God" announcement.
  4. Keep an announcements/emergency list.
  5. "End on a high note."
- **Developer-conference specialist** (Justin Halsall, hired MC for tech events - hackathons, startup pitches, multi-track developer conferences): still a paid, deliberate-craft role, but scoped to the same events this skill's community pole covers rather than to generic corporate clients. His own checklist runs the MC's prep through schedule and announcements, speaker introductions and sponsor recognition, mic and filming setup, and stage-side operations (time cues, sustenance, a communication hierarchy with whoever owns the run of show) - and treats thanking organizers and volunteers on stage as a standing checklist item, not an afterthought.

### The community-side difference

MC-as-craft, treated deliberately, clusters almost entirely on the vendor/corporate pole, and even its one developer-event-specific example above is still a paid specialist rather than an organizer wearing the MC hat. Community events distribute tone-setting among organizers who already embody the norms, and this pattern is sourced rather than asserted: DevOpsDays Halifax 2024 ran its emcee slot by having two of its own speakers "wear the hats as Emcees" instead of hiring one, with prep limited to knowing how to fill time and holding a few "groan-worthy dad jokes" ready for technical difficulties. This is a structural difference, not a gap to fix: on the community pole, route the "MC design" step to distributing hosting duties and briefing the organizer-hosts, not to hiring or imitating a professional host.

### The host brief (works for a professional MC or an organizer-host)

Hand whoever holds the microphone one page:

1. The purpose sentence (the specific one, past the category label).
2. The register attributes with their "never" clauses, plus the banned-register list.
3. The pop-up rules to announce, and the words to announce them in.
4. The opening script or beat sheet - no logistics in the first minutes; logistics get their own later slot.
5. The closing ritual - what the deliberate final moment is and who owns it.
6. Pronunciation list: speaker names, community in-terms, sponsor names.
7. The incident script: what they say, in what register, if something goes wrong mid-program - written in advance because the register shift (drop the humor, go plain and direct) is exactly what improvisation gets wrong.
8. One rehearsal, minimum, for anything scripted.

## Swag posture

### Inclusive sizing

DevOpsDays' organizing guide: **ask** attendees for sizing at registration - "Do not just guess, or you will find you've inadvertently made some attendees feel excluded" - and offer sizing beyond unisex S-M-L-XL. A merchandise-ordering decision most organizers file under logistics is where a stated "welcoming" value gets executed or silently broken. This rule applies at every swag rung: whatever you produce, size it inclusively.

### Less-but-better, or none-plus-donation (the sustainability axis)

For swag's measured waste and carbon line, and what a public sustainability claim may say about it, see `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-sustainability-plan`.

- Fast Company's Elizabeth Segran ("Can We Finally Kill Off Cheap, Disposable Conference Swag?") is the most-cited neutral piece; PCMA and BizBash document the planner shift toward fewer, higher-quality items or none.
- Donation-instead-of-swag is a real, named practice: Alation is cited by Prevue as replacing swag with a charity donation "for the past several years".
- HashiCorp's "we will never have a ball pit at HashiConf" is the sharpest statement of the underlying discipline: extras are subordinated to purpose.

### The signal logic (planner and industry perspective)

- Cheap commodity swag signals corner-cutting and reads as "you don't really get this audience".
- Fewer, well-made, useful items signal that the event's stated values extend to its purchasing.
- Deliberate absence - announced, with the budget visibly redirected (donation, catering, captioning) - signals seriousness; unannounced absence just reads as cheap.

### The two axes are separate questions

Inclusivity (who can wear it) and austerity/sustainability (how much and how well-made) are independent design questions with separate sourced precedent. An event can run a full inclusive-sized shirt program and a lean everything-else; a sustainability-first event that hands out commodity plastic contradicts itself regardless of sizing. Decide each against the stated register, not as one bundled "swag budget" call.
