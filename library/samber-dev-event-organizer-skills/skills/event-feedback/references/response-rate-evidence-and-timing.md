# Response-rate evidence and timing

Every figure below carries its source, its sample where one is disclosed, and a credibility classification. Cite the classification whenever you cite the number, since the gap between tiers affects how the figure can be used. Sources are named for full transparency, not as a product recommendation.

## 1. The tiering rule

- **Credible** - a named researcher or a disclosed sample: Explori's figures (attributed to a named senior research manager), Survicate's 267,564-response benchmark study, the PCMA/Sparks satisfaction thresholds.
- **Directional** - event-technology vendor claims whose underlying samples are not disclosed: SurveySparrow, QR Poll, Zuddl, Xola/SurveyAnyplace. Usable for a ratio or an order of magnitude, never as an authority.

A figure from the directional tier that agrees with the credible tier strengthens the ratio. A figure from the directional tier standing alone strengthens nothing.

## 2. Emailed post-event response rates

**Credible.** Explori (UK event-survey research specialist), via senior research manager Charlotte Penn:

- Conferences and corporate events land at **10-20%**, depending on event size.
- Live trade shows land at **12-15%**.
- A well-designed, well-timed survey can reach **20-30%**.

**Directional.** A general **20-30%** event benchmark is repeated by several event-technology vendors. That is consensus repetition of the same claim, not independent measurement - do not read four vendors agreeing as four data points.

**Working band for this skill: roughly 10-30% emailed.** The floor used in `SKILL.md`'s measurement section - roughly one in ten - is the bottom of Explori's conference range.

## 3. Survey length against response rate

**Credible, and the single most methodologically transparent source found.** Survicate's 2025 Survey Response Rate Benchmark Report, 267,564 responses analysed: micro-surveys of 2-3 questions reach the highest median response rate at **15.97%**; surveys of 7 or more questions drop to **6.87%** median. That is a factor of more than two, from length alone.

**Directional, consistent.** A separate claim of a **17% drop** for surveys over 12 questions or 5 minutes. Different study, same direction.

Cross-industry, not event-specific - the effect of length is a general survey property, which is precisely why it transfers. Use the ratio ("more than doubles"), not the point figures.

## 4. In-room and real-time collection

Two vendor figures exist here and they measure **two different quantities**. Keep them apart.

**Directional - response rate.** One vendor reports in-room QR polls at **40-60%** against **8-15%** for post-event email. Both halves are response rates: the share of the eligible audience that answers at all.

**Directional - completion rate.** A second vendor reports in-person collection at **85-95% completion**: the share who finish once they have started. Its own email comparison ("typically 20-30%") is a response rate, so that vendor's two numbers are not comparable to each other either.

The two sources do not disagree; they answer different questions. Any single band running 40% to 95% merges the first source's response-rate floor with the second's completion-rate ceiling and measures nothing.

`SKILL.md` therefore carries 40-60% as the in-room **response rate** and 85-95% as the in-room **completion rate**, separately, and compares channels on response rate only. In-room capture beats emailed collection by a **multiple** - roughly two to four times the 10-30% emailed band - rather than by a margin.

Mechanism, and the reason the ratio is believable independent of the sources: a code shown at the end of a session is answered while the session is still in the room's memory, by people who have not yet left. An email competes with an inbox three days later.

## 5. The documented high performer

**Credible.** Nicola Kastner, former Global VP of Event Marketing Strategy at SAP, interviewed by Explori's CEO Mark Brewster: sustains post-event response rates "consistently in the eighties and nineties", and treats anything below 75% as a bad result - which she reports having happened once.

Her stated method is the mechanism behind the third menu in `SKILL.md`: treat the event as a product and the survey as its improvement mechanism, and **always act on feedback so participants believe answering is worth it**. This is a single named practitioner at enterprise scale, not a distribution - it establishes that the ceiling is far above the benchmark bands, not that a community meetup should expect 85%.

## 6. Incentives

**Credible, single case study.** Explori reports a client whose headline speakers signed copies of their latest books, offered as a prize: **40% response rate against a 20-30% baseline.**

Explori's own guidance alongside it: choose either a high-value prize for few winners or a low-value prize for many, and prefer money-can't-buy, event-specific prizes over generic technology gadgets. The lift in `SKILL.md`'s channel menu - "roughly half again" - is this case study's ratio, and one case study is what it rests on.

## 7. Satisfaction and return-intent thresholds

**Credible.** The PCMA/Sparks _Metrics That Matter Most_ benchmark set (Lisette Sheehan and Lyndsay Merbach, Sparks agency, 2017 PCMA Education Conference). These are satisfaction thresholds, a different metric class from response rate:

- **≥67%** "definitely or likely to attend next year" - the early-warning line `SKILL.md` uses.
- **73%** of attendees rating content value excellent or very good.
- **75%** agreeing it was a must-attend or essential-for-contacts event.
- **≥75%** rating staff excellent or very good.
- A healthy new-versus-repeat mix of roughly **38% newcomers** and **28%** who attended three of the past four editions.

**Credible, published by the organization itself.** CNCF reports overall satisfaction of **4.2/5** across all its events.

## 8. Send timing, and why it is unproven

**Consensus, not measurement.** SurveyMonkey, ASAE, Cvent, Swapcard and Bizzabo all converge on sending within **24-48 hours**. Explori warns that the further from the event you collect, the more response rate you forfeit.

**Nothing measures the effect of send timing on a technical event's response rate.** Present the 24-48 hour window as strong practitioner consensus and follow it, since nothing argues the other way, but never as a measured effect. Do not quote a percentage improvement for it.

**Explicitly unverified, do not cite**: a claim that SMS within two hours gets "32% more completions", attributed to an unnamed mobile-engagement report on a vendor blog with no accessible methodology.

## 9. Reminder cadence

**Credible-adjacent, from two association and research sources that agree.**

- Explori: reminders 3-5 days after launch, then another 3-5 days later, varying the day and time.
- SurveyMonkey: one reminder at 48-72 hours, a final one around day 7.
- ASAE: the 24-48 hour send plus a polite reminder days later.

The shared ceiling is what matters: **at most two reminders.** Beyond that, every source describes fatigue rather than incremental responses.

## 10. Where a percentage is the wrong instrument

- **Hackathons.** MLH holds league-wide comparative data and shares it privately with member organizers, so a public hackathon has nothing open to compare itself against. Academic hackathon studies report 85-87.5% response rates on captive samples of roughly 35-40 people - not generalisable to a public community hackathon.
- **Speakers and sponsors.** Every band above is attendee-scale, drawn from conference-industry survey research. A six-sponsor list where two answer is a 33% response rate and a meaningless one; treat small-population roles by name, not by percentage.
