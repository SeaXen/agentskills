## 1. The alcohol-free provision, designed as content

Construction throughout, argued from named organizers' practice rather than from a standard.

The design question is not "is there something to drink". It is **whether the person not drinking alcohol is having the same evening as everybody else, or standing beside it.** A jug at the end of the bar fails that test while satisfying the letter of "we provided options".

Four properties to build for. Each is a deliberate choice, not a courtesy:

- **Same place, same moment.** The alcohol-free option is served from the same table by the same person, not from a corner nobody walks to. Whoever is pouring should be able to offer either without asking why.
- **Comparable, not consolatory.** Something made rather than something left over. The point is that choosing it is a choice rather than an abstention.
- **Unremarkable to take.** Nobody should have to explain, and nobody should be asked. This is the property that serves a recovery, medical, religious or pregnancy reason without ever requiring a disclosure - which is exactly why it is designed as a default rather than as an accommodation.
- **Named where the evening is announced**, not discovered on arrival. Somebody decides whether to come based on it.

**The seam this sits on.** `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-accessibility-inclusion` says nothing about alcohol from its side. The split below is therefore written from this side only, for that skill to confirm or contest.

This skill owns the event's default posture and the content above. That skill would own it only where a specific accommodation request turns a default into a stated access commitment with an owner and a published depth. Re-check when that skill revises.

**More than one named anchor, still short of a standard.** One named event lists non-alcoholic options alongside quiet spaces, gender-neutral restroom signage and free childcare - a set of accessibility choices doubling as cultural statements. That is attendee-account and interview sourcing rather than a published policy, and it is one event.

A second named tech-conference organizer gives the same posture from the reduction side. Kathleen Danielson, who has organized State of the Map US (the OpenStreetMap community's annual US conference), wrote publicly: "As an event organizer, I am 100% a part of the problem, so I want to personally apologize to anyone who has felt uncomfortable or less welcome at an event because of alcohol, and a lack of ample non-alcoholic choices." She adds that "the tech community... is slowly becoming more aware of the need to make events more inclusive, and lessening the reliance on alcohol is one important way to do that." That names the shift as a deliberate organizing choice rather than an incidental one.

Two events-industry trade sources widen the reasoning beyond any single event. A meetings-trade article quotes a hospitality consultant asking why a business event would risk "mistakes, regrettable choices, inappropriate behavior" by centering alcohol, and quotes an events director naming non-alcoholic provision as "another key component" of inclusive event design.

The Professional Convention Management Association's own coverage of the same shift quotes a software developer's design principle for event bars: non-alcoholic drinks should be "at least as easy" to get and "at least as varied or fancy" as alcoholic ones. That independently makes the claim the "comparable, not consolatory" property makes above.

Together these argue for deliberate alcohol-free provision from more than one direction: reducing a named harm, widening who can fully attend, and matching a demand shift. None of them is a published, adopted cross-conference standard, and none may be quoted as one.

## 2. Cap mechanics for bounded hosted service

Construction. A cap is only real if it is decided before the evening and held by somebody who is not enjoying it.

Three cap shapes, none strictly better:

- **By hours.** The bar opens and closes at stated times, published in advance. Simplest to hold, and it produces a rush at closing.
- **By tokens or drink tickets.** Each person gets a fixed allocation. Distributes rather than concentrates, and it makes the cap visible without anyone policing it. It also creates a small trade in tokens, which is worth expecting rather than being surprised by.
- **By a stated bar total after which service stops.** Easiest on a budget line and the least legible to attendees, because nobody can tell how close it is.

Whichever shape, decide these before the night:

- **Who holds the cap.** Name a person who is not hosting, not speaking and not drinking, and who has the authority to stop service. An unowned cap is not a cap.
- **What happens when it is reached.** The alcohol-free provision keeps running; the evening does not end when the bar does. Say this in advance or the cap reads as the event closing.
- **Who may not be served.** Anyone under the local legal age, and anyone the servers judge should not be. If the event admits minors at all, this is a hard operational constraint on the whole evening, not a note - route it to the risk register.
- **How people get home.** Construction, and the thing most often left out: know what transport exists at closing time and say so where the evening is announced.

## 3. What the evening programme's brief carries

Construction throughout, with one exception: the default rung's value case rests on the organizer account quoted in SKILL.md's venue-based mixer bullet. Sourced material otherwise confirms this line item exists - a delegated team role, a sellable branded surface, an access lever - without designing most of it.

A brief that is actually executable names:

- **The owner.** One person. `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-first-edition`'s sourced nine-role delegated team names evening-event logistics as a role in its own right. `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-team-structure`'s standing taxonomy does not, so on a recurring event the owner is placed under an existing functional lead rather than found ready-made.

  Without one, the SKILL.md menu deletes every rung above "no organised programme", and that deletion is the right answer rather than a shortfall.

- **Who it is for.** The whole audience, or a subset. If a subset, say which and why, because "the evening event" and "an invited dinner" are different artifacts and conflating them is how people find out at the door.
- **Where, and whether that place was chosen for access.** A venue picked for atmosphere and not for a step-free route is a decision about who attends.
- **What it costs the attendee.** Ideally nothing. Anything else is a filter, and it should be a deliberate one.
- **The food and drink**, from the two menus in SKILL.md rather than decided afresh at night.
- **The start and the end**, published. An evening with no stated end runs until it drifts somewhere unhosted.
- **How long, and how the room is built.** An events-industry planner's published guidance treats two hours as the ceiling worth aiming for, and three-plus hours as a named planning mistake: a party that runs long thins out rather than building momentum. The same guidance recommends a venue that lets attendees spread out and move rather than clustering them, since that is what makes mingling easy instead of just giving people somewhere to stand. (Sourced, general events-industry practice, not dev-conference-specific.)
- **The access lever, where one applies.** One organizer guide names family-inclusive evening programming as an access lever distinct from the code of conduct (sourced). Read as: the format decides who can come at all, and a caregiver's attendance is decided by it long before the night.

## 4. Handoffs: the scope clause and the risk register

Two siblings must receive the evening decision, whichever rung wins:

- `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-code-of-conduct` owns the scope clause, which decides "which people, which spaces, which dates, and what happens off-site". An evening in your own building and an evening somewhere else are different answers to it, and an unhosted drift is the case where the clause is weakest exactly where the risk is highest. Hand it the venue, the times and the population.
- `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-risk-management` scores alcohol service as a named impact amplifier on its matrix and owns the insurance ladder, where host-liquor liability attaches. `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-sponsor-agreement` separately promotes layered coverage when alcohol is served.

Neither is a decision made here. Both need the posture as an input, and both are cheaper to answer before a venue is held than after.

Where the evening is offsite, add the second venue's own insurance ask and transport arrangements to the same handoff. Both belong to `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-risk-management`. Neither is a hospitality decision.

## 5. What to publish, either way

Construction, and the cheapest thing on this page.

Publish the alcohol posture and the evening decision on the schedule and wherever the event describes the day - including when the answer is "nothing organised" and when it is "no alcohol served". Both are legitimate postures, and both fail the same way when left unstated: people fill the silence themselves, in a venue nobody chose, under a scope clause that may not reach them.

Three sentences are usually enough:

> After the last session we are running {the format} at {place}, from {time} to {time}, open to everyone with a badge and free to attend.
> We are serving {the alcohol posture, stated plainly}, and {the alcohol-free provision, named as something rather than as an absence}.
> {Transport at closing time, or the absence of it.}

Where the answer is nothing: say that the day ends at the last session, and say it early enough that people can plan their own evening rather than discovering the gap when the last session ends.
