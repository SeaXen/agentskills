# Precedent and evidence

## tech&fest (Grenoble, France) - the one documented account

tech&fest, an annual deeptech conference at Alpexpo in Grenoble, ran "Learning Expeditions" as an official programme track in its 2024 and 2025 editions. The inaugural announcement named "learning expeditions" as a planned track alongside hosting international delegations for the first, February 2024 edition ([lemondeinformatique.fr](https://www.lemondeinformatique.fr/actualites/lire-grenoble-inaugure-son-techetfest-en-fevrier-2024-92520.html)) - the format ran from the conference's first year, not as a one-off.

The 2025 programme listed CEA, Teledyne e2v, Inria, Constellium, HRS, and HPE as Learning Expedition hosts, with attendees bussed from the main venue to the host site of their choice: several parallel single-host tracks running alongside each other, not one sequenced circuit through all of them.

HPE's 2025 expedition is the one instance with a first-hand account. Attendees toured HPE's own Grenoble site to see its Champollion AI supercomputer, built with Nvidia, and a reporter who joined wrote that the machine's noise was still ringing in their ears afterward, "nos oreilles s'en souviennent encore" ([maddyness.com](https://www.maddyness.com/2025/02/07/techfest-une-passerelle-entre-la-tech-et-la-recherche-qui-continue-de-monter-en-puissance-a-grenoble/), 7 February 2025). The account confirms a real host-led visit: a working machine on a working floor, shown by the host that operates it.

These two accounts establish the host list and the one site's content, nothing more. They leave attendee count, transport, briefing, sign-up and any access restriction unmentioned, for HPE and for the other five hosts alike.

## A same-name format that is not this skill's subject

Viva Technology, a much larger Paris conference, also sells something it calls a "Learning Expedition," through HUB Institute as an official partner since 2022 ([hubinstitute.com](https://www.hubinstitute.com/learning-expeditions/viva-technology)). That product is a curated, expert-guided tour of the conference's own exhibition floor: a roadbook of roughly 100 startup booths, an accompanying analyst pair, and a post-visit report. It never leaves the conference's own venue, and the "host" is the event itself, not a third party's working site. The same term covers two different products in the field - read which one a request describes before routing it here.

## The format's general shape, independent of one conference

Event-agency material describing "Learning Expedition" as a standalone corporate format ([agence-evenementielle-innovevents.fr](https://www.agence-evenementielle-innovevents.fr/learning-expedition-format-immesion/)) names the same host-side asks this skill's interview probes for: the host sets objectives and three to five research questions, supplies the participant list, sets the confidentiality level including an NDA where required, and decides access based on competitive sensitivity. It also names a typical rhythm for a group of ten to twenty-five: a briefing, a visit or talk of an hour to ninety minutes, a debrief, and a closing synthesis workshop.

This is a corporate benchmarking product bought outside any conference, not a conference programme, so it does not by itself confirm a host-dependency or access rung. It corroborates that hosts genuinely impose the confidentiality and NDA terms this skill's access-and-repeatability posture already lists as rungs.

## Whether a pre-entry undertaking unlocks more access than after-the-fact review

No host, tour operator, academic study or industry body publishes a direct comparison of a pre-visit confidentiality agreement against after-the-visit publication review as alternative routes to deeper access. The two mechanisms appear in the literature doing different jobs: a pre-visit agreement is documented as a precondition for entry, deciding whether the visit happens and what gets shown, while a post-visit review is documented as a publication control, deciding what a visitor may later say. Corporate benchmarking's own standard, the APQC/TRADE Benchmarking Code of Conduct, treats confidentiality as owed to a partner "from the outset" and recommends negotiating an NDA before visiting a competitor specifically "to satisfy the legal counsel representing each partner" ([bpir.com](https://www.bpir.com/benchmarking-code-of-conduct/)). US securities law gives the clearest structural reason a pre-visit instrument would matter: an issuer can only share material nonpublic information during a facility visit without triggering Regulation FD if the recipient has expressly agreed to confidentiality beforehand, and post-visit review has no equivalent exemption in that framework ([securities-law-blog.com](https://securities-law-blog.com/2023/07/25/regulation-fd/)).

The one detailed first-hand account of an NDA-gated visit argues the opposite. Industrial engineer and lean-manufacturing consultant Michel Baudin documented signing an NDA barring photos, sketches and social posts before a Tesla factory tour, then compared what he saw to what other visitors had already published without any agreement: "If there is anything in the tour I took last week that is not already published, I missed it, so I am not at risk of accidentally disclosing it." He adds that signing the NDA made him believe he was about to see something secret, which "enhanced my perception of the value of the visit" independent of whether it actually was more revealing ([michelbaudin.com](https://michelbaudin.com/2016/05/01/tesla-plant-visit-and-non-disclosure-agreement/)). A counter-data-point predates the instrument question entirely: Henry Ford's Highland Park plant opened fully to journalists from 1913 with no confidentiality mechanism of any kind, an openness "fairly novel" at the time that the resulting 1915 book _Ford Methods and Ford Shops_ documents, and that other manufacturers went on to imitate ([scopeofwork.net](https://www.scopeofwork.net/on-factory-tours/)) - maximal, unscripted access achieved with neither a pre-entry nor a post-hoc instrument. Separately, trade journalism supplies a case of the pre-visit agreement being refused outright: The Register declined the NDA offered at Intel Developer Forum, stating "we believe journalists should not and need not sign NDAs" ([theregister.com](https://www.theregister.com/1999/03/01/intel_nda_found_on_floor/)).

Treat the ordering as unresolved rather than settled. The strongest direct account on the actual question - what a host shows more of - runs against a pre-entry instrument unlocking more. The strongest support for a pre-entry instrument comes from a narrower legal exemption, whether disclosure is permitted at all in a regulated domain, rather than from any demonstrated difference in what gets shown.

A single anecdote cannot generalize either way, so the question stays open.

## Group size, site count and lead time in documented programmes

None of these figures come from a technical conference - the tech&fest account above says nothing about them - but named study-tour operators publish real ranges for the same shape of programme, useful as context for what other organizers have actually run rather than as a number to hand a reader.

Shinka Management's week-long Lean Japan Tour caps its standard departures at 20 participants and suggests 15 to 20 for a customized group booking, visits the genba of around five lean/TPS-focused organizations across the week, and tells participants which companies they will visit about one month before the programme starts ([shinkamanagement.com](https://shinkamanagement.com/lean-training/lean-tour/)). The Silicon Valley Study Tour, running since 2005, is also structured as a week-long tour and visits 8 to 10 companies ([siliconvalleystudytour.com](https://www.siliconvalleystudytour.com/how-it-works/)). A University of Wisconsin-Madison student delegation of 11 toured six named technology companies plus NASA Ames Research Center and a Stanford robotics lab on one trip ([ita.it.wisc.edu](https://ita.it.wisc.edu/2017/05/02/ita-students-inspired-by-silicon-valley-trip/)) - a smaller academic cohort against a similar site count.

Lead time for a single institutional site visit is a different question from lead time to design a whole expedition, and the only published figure is institutional rather than corporate: the University of Georgia's genetics department requires a minimum of two weeks' advance notice to book a group tour of its own facility ([dna.uga.edu](https://dna.uga.edu/tours)). No published source states a host organization's own turnaround time to approve or decline a visit request once asked, or a briefing-session length distinct from the one-to-ninety-minute visit-plus-talk rhythm the general-format spec above already gives.
