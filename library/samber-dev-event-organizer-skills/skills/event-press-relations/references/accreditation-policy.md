# Accreditation policy - eligibility, proof, comps, caps, refusals

Every quoted line below comes from a named event's own published policy page, except where marked. Policies change yearly and by edition: Web Summit's accreditation page still reads "Last updated: October 2020", while MWC/GSMA and SXSW maintain theirs more actively. Re-check the date on any page before copying its wording.

## 1. Eligibility is defined by function, not by title

The dominant model across MWC/GSMA, Web Summit, SXSW, RSA Conference and CES: accreditation goes to people whose **primary occupation** is editorial journalism, and commercial roles are excluded by name.

- **MWC Barcelona (GSMA)**: "The accreditation criteria assume that applicants are full-time in-house journalists, freelancers..." and excludes "academics, account representatives, engineers, financial analysts, general managers, marketing executives..."
- **Web Summit**: accreditation "will not be provided to marketing, advertising or public relations firms, consultants or advisors..."
- **RSA Conference**: "Those with sales, social media, marketing or account management roles will not be considered for press [accreditation]."
- **CES**: media registration goes to "working media that cover technology... including journalists, industry analysts, and content creators," and "will not be granted to publishers, general managers, account managers, sales representatives, brand ambassadors, PR/marketing and non-editorial media professionals." CES also states paywalled or password-protected content "may not be accepted" as proof.

**What to copy at community scale**: the _function-not-title_ principle and the commercial exclusion, in one or two sentences. Not the full exclusion list - a list that long reads as a wall and screens applicants nobody was going to get.

## 2. The proof stack, escalating with outlet type

MWC/GSMA publishes the most detailed requirements of any policy quoted here:

| Applicant type   | Proof required (MWC/GSMA)                                                                                                                                             |
| ---------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Print / newswire | Masthead copy or staff-listing link, plus "4-5 relevant bylined articles... published within the last three months", possibly an editor's confirmation email          |
| Online media     | Editorial-capacity link, 4–5 bylined articles, and "a recent monthly traffic report from an external demographic tool (like Google Analytics)"                        |
| Freelancers      | "Freelance journalists/photographers will not be accredited without a valid assignment letter/email" - "A press card alone will not be accepted as press credentials" |

SXSW requires proof of being "on assignment from a media outlet."

**Third-party press cards are not credentials.** Vendors such as USPA sell them, and MWC/GSMA states the line plainly: "a press card alone will not be accepted as press credentials". An applicant who offers one as their only proof is declined against a published criterion, not by a judgment call.

**What to copy at community scale**: one item of proof, not three. A link to two recent pieces with the applicant's name on them answers the same question as the full stack, and costs an applicant thirty seconds rather than an afternoon.

## 3. The creator lane

The split between editorial journalists and independent bloggers, podcasters and video creators is standard practice, not an edge case.

- **SXSW** runs two different intake forms: "Submit the Press Application if your primary work is editorial journalism... Submit the Content Creator Application" for creators, reviewed separately.
- **Web Summit**'s policy "does not exclude freelancers, contributors, analysts, filmmakers, crew, influencers, bloggers, YouTubers" but subjects them to "a more extensive assessment."
- **MWC Barcelona (GSMA)** publishes a quantitative bar: "100,000 or more subscribers or followers and videos receive on average 2,500 views."

⚠️ **The MWC bar is one event's threshold for its own creator lane, at MWC's scale.** Never present that number as a standard and never transfer it to another event's policy. Set a bar proportionate to your own audience, publish it as yours, and be willing to defend the number you chose.

**The creator lane is never the lighter one.** Beyond MWC, three more published policies draw the same line:

- **IBIE Baking Expo** requires its "Bloggers & Content Creators" lane to show "at least 5,000 unique visits per month," "at least 1 year" of proven on-topic coverage, and updates "at least biweekly" - while its journalist lane asks only for a business card or an assignment letter. It states outright that "personal websites, personal travel blogs, or personal social media accounts will not qualify."
- **IFEBP** admits a blogger only if they are "working journalists" or "affiliated in an editorial capacity with an established publication," or can show "consistent" on-topic writing; "other bloggers, personal website writers, designers, editors or creators do not qualify for complimentary press registration" at all.
- **ALA (American Library Association)** states "personal web pages and blogs will not be accepted"; an online creator qualifies only if the site "contain[s] editorial content and cover[s] library issues," where a traditional reporter clears the bar with a business card.

Four for four: every event that publishes both a press lane and a creator lane holds the creator lane to an equal or higher verification bar - a follower/traffic threshold, a publishing-history requirement, or both - never a lighter one. "Anyone with a blog gets in easier" is not a pattern any of them show.

One mechanic looks like a counter-example and is not. IBIE also runs a separate "Influencer" category: 15,000 followers on one platform, a minimum of three posts before or during the event, tagged and hashtagged. No traffic verification and no coverage history make that a lighter bar in isolation. But it obligates specific promotional output in return for the pass, which makes it a barter exchange rather than an unconditional credential, and that shape belongs with `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-media-partnerships`.

## 4. Comped versus paid

Free press admission is the norm at large commercial events. Web Summit, SXSW, RSA Conference and CES all comp: "Accepted applications will receive complimentary admission to the conference" / "complimentary press pass."

**DEF CON is the categorical exception**: "Press passes aren't complimentary and that policy is universally enforced regardless of tenure or masthead". Everyone buys a badge; accredited press still get "access to the press room, expedited badge pick up, and logistical support from the Press Goons."

For a community event, comping a small number of accredited press and creators is the norm, and DEF CON's paid-badge model is the outlier rather than the default. Decide it consciously and write the number down before the first application, because the constraint is comped seats, not policy text.

## 5. Caps and deadlines

Hard numeric caps are rare but real:

- **DEF CON**: "there is a limit of 2 badges per outlet" in some years; described elsewhere as a hard limit on the total number of press each year.
- **ISTE**: for its 2026 edition, "we will make approximately 20 registrations available for bloggers and podcasters". Treat it as one past edition's own number rather than a standing promise.

Each figure is one event's own published cap for its own edition, not a ratio anyone can transfer. What transfers is the practice of publishing a cap at all - a cap stated in advance turns a later "no" into arithmetic rather than a verdict on the applicant.

## 6. Approval rates

Events describe the accreditation decision as discretionary and case-by-case; SXSW's published wording is that "credentials will be allotted on a case-by-case basis and are never guaranteed just because you requested them". Never quote an approval rate - including your own from one edition - as though it predicts the next.

## 7. Editorial-independence conditions

Some events attach conduct terms to the credential. **Web Summit**: "It is expected that media accreditation holders credit Web Summit as their source," and the event "retains the right to cancel any media accreditation at their discretion and at any time." **MWC Barcelona (GSMA)** enforces the same idea at the organisation level: knowingly submitting a false assignment letter for an unqualified applicant "can result in an entire organisation losing accreditation."

A community event can reasonably attach two conditions and no more: disclose press status when interviewing anyone, and respect the attendee opt-out mechanism. Both are enforceable by withdrawing the credential, which is the only lever that exists.

## 8. Writing the refusal (unsourced template)

⚠️ **Unsourced template. Nothing in this section is copied from an event's observed practice.** No event publishes its refusal process, so the five steps and both examples below are constructed from the eligibility and editorial-independence rules in sections 1-7. Treat them as reasoned, not observed, and say so when handing them to an organizer.

The refusal, not the policy, is what becomes public. Write it so a refused applicant has nothing to quote except the criterion they were measured against.

1. **Publish the bar before the first application opens.** A criterion invented after an application arrives reads as a criterion invented about that applicant.
2. **Refuse in writing, quoting the published criterion.** One sentence naming which requirement was not met. No commentary on the outlet, the audience, or the person.
3. **Name what would change the answer.** "Two published pieces with your byline in the last three months" is checkable; "a more established outlet" is not, and invites the argument.
4. **Offer the fallback that costs nothing**: an ordinary ticket at the usual price, the ungated media page, and the post-event stats release. Most refusals are about a comped seat, not about access to information.
5. **Route it through the single designated voice.** A refusal answered a second time by a different organizer, in different words, is how one decline becomes a story.

**Negative example** - do not send: _"We've decided to keep press passes for established outlets this year, and your channel isn't quite the right fit for our audience."_ It names no criterion, implies a judgment about the applicant's work, and gives them nothing to satisfy - every clause is quotable and none is defensible.

**Positive example**: _"Our creator lane asks for a channel with an audience over [your published number] and two pieces on this subject in the last six months. Your channel meets the first and we couldn't find the second - send two links and we'll re-open it. A standard ticket is available at [price], and the media page and post-event stats are open to everyone at [URL]."_
