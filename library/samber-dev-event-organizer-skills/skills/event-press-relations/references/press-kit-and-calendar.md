# Media page contents and the announcement calendar

Every element and lead time below comes from a published event media page or a real annual cycle: CNCF/KubeCon, CES, SXSW, RSA Conference, ASCO, PyCon US, DEF CON, AWS re:Invent. All of those pages are built at commercial scale.

One passage is the exception. The "cheapest version that hits every element" at the end of section 1 is a scaled-down subset for a community event, and no event publishes a minimum of that kind. Present it as a judgement call, never as a published standard.

## 1. The eight elements of an event media page

Listed in the order real event media pages use. Keep the whole page **ungated** - every element on the pages named above downloads with no registration.

1. **Fact sheet** - dates, venue and city, format (in-person, hybrid, online), ticket types, expected scale, one-line description.
2. **Boilerplate "about" paragraph** - the reusable text you want quoted verbatim. CNCF's is the cited model, opening "The Cloud Native Computing Foundation... hosts critical components of the global technology infrastructure..." Write one paragraph, freeze it, and reuse it across every release so a journalist copying it produces the same sentence every year.
3. **Brand assets** - logos in multiple layouts and formats (PNG with transparency prioritized, SVG for design teams) with usage rules. KubeCon's speaker guide is explicit about its own: "you may not refer to the conference as KubeCon only" - "You must use the full KubeCon + CloudNativeCon conference logo." If your event's name has a rule, state it here rather than correcting people afterwards.
4. **Photography and B-roll** - high-resolution images from past editions and broadcast-quality video. CES provides "a media kit, high-quality photos, press releases, and B-roll video content"; SXSW's Press Center offers "downloadable press images, press releases, event statistics." Shoot these at the edition you are running now, because next year's page needs them.
5. **Speaker bios and headshots** - short bios plus professional headshots; for keynotes, links to prior coverage.
6. **Stats on past editions** - the event-specific credibility currency, and the element a community event can actually compete on. Real published examples, each with its source and the quantity it measures:
   - **KubeCon NA 2024** (CNCF's own recap): 1,937 submissions, 218 sessions, 87 CNCF project maintainer-hosted sessions, curated by 93 community members and 22 track chairs.
   - **Web Summit 2023, Lisbon** (organizer figures cited by Axios): 70,236 attendees from 153 countries, 2,608 companies exhibiting.
   - **PyCon US** (PyCon's own blog recaps): 2024 - 2,991 total attendees (2,551 in person, 440 online) from 95 countries; 2025 - 2,225 total attendees from 58 countries.
   - **SXSW 2023 economic impact** (an analysis SXSW commissioned from Greyhill Advisors): "generated $380.9 million in spending activity". Local and business press cares about this figure class specifically, which is a different audience from the attendee-count figures aimed at trade press.

   Each is one event's own figure for one edition. None is a benchmark, and none transfers - publish yours.

7. **Embargoed-announcement area** - a credentialed-login area holding embargoed releases that open as lift times pass, run on a commercial wire-service distribution platform. Two large conferences' media centres work exactly this way: credentialed reporters "can log in to view embargoed press releases", and content is released "as the embargoes lift". This is the top rung of the embargo menu, and most community events never need it.
8. **Media contact** - a named person or team with a working address. CNCF routes through a PR team; RSA Conference lists a `PR@` address; SXSW uses a `press@` address. One address, monitored, answered by the designated voice.

⚠️ **The cheapest version that hits every element is a judgement call, not a published minimum.** No event publishes a minimum viable subset, so name this one as your own choice when handing it over.

It holds a fact sheet, the boilerplate paragraph, a logo pack, past-edition stats, and one named contact: one page, no gate, updated once a year. Elements 4, 5 and 7 are additions, not prerequisites.

## 2. The six announcement beats

Lead times below come from real annual cycles: PyCon US's own published timeline, KubeCon/CNCF's schedule posting and recap cadence, DEF CON, AWS re:Invent. Each is one event's cycle, not an industry average.

| Beat                            | Lead time                                                                                        | Who covers it                                                                             |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| CFP opens                       | ~8–12 months before the event (PyCon US's published timeline)                                    | Niche and developer press, CFP aggregators, community channels - **not** mainstream press |
| Speaker lineup / initial reveal | ~2–4 months out                                                                                  | Trade and developer press. **The biggest earned-media moment of the cycle**               |
| Full programme / agenda release | ~6–12 weeks out; KubeCon schedules post roughly 3 months ahead                                   | Trade and developer press; often bundled with the lineup release                          |
| Ticket on-sale / early bird     | Varies widely, often 5–7 months out. PyCon US opens registration around December for a May event | Local and trade press                                                                     |
| Day-of / live coverage          | -                                                                                                | All tiers: trade, developer, local, independent creators                                  |
| Post-event wrap-up and stats    | Days to ~2 weeks after; CNCF publishes recaps "within two weeks" and posts session recordings    | Trade and local business press; this data feeds next year's fact sheet                    |

**The lineup/schedule reveal is the single highest-leverage moment in the whole annual cycle.** It draws the widest press tier and every named example treats it as a dedicated press moment rather than a passive schedule-page update - real headline patterns include "Lineup Announced" on a wire service and "CNCF Unveils Schedule for KubeCon + CloudNativeCon Europe 2026." If only one beat gets worked properly, it is this one.

One mechanic is worth naming for the on-sale beat: AWS re:Invent's early cycle sold its "first 500 tickets in the first 30 days". That is a 2015-era figure, cited to illustrate scarcity rather than as a current benchmark. A first-N-tickets or dated cutoff gives the beat a hook.

## 3. Who covers developer events, mapped by beat

- **Mainstream tech and trade outlets** cluster around the lineup/schedule and day-of beats.
- **Local and regional business press** covers economic-impact and attendance angles, mostly post-event.
- **Independent creators and podcasters** increasingly cover both live and post-event, and organizers treat them as a growing constituency distinct from staff journalists - which is why the accreditation policy needs a second lane.

## 4. Writing the beat pitch (unsourced template)

⚠️ **Unsourced template. No named event's observed practice stands behind these steps.** The five steps and the negative example below are constructed from the beat table above and ordinary pitching practice. Treat them as reasoned, not observed, and say so when handing them to an organizer.

1. **Lead with what changed**, not with the event. "First edition in this city", "the keynote is X", "the CFP drew N submissions" are news; "our conference is happening" is not.
2. **Attach the fact sheet and the boilerplate**, so the journalist's least-effort path produces the sentence you wanted.
3. **Say the date the embargo lifts, with a timezone**, or say plainly that there is none.
4. **Name the one person who can answer follow-ups** - the designated voice, not the general inbox.
5. **Send it once.** The one asset this skill accumulates is a journalist's willingness to open the next email, and a second nudge on a beat they already declined spends it.

**Negative example** - do not pitch: a schedule page going live on its planned date, framed as an exclusive. It is a publication date, not news, and pitching it teaches the recipient that your subject lines overstate.
