# Insurance coverage types

What each coverage category does, what it pointedly does not do, and what to ask a broker.

**Not insurance advice.** Every carrier, limit and premium band below is an illustrative example of what a coverage category looks like in one market, drawn from broker and vendor marketing material.

None is a quote, a recommendation, or a claim of availability in the user's jurisdiction. Whether a policy responds to a specific loss is a question for a licensed broker reading the actual wording.

## Commercial general liability - the baseline that covers none of your own losses

Third-party bodily injury and property damage. This is the cover venues contractually require, usually with the venue, lessor or municipality named as an additional insured, and often with host-liquor liability added where alcohol is served.

Illustrative limits:

- $1M per occurrence with $2M-$3M aggregate, steppable to $2M/$4M.
- One university's conference services requires $1M/$2M plus a $5M umbrella.
- Exhibitor-booth premiums in the roughly $79-$175 range.

The critical point: general liability explicitly does **not** cover cancellation, lost revenue, or any of the organizer's own financial loss. Organizers who assume "we have insurance" mean this policy and discover the gap at the worst moment.

## Event cancellation and postponement - the organizer's own financial loss

Covers net financial loss - lost revenue, committed non-recoverable expenses, or both - when the event is cancelled, abandoned, interrupted, curtailed, postponed or relocated for a covered cause beyond the organizer's control. Some forms extend to "enforced reduced attendance". Carriers writing this class include Tokio Marine HCC.

**Standard exclusions, every one of which is a real gap:**

- Voluntary cancellation.
- Poor ticket sales or lack of interest.
- The organizer's own financial failure.
- Pre-existing or already-known circumstances.
- Failure to secure permits.
- Contractual disputes.
- Communicable disease, unless specifically endorsed.

Premiums typically run 1-3% of the total insured event budget. Large events bind 12-18 months out. Disputes in this class turn on whether the loss was genuinely "beyond the policyholder's control".

### The communicable-disease buy-back

Insurers excluded this peril market-wide from late January 2020 onward. It is available since only as a specifically negotiated endorsement - the "buy-back" - and it is the single decision on this whole ladder most likely to determine whether a policy pays when it matters. Two things follow:

- Ask for it explicitly and price it. Never assume a cancellation policy includes it.
- A government shutdown order does **not** rescue coverage where the exclusion applies. The exclusion attaches to the peril, not to who ordered the closure. One festival organizer confirmed publicly that despite carrying terrorism, injury, property and weather cover, the pandemic exclusion left them uninsured for the actual cause.

Record the decision either way, with its reason. A later organizer needs to know it was considered and declined, not merely absent.

## Non-appearance / key-speaker

Indemnifies revenue and expenses when the event is cancelled or curtailed because a named essential individual dies, is injured, falls ill, or is delayed in transit. For a technical conference this maps to a marquee keynote the program and the ticket sales were built on.

Typically must be bound at least 14 days before the event. Extensions can add adverse weather, terrorism, and computer-systems failure. Carriers in this class include Tokio Marine HCC and Howden.

Only worth buying where a single named person genuinely carries the event's value - a balanced multi-track program is not exposed this way.

## Adverse weather, including parametric

Two structures:

- **Indemnity**, inside a cancellation policy: pays proven loss, requires an adjuster.
- **Parametric**: pays a pre-agreed sum when a defined index crosses a trigger measured at a named station - rainfall, wind speed, temperature. No proof of loss, no adjuster, fast settlement (one provider cites funds within 72 hours; a Lloyd's example settled in 14 days).

Illustrative parametric triggers run from 0.10" (light) to 0.50" (heavy) rain during defined "key event hours". One indicative quote: a festival-day 0.25" rain trigger paying $50k, priced around $1,895-$3,500. A broker example paired a "10mm rainfall" trigger against an anticipated 25% reduction in on-site spending.

The effort here is real and sits before the premium: choosing the index, agreeing the measuring station, and negotiating a trigger that actually correlates with the loss. A trigger that never fires in a bad year is worse than no cover, because it was paid for.

## Participant accident

Blanket accident-medical cover (optionally with accidental death and dismemberment) for attendees, participants and volunteers injured during a sponsored activity. Pays medical expenses regardless of fault, and is typically excess over the injured person's own health cover. Carriers writing this class include Mutual of Omaha, Chubb, The Hartford, Philadelphia Insurance Companies, K&K and Arch.

It complements general liability specifically because general liability frequently does not pay medical expenses. Directly relevant to hackathons, hardware events, and anything multi-day or physically active.

## TULIP - tenant/user liability

A venue-side program letting a third-party event host without its own general liability policy buy short-term cover through the facility's master policy, automatically naming the venue as an additional insured. Providers include Philadelphia Insurance Companies (marketing premiums "as low as $100 per event"), Tokio Marine HCC, K&K (with attendance caps around 3,000, up to 10 consecutive days, single US location) and Gallagher. Several universities require it of outside groups.

Covers bodily injury and property damage, optionally host liquor; excludes certain high-risk activities. For a community event using a university or municipal venue, this is frequently the entire insurance answer - the whole ladder collapses to this one rung.

## Cyber, for registration systems and attendee data

First-party breach response (forensics, notification, credit monitoring, ransomware, data restoration, PR) plus third-party liability (privacy claims, regulatory defense). Illustrative pricing: roughly $150-$500/year for a small vendor policy; $25-$75 for a one-day event policy.

Watch for "silent cyber" gaps - a general liability or property policy may simply not respond to a registration-platform breach. If the event self-hosts registration or holds an attendee list, this rung is cheap and the exposure is real. If a third-party ticketing platform holds all of it, ask who carries that risk contractually before buying anything.

## Questions to put to a broker

1. Does the cancellation form include a communicable-disease buy-back, and what does it cost?
2. What are the binding deadlines for each cover, and which have already passed for our date?
3. What must we disclose as a "known circumstance" today, and what voids the policy if we don't?
4. Does the venue's certificate requirement demand an additional-insured endorsement, and by what date?
5. Does general liability respond to attendee medical expenses, or do we need participant accident cover?
6. Who carries the risk on our registration platform - us, or the platform?
7. For any parametric trigger: which station measures it, over which hours, and what would it have paid in each of the last five years?
