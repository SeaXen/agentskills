# Naming rungs, disclaimer wording, and exposure routing

The four naming rungs as sentences you can actually send, what a non-affiliation line has to contain, and the exposure categories with the person each goes to.

## The four rungs as wording

Send the rung, not a paragraph about the rung. Each of these goes in the approval response and is repeated on whatever the satellite organizer publishes.

**No use of the event's name.** They may state facts about time and place and nothing else. The satellite's own page says when and where it is, and never what it is near. This is the rung that costs you discoverability entirely, and it is the honest one where you will not review anybody's materials.

Two named conferences run exactly this rung as enforced policy, not as a fallback.

- **SXSW.** Its trademark guidelines state that use of the SXSW Marks "is not permitted to promote participation in any events that are not officially affiliated with SXSW," ban "Variation Marks" built from the name outright (Soul By Soul West, SXSA, the South By Party, South by [Name]), and hand organizers a substitute name for the case where they still want to say where their event sits: "ATX Spring Festival" or "Spring Fest ATX" (SXSW Trademark Guidelines).
- **CES**, under its own name for the rung, "outboarding": the Consumer Technology Association defines it as any activation "created and deployed outside the scope of CES but promoted or implied as being a part of CES," and backs the rule with consequences an organizer actually feels - loss of priority points for future CES space, exposure of the offending company to CTA members, media, customers and shareholders, and loss of CTA membership (CES Outboarding Policy).

RE+ Events runs an adjacent policy under the same "Suitcasing/Outboarding" name, but it restricts exhibitors from _hosting or attending a competing event during official show hours_ - a calendar-exclusivity rule, not a name-usage rule - and is cited for that reason under Calendar deconfliction depth instead, not here.

Neither policy leans on a disclaimer sentence; each removes the name and replaces it with enforcement. Read that as a statement about what a registered-mark portfolio, plus a penalty an organizer can actually make stick, buys you. It is not a verdict against the disclaimer rung below: most events this skill is written for have neither, which is one reason the default sits one rung up.

**Factual reference only.** They may name your event inside a plain factual sentence and use no marks: no logo, no lockup, no colour scheme, no word suggesting official status. `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@cross-event-promotion` describes this same shape from the requesting side, a smaller event positioning itself as running "the day after [anchor] ends, same city" with no agreement at all. The bracketed placeholder is that wording's, not a variable of yours.

KubeCon + CloudNativeCon enforces a related shape by format rather than by sentence. A sponsor-hosted co-located event's own title must read "Event Name hosted by Company Name," never the conference's own name standing alone, and any project name inside that title still has to clear the Linux Foundation's own trademark guide (Linux Foundation Events, KubeCon + CloudNativeCon sponsor-hosted co-located event guide).

Watch what a factual sentence quietly becomes. _During_ your event is factual. _Part of_ your event is not. _Official_ is not. _Pre-conference_ is not, because it claims a relationship to your programme. Say which words are out, rather than describing a spirit.

**Mandatory unofficial-disclaimer wording.** They may name your event and must carry your sentence wherever they do. One sentence, written once by you, identical everywhere, supplied in the approval response so nobody drafts their own version of it.

**Licensed co-branding.** A named grantee, a named purpose, a stated end date, and a requirement that materials be shown to you before they are printed. Only where somebody clearly holds the marks and has agreed to grant.

## The disclaimer's five components

A non-affiliation line that omits any of these leaves the reader to fill the gap, and readers fill it in your favour, which is the direction that hurts you.

1. **Who runs it** - named, not implied by absence.
2. **Who does not** - your event, stated as a negative rather than left unmentioned.
3. **What you did not do** - not organised, not vetted for content, not staffed by you. Choose honestly: if you _did_ vet it against criteria, do not claim you did not.
4. **Where to complain** - the satellite organizer's contact, not yours. A disclaimer that gives no alternative address sends the complaint to you anyway.
5. **What still applies anyway** - if you made code-of-conduct adoption an approval condition, say so, because a blanket denial of responsibility contradicts a condition you actually imposed.

Component 5 is the one most often dropped, and dropping it is what makes the other four read as a lawyer's reflex rather than a description of a real arrangement.

Research on whether a disclaimer sentence actually does this job is not encouraging. The trademark-specific literature found it unreliable at curing the confusion it is meant to cure (Jacoby, Nelson and Hoyer, "Disclaimers in Trademark Infringement Litigation: More Trouble Than They Are Worth?", Trademark Reporter, 1986, and the same authors' "Why Disclaimers Fail", Trademark Reporter, 1994).

A broader review of advertising disclaimers reaches the same conclusion at larger scale: a meta-analysis of eighteen controlled studies found a mandated disclaimer ineffective or actively harmful to the reader's judgment in fifteen of them, and the authors' own experiment recorded a disclaimer making participants more likely to pick the wrong option, not less (Green and Armstrong, "Evidence on the Effects of Mandatory Disclaimers in Advertising", Journal of Public Policy & Marketing, 2012). Neither line is event-specific; both come from general advertising and trademark research rather than from event-industry practice.

Write the five components anyway. The alternative the research points toward is banning the name outright, and most events lack the registered-mark portfolio and enforcement leverage SXSW and CES have to make that stick.

## The licence shape

`samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-sponsor-agreement` records a real contract granting "a limited license to use Sponsor's trade name, trademarks, and service marks for the purpose of promoting Sponsor's sponsorship." (CFA Institute sponsorship contract, recorded in that skill's clause library). Three properties transfer to a satellite-event grant, and they are the whole of what makes a licence safer than silence.

- It is **limited**.
- It names the **marks** rather than gesturing at branding.
- It is **purpose-bound** to one use.

The same sibling adds two qualifiers it applies to sponsor marks, and they apply identically here.

- Use is subject to your brand guidelines.
- The licence expires automatically rather than continuing silently.

A grant with no end date is a grant for every future edition, and the person who made it will have left the team.

## Exposure categories and routing

Name each live category, say every time that the specific rule is jurisdiction-specific, and route. Never answer.

- **Use of your name and marks by somebody you do not control.** Route to whoever holds the marks - the organizing entity, a parent body or foundation - and to counsel where a grant is being made or a misuse is being challenged.
- **Apparent endorsement created by listing or promoting an event you did not run.** This is the one the listing rungs create and the one an organizer least expects. Route to counsel, and mitigate immediately with the framing and the disclaimer above.
- **What happens to a person at a third party's venue.** Route to counsel, and separately to `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-risk-management` if you decide to carry it as a scored register row rather than only as a disclaimer.
- **Alcohol served by somebody else.** `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-hospitality` already treats your own alcohol posture as pulling host-liquor liability into the insurance question. Somebody else's bar is a different party's exposure and possibly also yours if you sent people to it. Route to counsel.
- **Personal data a satellite organizer collects** from people who reached them through your listing - an RSVP list, a ticket sale. Route to whoever holds the organization's privacy obligations, the same route `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-code-of-conduct` uses for incident records.

Where an umbrella or franchise agreement governs your event, check what it already says about events run around yours before routing anything; the answer may not be yours to give.

## The standing policy

This skill states no legal threshold, no jurisdiction, no form or instrument name, and no monetary figure. That is a standing policy rather than an omission to be filled in later. `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@hackathon-cash-prize` holds the identical policy for prize law and states the reason this file adopts unchanged - a plausible-sounding rule in a document telling an organizer what they are responsible for is worse than no guidance at all, because it is confident enough to be relied on and wrong often enough to matter.

What this file does instead is name the categories, say that the specific rule is jurisdiction-specific, and route.
