# On-site speaker care

The packet, the briefing, the tech check, the green room, the host, and the dinner. This section covers how to deliver the promised speaker experience on the day, with practical operating detail for each element.

## The speaker packet

One document or one page, linked from every message, updated in place rather than re-sent as versions.

- **Your slot** - date, start and end time, room, format, length, and how strictly the length is enforced.
- **Who introduces you**, and what they will say. Ask for the name pronunciation and the pronouns to use, and use them.
- **Every deadline**, with the reason behind each and the person to tell if one will slip.
- **The recording and publication policy for this speaker's format** - including where no opt-out is offered, which has to be stated plainly in advance rather than discovered.
- **Travel, lodging and reimbursement mechanics**, or an explicit "not covered" where that is the answer.
- **Arrival instructions** - where the door is, what time to be there, who to ask for, what the badge situation is, and where the speaker room is.
- **What the room is like** - capacity, layout, whether there is a lectern, whether questions happen, whether there is a confidence monitor, how time signals are given.
- **The code of conduct**, and how to report something.
- **The contact** - one name, one address, one stated response window, plus a same-day channel for the event days themselves.

Write it once and reuse it every edition. Most of it does not change, and the parts that do are the parts a speaker most needs to be current.

## Briefing

The packet is the briefing for most speakers. Add a live briefing - a short call or a session on arrival - for:

- Keynotes.
- First-time speakers.
- Anyone whose session has unusual mechanics: a panel, a co-presented talk, a live demo with a hardware dependency.

Where the event's community has an established orientation course for speakers, recommend it. It costs you nothing to link, it carries more authority than a paragraph you wrote, and it covers ground - inclusive language, accessible slides - that an organizer's logistics briefing usually skips.

Never use a briefing to renegotiate the talk. Content feedback ended at selection. This is logistics.

## Tech check

Run it on the actual stage, with the actual equipment, before the audience is in the room. A tech check in a side room proves nothing about the projector that matters.

1. Connect the speaker's own machine to the real projector or screen and confirm the resolution and aspect ratio look right.
2. Test the microphone the speaker will actually use, at speaking volume, with the room's own gain settings.
3. Play audio if the talk has any, through the room's system.
4. Run the demo's riskiest moment - the network call, the SSH session, the hardware - not the whole demo.
5. Show the clicker, the confidence monitor and the timing signals, and let the speaker hold each one.
6. Point at the exit, the water, and the person who will be in the room if something breaks.
7. Agree what happens if the demo fails live: skip to a slide, show a recording, or talk through it.

Schedule it at a named time in each speaker's cadence. "Come find us before your talk" produces a queue of three speakers in the last ten minutes before the first session.

## Green room

The list below is a working default, and a small event with no spare room should not manufacture guilt about lacking it.

The purpose is a room where a speaker can be not-on. That single purpose decides everything else: it is not the organizer office, not a sponsor lounge, and not where the volunteer briefing happens.

- **Quiet, and lockable if possible.** A speaker needs somewhere to leave a bag and somewhere to be unobserved for ten minutes.
- **Power, wifi and a table** - the last-minute slide fix happens here whether you plan for it or not.
- **Water and something to eat** that does not require leaving. A speaker whose slot lands over lunch will otherwise not eat.
- **A clock showing the real time**, and the current schedule, ideally with slippage marked.
- **Somebody who knows what is happening**, at least intermittently - the host, or a named volunteer who can find them.
- **A stated norm about who else may enter.** The room stops working the moment it becomes a networking space. Say so in the packet and mean it.
- **Where there is no green room**, say that too, and name the alternative: a reserved table, a quiet corner, a locked cupboard for bags. An honest substitute beats a promised room that turns out to be the sponsor lounge.

## The on-site host

The pre-event contact and the on-site host should be the same person or pair. Split it only if team size forces it, and introduce the second person by name in advance rather than at the door.

Their duties, in order of the day:

1. Know each speaker's arrival time before the day starts, and notice when one has not appeared.
2. Meet them at arrival, hand over the badge, and walk them to the speaker room rather than pointing.
3. Confirm the tech-check time face to face.
4. Be findable in the twenty minutes before each slot - that is the window where problems surface.
5. Walk the speaker to their room, hand them to the MC or introducer, and confirm the timing signals one last time.
6. Be in the room, or reachable from it, during the talk.
7. Afterwards: get them out of the corridor conversation when they want out, and tell them the talk went well if it did.

The host holds the speaker's experience of the day. Minute-by-minute conduct of the event itself belongs to the run-of-show document, and speaker timing needs are an input handed to it rather than a schedule written here.

## The speakers' dinner

A real, budgeted expense line - "hosting a speakers' dinner and small appreciation gifts". It does two things the free ticket does not:

- Thanks people out loud.
- Lets speakers meet each other, which is most of why experienced speakers say yes again.

- **Ask about dietary needs when you collect everything else**, not at the table.
- **Invite the organizers who did the work**, not the sponsors. If sponsors attend, say so in the invitation - a speaker who expected peers and finds a sales conversation was misled.
- **The gift matters less than the fact of one.** Something local and small reads better than something expensive and branded.
- **A speaker who cannot attend has not declined the thanks.** Say thank you separately.
- **No budget is a legitimate answer.** A drink after the last session, or coffee before the doors open, does most of the same work.
