# Ladder design mechanics

The design rules behind workflow step 5 and the architecture menu.

## Tier count and ratio

- Tier count:
  - Two tiers: a minimal viable card
  - Three tiers: the standard shape
  - Four tiers: works only when the fourth is a real persona tier
  - Five tiers: one tier too many at any scale

  The SaaS guidance that four-plus tiers risks decision paralysis matches that 2-4 range exactly.

- Top:floor ratio: the published community range is 2:1 to 7.5:1 with 4-5:1 modal; the best-tier rule says the top should be 2-3x the entry. Report both with the caveat: community sponsorship runs hotter because the floor tier (logo plus a ticket) has near-zero marginal cost to the organizer, where a SaaS entry tier still carries serve cost. Pick inside the published range; justify leaving it.
- Space tiers so each step-up buys a visible benefit jump, not just a bigger number - a step that only changes the price is the mid-tier dead zone the fill-pattern measurement catches.

## Anchoring and decoy placement

- Present the top tier first - it anchors every price beneath it.
- Make the middle tier the visibly best value and the default the card points at; the top tier partly exists to make the middle look reasonable.
- The floor tier's job is entry and social proof (logo wall density), not revenue.

## Feature-gating map

The four SaaS gating levers map one-to-one onto sponsorship's physical and attention scarcity. Gate every tier on these axes and no others:

| SaaS lever           | Sponsorship lever           | Gating examples from real cards                                                              |
| -------------------- | --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Feature access       | Floor presence + stage time | shared table → full table → none below the floor; pitch minutes only at the top, hard-capped |
| Usage limits         | Ticket allotment            | counts stepping down tier by tier; each pass priced as a component                           |
| Support level        | Organizer attention         | named contact, intro-making, priority placement choices                                      |
| Access/customization | Branding prominence         | dedicated vs shared slide, logo size/position class                                          |

## Persona tiers

A persona tier serves a different sponsor type at a different willingness-to-pay, with a benefit set shaped for them - a startup alley with lighter benefits, a free community tier reserved for non-commercial local orgs (logo and a ticket, no table, no pitch). It is packaging by buyer, not a discount rung. The free community tier is a reserved category, never a freemium conversion funnel - nothing about it upsells.

## The booth price floor

Floor presence begins at a hard tier; there is no cheap route onto the floor beneath it, however many logo-only tiers exist below. This protects the top tiers' core scarce benefit and keeps the floor from filling with sponsors who paid logo money.

## Custom-pricing triggers

Drop a line to "contact us" instead of a fixed price when:

- The slot is genuinely custom (an evening event, a bespoke activation)
- The fixed slot sold out (real cards switch sold-out add-ons to "contact us")
- The deal needs a custom contract

Everything else keeps a printed number - at single-event scale a mostly-unpriced card stalls deals behind organizer availability.

## Scarcity mechanics

- Cap scarce inventory visibly on the card: "capped at 3", "one available".
- When a surface sells out, strike it through and leave it on the page - live scarcity plus social proof for what remains.
- A sellout is the repricing signal for next edition's equivalent surface: price it higher, never lower.

## Deadline structure

- Sponsors sign roughly a fiscal quarter before the event; foundation-scale prospectuses make contracts and add-on purchases due on that horizon. Set the card's own deadlines to that lead time - sponsors do not decide on ticket-buyer timescales.
- Place the early-bird cutoff (if the discount menu picked one) where it catches budget lock-in, on the top tier only, as a dated price rather than a percentage banner.

## Edition-over-edition price rises

The SaaS rollout discipline transposes as a guess for raising a returning sponsor's tier price:

- Test the new price on new sponsors first
- Treat any grandfathering as a dated transition rather than a permanent rate
- Communicate the rise with the post-event report, months ahead, with the why

## What does not transpose from SaaS pricing

- **Per-seat or metered value metrics** - a sponsorship is a flat package, not a consumption meter; do not force a per-attendee metered framing onto a card.
- **Freemium and free-trial mechanics** - a sponsor doesn't trial a tier; the free community tier is a reserved category, not a funnel.
- **Willingness-to-pay survey methods** (Van Westendorp, MaxDiff) - they need hundreds of prospective buyers; no single event has that pool. Comparable events' published cards are the practical substitute.
- **Pricing-page conversion/AI-readability optimization** - a rate card ships as a prospectus page or PDF and closes by email; page-teardown tactics have no purchase here.
