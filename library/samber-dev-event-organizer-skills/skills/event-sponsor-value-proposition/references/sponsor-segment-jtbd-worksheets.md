# Sponsor-segment JTBD worksheets

The six-part value-proposition template below applies a jobs-to-be-done structure to the sponsor as the "customer." Everything sponsor-specific in the worksheets - title signals, motivations, honest How content - comes from the event-organizer material (MLH hackathon organizer guide, DevOpsDays organizing guide), labeled inline.

## The six-part template

Fill it for **one segment at a time**, in order:

1. **Who** - the sponsor segment: a named budget-holding role, not a company. "Sponsors" is not a Who.
2. **Why** - the segment's job to be done: what progress are they trying to make (ship a better product via real user feedback, fill a hiring pipeline, shift brand perception, stay visible in a community they depend on).
3. **What Before** - what they do today without this event: a booth at a bigger vendor conference, paid job-board ads, cold outreach to the same audience, a generic brand campaign. Naming the status quo is what makes the event's value legible instead of assumed.
4. **How** - what the event genuinely delivers against that job, bounded by the pole (community-run events cannot promise lead lists or purchased slots). Every How claim needs a proof point.
5. **What After** - the sponsor's improved state: a warmer hiring pipeline, a validated roadmap decision, a name recognized inside the community that matters to them.
6. **Alternatives** - the other events, channels, or sponsorship products competing for the same budget line, and why this event wins _for this segment_. This section forces the statement to confront substitutes instead of asserting value in a vacuum.

Why this template over a value-proposition canvas (Strategyzer/Osterwalder): the canvas starts from the offer side (pain relievers, gain creators) and maps several segments at once - which invites the exact failure this skill guards against, starting from "what we're selling" and blending segments. The six-part template forces one segment and ends in a reusable statement, the shape the prospectus and rate card need as input.

## Segment worksheets

Title signals and motivation notes are from MLH's organizer guide ("Understanding your sponsors") unless labeled otherwise. Pole adjustments apply the honesty rules and are judged rather than established practice.

### Product-feedback buyer

- **Who**: Developer Relations / Developer Advocate titles. Budget-holding, already familiar with technical events - but a narrower, harder-to-find role (MLH).
- **Why**: real-time feedback on their products, "especially APIs and development tools" (MLH); routed internally to Product (feature validation, beta recruits) or Engineering (bug reports) - the DQL routing pattern (via Mary Thengvall).
- **Before**: feature surveys, beta programs with low-engagement participants, support-ticket signal.
- **How** (both poles): structured contact with a technical, opinionated audience - workshop slots at a hackathon, a staffed table where attendees bring real use cases, office hours. Community pole: conversations only, volunteered contact.
- **After**: a validated (or invalidated) product decision, named beta users, direct quotes for the roadmap discussion.
- **Alternatives**: a bigger vendor conference (broader but shallower contact), their own user conference (existing users only - no outside perspective).

### Recruiting buyer

- **Who**: Hiring / Recruiting / Talent Acquisition / HR titles. Budget-holding, but need more explanation of what the event actually is (MLH - budget for this in the outreach handoff).
- **Why**: interns and full-time hires, "especially during the fall season" for hackathons (MLH); a candidate pool pre-filtered for the exact skills they struggle to source.
- **Before**: job boards, agency fees, cold LinkedIn outreach with low response.
- **How** - pole-dependent: community pole, conversation-based only (a staffed table, office hours, being visibly present as engineers rather than recruiters) - never a resume database or scanned list; vendor pole, may add consented contact capture.
- **After**: a warm, self-selected shortlist that already met the team; employer-brand lift inside the target population.
- **Alternatives**: career fairs (volume without self-selection), sponsored job-board placement (no conversation).

### Brand buyer

- **Who**: Marketing / PR titles, or an Employee Resource Group tied to the event's demographic. Persuaded more by photos and evidence of past events than by a pitch alone (MLH).
- **Why**: raise awareness or _change the perception_ of the brand/product with a technical audience (MLH). Events sit in the awareness family by default (via Phil Leggetter's AAARRRP model).
- **Before**: paid media the audience blocks or distrusts, generic developer-marketing campaigns.
- **How**: visibility inventory (stage mentions, signage, content) _plus_ the association value - at a community event, sponsoring signals the company subscribes to the community's ideas ("it amplifies the ideas and shows that many companies subscribe" - DevOpsDays).
- **After**: measured recall or perception shift inside a population paid channels can't reach credibly.
- **Alternatives**: developer-media advertising, content sponsorships, a bigger conference where the logo is one of forty.

### Community-member sponsor

- **Who**: a practitioner, founder, or team inside the community itself - many sponsor contacts are community members ("many of them work at sponsors!" - DevOpsDays). Often a small first-time sponsorship.
- **Why**: belonging and standing, supporting infrastructure they depend on, staying close to where their field is going - the JetBrains/PyCon renewal logic: support the community, meet existing users, sense what's coming (PSF prospectus testimonial; one vendor's stated reason, not a benchmark).
- **Before**: individual participation without company visibility; watching a competitor's logo on the community they helped build.
- **How**: named support of a thing they already value, peer-level presence, market intelligence from the hallway track.
- **After**: standing inside the community, existing-user relationships maintained, early signal on ecosystem direction.
- **Alternatives**: OSS sponsorships, meetup hosting - often complements rather than substitutes; say so honestly if the event is not their best channel.

## Sponsor-type taxonomy (who is paying, and how)

Five types from MLH's organizer guide; one company can span several. The segment worksheets above describe the _buyer_; the type describes the _deal shape_ - note both in the statement.

- **Financial/monetary** - pays cash; the most common type and the default assumption of the worksheets.
- **Presenting** - co-hosts/co-brands; a premium arrangement the guide warns not to over-concede control. The value story must still never include program or judging influence.
- **In-kind** - donates goods/services (hardware, platform credits). Evaluate case by case: the guide flags in-kind offers as a common attempt to avoid a cash fee.
- **Strategic/media partner** - promotion help, often publications; value flows both ways, usually no cash.
- **Community partner** - helps recruit attendees, mentors, judges, volunteers, typically free; goodwill economy, not a sales target.

## Worked example: statement pair

Every figure in the pair below is invented - the event, the headcount and the seniority split illustrate the shape of a statement, never a benchmark to reuse.

**Positive** (recruiting buyer, community-run 300-person conference, second edition):

> For early-talent leads hiring backend engineers (Who) who need candidates with real distributed-systems exposure (Why) and today burn budget on job boards with low signal (Before), sponsoring gives your engineers a staffed table and two office-hours slots in front of ~300 attendees - 60% senior+ per last edition's registration data; no attendee list is provided, conversations are the channel (How, evidenced + honesty rule stated). You leave with a self-selected shortlist that has already met your team (After). A career fair gives you volume without self-selection; a bigger conference gives you reach without this seniority mix (Alternatives).

**Negative** (what this skill must never produce):

> Sponsoring gives you premium exposure to hundreds of top engineers, qualified leads from our engaged audience, and the chance to get your product in front of decision-makers - plus a speaking slot to tell your story.

Every failure in one sentence: no segment, unverifiable superlatives instead of composition data, a lead promise a community event can't honor, and a purchasable slot organizer guides forbid outright.
