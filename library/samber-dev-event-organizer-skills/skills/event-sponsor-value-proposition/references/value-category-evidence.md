# Value-category evidence

The evidence behind the value categories the skill maps benefits against, and the honesty constraint bounding them.

## Three named frameworks agree on the same category set

Brand, product feedback, and recruiting recur across all three, which is why the category set holds:

- **DQL routing** (Mary Thengvall): a sponsorship conversation routes internally to Marketing (case study/brand), Product (feedback, beta), Engineering (bug fix), BizDev (partnership), Recruiting (candidate), or Sales (customer) - never all of them. Thengvall's own position is that DevRel should never carry a sales metric - organizer-side proof that product feedback and community goodwill are as real a value category as leads, not a softer consolation prize.
- **AAARRRP goal mapping** (Phil Leggetter): events and conference talks sit in the Awareness/Acquisition family by default - the corrective against pitching every sponsorship as pipeline when the honest primary payoff is visibility.
- **MLH's three primary motivations** (MLH hackathon organizer guide): product feedback ("real-time feedback on their products, especially APIs and development tools"), recruiting ("interns and full-time employees, especially during the fall season"), brand ("raise awareness (or change the perception) of their brand/product or service"). Secondary reasons named in the same guide: community-values alignment, increased product engagement, and region-targeted goals (companies with a local campus presence wanting event-specific stats to justify budget).

A sponsor's own budget review also tends to score against a five-objective split:

- Awareness
- Adoption/Activation
- Pipeline
- Ecosystem/Partners
- Recruiting

Keep every claimed benefit legible against one of those five plus goodwill: that is how the sponsor will judge, and renew, what was promised.

## Community goodwill is real, and distinct from pipeline

Jodie Burch, JetBrains, quoted in the PSF/PyCon US 2026 prospectus: "PyCon US is an unmissable event for the PyCharm team. It's our chance to support the Python community and PSF, catch up with our friends and users, and get a sense of what's coming up in the Python world." That is community support, existing-user relationship maintenance, and market intelligence - three genuine value categories, none reducible to a lead count. Treat it as one testimonial illustrating a real category, not a universal renewal driver: one vendor's stated reason, not an industry benchmark.

DevOpsDays frames the same category as a deliberate pitch angle, not an afterthought: "The more companies that sponsor, the better: it amplifies the ideas and shows that many companies subscribe to the devops idea." The value on offer is association with ideas the sponsor already holds - not reach. And the guide's note that "many of them work at sponsors!" (about fellow organizers) means sponsors at a community event are frequently drawn from the community's own membership: belonging, not just marketing spend.

## The honesty constraint, in the organizers' own words

DevOpsDays' organizing guide states the rule twice: "They are never given attendee contact info by a devopsdays event's organizers, nor are they allowed to purchase speaking slots" - and again under its sponsorship packages: "we do NOT offer speaker slots in return for sponsorship... we do NOT ever give out or sell lists with contact details of attendees. You can share demographics in aggregate to give sponsors a feel for what kind of crowd will attend." Note the pattern: sponsors are told what they will _not_ get before what they will - copy it.

The opposite pole is equally real: vendor- and foundation-run conferences (KubeCon-style) offer consented badge scanning that returns name, title, company, and contact details to the sponsor. Pipeline claims are honest there, bounded by consent. The pole is a property of the event, not of the sponsor's wishes.

Editorial boundary, both poles: "No sponsor should be able to buy their way into the program" (DevOpsDays). Money buys placement and access - never a keynote's content, a judging result, or roadmap influence. A statement implying otherwise is a false claim, and the organizer guides state the rule as hard, not negotiable.

## The sponsor's side of the table

Two lists worth answering before any statement ships:

- **The three questions sponsors always ask** (DevOpsDays - have them ready before any outreach): "What audience will attend? Why should they sponsor? What they get with what level of sponsorship?" The first two are this skill's deliverable; the third belongs to the prospectus but must be derivable from the statement. Real organizers converge on this Who/Why/How shape independently of any named framework.
- **The four practical concerns before saying yes** (MLH):
  - Attendee Value: is this audience a fit, and how hard would reaching it be without the event?
  - who else is sponsoring: competitor co-presence cuts both ways
  - Package Value: are they getting their money's worth?
  - "The Force": gut feel about organizer competency and professionalism to date - the last one means every interaction before the pitch is already part of the value proposition

One framing note from MLH's guide: "You should consider this as a sales pitch for your hackathon" - the value proposition is a persuasion document built on true claims, not a neutral inventory.

## Sponsor demand follows the hiring market

Treat it as field experience, not established practice, and check it against the organizer's own sponsor history.

- **Two buying reasons dominate.** Local tech-event sponsors pay either to recruit tech staff or to sell to tech teams. Most other categories in this file ride on one of those two budgets.
- **Recruiting sponsors leave first.** When hiring slows or budgets shrink, the talent budget is cut before the marketing or DevRel budget, so the recruiting segment shrinks fastest.
- **Booths stop selling before visibility does.** A booth costs the sponsor staff days on top of the fee - the denominator this skill already tells you to name. In a tight year, logo and digital visibility still sells while booth-led packages stall.
- **Implication for the proposition.** Read the segment's current hiring pressure before choosing recruiting as co-lead, and give every segment a staff-free way to buy. The pricing consequence (a staff-free visibility tier) belongs to `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-sponsor-pricing`.

Signals of hiring pressure the organizer can check without paid data, all self-read:

- Open tech roles posted by the companies on the prospect list, compared with the same period last year.
- Whether past recruiting sponsors still send talent-acquisition contacts or have gone silent.
- Attendee-side signal: more job-seekers and fewer hiring managers in the room, as reported by the organizers themselves.
