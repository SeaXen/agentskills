# Program-fit checks

None is a measured finding or established practice. Present each one as a field heuristic, and replace it with your own edition's evidence as soon as you have some.

## Lesson-transfer test

Applies to any proposal from a vendor, a start-up, or a speaker whose employer sells into the audience.

1. Strip the product name from the abstract.
2. Ask what the audience would still walk away with: a lesson, a failure story, a method, an inspiration.
3. If something transferable remains, the talk passes, whoever the speaker works for.
4. If nothing remains, or the talk centres on a live product demo, it fails at review. Route it to a sponsor or open-space slot if the structure has one (`samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-cfp-design` publishes that routing).

Why the test, rather than a vendor ban: start-up founders are often the people living a problem first, and practitioners report that their lessons are among the most useful talks a local meetup hosts. The failure is the product as takeaway, not the employer.

### Commercial-content rule for the acceptance email

Add one line to every acceptance, after the "what we need next" line:

> One rule for every speaker: no commercial demo or sales pitch. Name your product once if it matters to the story; the audience should leave with the lesson, not the pitch.

`samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-speaker-experience` repeats the same line in the speaker packet, so the speaker hears it twice before building the deck.

## Topic freshness

- A fast-moving field like generative AI turns last year's hot topic stale within months.
- Review runs weeks or months before the event. A proposal accurate at submission can be outdated on stage.
- Check every "state of the art", "latest" or "what's new" promise against the event date.
- Hold a self-set number of slots unfilled after review. Fill them by invitation close to the event, through `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-speaker-sourcing`, on whatever broke in the news cycle.
- Tell the committee which slots are held, so the cut rule is computed on the remaining count.

## Depth gradient for a mixed room

The problem: one evening, one track, an audience mixing developers, curious non-technical attendees and a few specialists. Splitting into two tracks carries a risk: it worked somewhat, but attendees got lost choosing between rooms.

The alternative is a depth gradient, at two scales:

- **Within each talk** (a "funnel talk"): open where nearly everyone follows - the problem, the stakes, a concrete example. End on material most of the room cannot follow - the internals, the numbers, the code. If the speaker did not lose most of the room by the end, the talk did not go deep enough.
- **Across the evening**: order talks from the broadest to the most advanced. Hand the order to `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-schedule-design`.

At review, ask whether a proposal can carry both ends. A pure introduction and a pure deep-dive each serve half the room.

The gradient depends on coaching after acceptance (see brand promise below). Coaching time is often the first thing dropped when organizers are busy, and talk quality suffers as a result.

## Audience level as it is

- Experts: hard to attract to general evening meetups, since family commitments and evening availability compete with a talk they could follow online.
- Regulars skew toward developers moving into the field.
- Read last edition's attendance before setting the level the program targets. Pitch each talk's deep end at the people who actually come.

## Brand promise chain

If the event's positioning promises something every edition - a memorable "wake-up call" moment, a talk people still discuss weeks later - then:

1. Selection applies a shared good-talk framework, written down, so reviewers score against the promise and not only against the topic.
2. Accepted speakers get coaching against the same framework (`samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-speaker-experience`).
3. Talks are rehearsed in front of someone before the event.

Break any link and the promise is luck. If the team lacks coaching capacity for an edition, adjust the promise rather than the program.
