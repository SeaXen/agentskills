# Selection evidence, benchmarks, and what this domain does not document

Contents:

- Acceptance-rate and oversubscription figures.
- Diversity rules and targets.
- The published-criteria baseline selection has to honour.
- The evidence gaps, stated as gaps.

Nothing here is a target - they are calibration points from events whose pool, scale and reputation differ from yours.

## Acceptance rates and oversubscription

| Event                               | Figure                                                                           |
| ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| PyCon US 2025                       | 938 proposals, ~15% selected (≈6.5:1)                                            |
| KubeCon EU 2024                     | 2,541 submissions from 2,746 speakers at 1,228 companies; 223 talks accepted; 9% |
| DjangoCon Europe 2024               | 174 proposals from 138 submitters, 51 first-time speakers                        |
| Established single-track conference | 8-12 speakers per day per track; applicant pools from ~200 to 800-1,200          |
| CHI 2024 (academic)                 | 26.4% acceptance                                                                 |

How to read these:

- **Turning away most submissions is the healthy state**, not a failure. An 8-30x oversubscription at a popular single-track event is normal.
- **The academic number does not transfer.** Peer-reviewed venues accept anything that clears a quality bar; a conference accepts what fits a fixed number of slots in a room. A higher academic acceptance rate is not a looser standard, it is a different question.
- **A ratio that swings hard between your own editions** usually means the call changed - its reach, its deadline, its promotion - not that the community's quality did.
- The demand reading of a high ratio belongs to `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-market-fit` in this collection, not to selection. A high ratio is not a live cue to add a track; the track count was fixed before the call opened.

## Diversity and balance: what is actually published

- **Panel composition, a hard rule**: "All sessions with 3+ speakers must have at least one speaker who does not identify as a man", plus a ban on all panelists sharing one organization. This is a gate applied at selection, not a scoring input, and it is the only hard composition rule documented.
- **Panel review guidance** additionally asks reviewers to assess diverse perspectives across panelists as a scoring input.
- **Quantified event-level targets**: "at least 50% of speakers who are new speakers if possible" and at least 25% from end-user companies. Both are event-level aims; neither appears as a line item in the rubric reviewers actually see. That gap between a stated target and an operationalized one is worth naming when an organizer sets targets of their own.
- **Outreach, not review, is where under-representation is addressed** - one organizer guide states plainly: "under-represented people in tech are much less likely to respond to your CFP". The implication for selection is uncomfortable and worth stating: a balance goal that was never supported by outreach before the call closed cannot be fixed by the review process afterwards, only traded for at the shortlist stage.
- **Local versus out-of-town balance** and **favouring new speakers** are published as stated criteria at the community pole, which makes them legitimate inputs to a program-fit pass rather than a thumb on the scale.

## The published criteria selection must honour

Where the call published criteria, review executes them. One concrete published set is six points stated openly to speakers:

1. "no speaker spots can be bought by sponsors: not ever - period."
2. Encourage new content; already-published material is considered but less attractive.
3. A good balance between local and out-of-town speakers.
4. Favor new speakers.
5. Encourage bold subjects.
6. Route specific technology/product talks to open space rather than the main program.

Point 6 only works if the structure includes an open-space or equivalent block - otherwise it reads as a rejection dressed as a redirect.

Organizer incentives often include "sell tickets, have a great content mix, and sell next year's tickets, in roughly that order", which implies name recognition and topic spread genuinely shape selection. The honest position is to publish criteria you will actually apply, then apply them - not to publish a purer set and quietly run a different one at the shortlist stage.

## What this domain does not document

State these as gaps when the question comes up. Do not fill them with plausible-sounding numbers - the temptation is strongest exactly where the evidence is thinnest.

- **No evidence that anonymized review changes acceptance outcomes by demographic.** What exists is stated organizer intent and mechanism design. Blind review is defensible as a process commitment; it is not a demonstrated equity outcome, and presenting it as one is a claim nobody has substantiated.
- **Three real rubrics exist** in reproducible detail: a multi-criteria star scale, a letter-grade advocate scale, and a numeric 0-3 scale used by a distributed reviewer pool. There is no published comparison across the three, and no evidence any of them produces better programs than another.
- **No waitlist-to-acceptance conversion data.** One waitlist size is documented (about five talks, single-track). How often a backup gets promoted is unmeasured everywhere.
- **Conflict-of-interest handling is honour-system only.** Three self-recusal rules are documented - abstain on a close colleague or competing organization, abstain on a suspected identity, and a third that adds a disclosure obligation on top of abstention: a reviewer with a lesser conflict (same employer, a friendship, a topic bias) who continues reviewing must state the conflict in the review text itself. No declared-conflict registry, escalation path, arbitration procedure or audit mechanism appears anywhere in any of the three. Any escalation you design is your own.
- **Program-fit and balance-pass mechanics are undocumented** beyond one line: co-chairs weighing committee scores and diversity together at the final cut. How a trade-off is made, recorded, or bounded is not published by anyone.
- **No reviewers-per-proposal ratio or committee-size curve, beyond one real threshold.** Two scale points exist - roughly five graders at small scale, a three-tier structure at very large scale - and nothing between them, except one distributed-pool model's rule that a submission stops being actively resurfaced once it holds three or more reviews. That is a de-prioritization threshold, not a target ratio, and no curve connects any of the three points.
- **No published sponsored-to-CFP slot ratio** for vendor conferences. It varies widely and is rarely disclosed.
