# Access rungs and group mechanics

How the three disclosure rungs actually work on real registration pages, and what each one costs the organizer to run.

## Contents

- The three rungs compared
- Published persona tier: mechanics
- Discretionary scholarship: application mechanics

## The three rungs compared

|                  | Published persona tier | Date-or-volume gate           | Discretionary scholarship         |
| ---------------- | ---------------------- | ----------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Price visible    | Yes                    | Yes                           | Usually not                       |
| Gate             | Self-identification    | Calendar date or ticket count | Human review of an application    |
| Buyer effort     | Checkout               | Checkout                      | Write and wait                    |
| Organizer effort | One-time               | One-time                      | Standing, per application         |
| Reaches          | A named category       | Everyone, earlier             | Need a category definition misses |

They combine. Several events run an informal student discount code _and_ a broader hardship route, because a fixed student price excludes the unemployed, the career-changer, and the non-student in difficulty.

## Published persona tier: mechanics

- The tier is a **price**, not a benefit change: the same seat, the same access, a different amount by payer type.
- The gate is the honor system, occasionally an institutional email domain check, and nothing beyond that.
- Name the tier after who pays, not after who deserves it - "Corporate", "Business", "Individual", "Personal", "Student", "Education" are the labels real ladders use, and each reads as a self-description a buyer can pick without justifying it.
- Registration pages list personas in a plain reading order, not in an anchoring order (highest tier first, decoy in the middle). Do not import anchoring advice from software pricing here; it is unconfirmed for tickets.

## Discretionary scholarship: application mechanics

Community-conference pages offering a reduced or free ticket for students, unemployed, or underrepresented attendees gate it behind an application, never a self-service checkbox. In rough order of organizer effort:

- **Discount code on request by email** (devopsdays Boise 2019/2020) - unpriced; the organizer replies with a code. Lowest overhead, no written criteria.
- **Flat scholarship price, email with motivation** (devopsdays Amsterdam 2024: €50; 2023: €20 two-day / €30 conference-plus-workshop) - a published price, but the route to it is an email an organizer reads.
- **Unpriced "significant discount", email application** (devopsdays Prague 2023) - deliberately vague on amount, which keeps the decision with the reviewer.
- **Form with a stated window and named reviewers** (devopsdays Austin 2023) - a Google Form open until the day before the event, routed to three named local organizers who "respond as quickly as we're able."
- **Two named routes** (devopsdays Halifax 2026) - "inclusive access tickets" for financial hardship, plus a separate students and underrepresented-communities voucher, both by email request.
- **Formal scholarship program with a reviewer panel** (devopsdays Auckland 2019) - a complimentary pass, possible travel and accommodation support, a panel scoring need and impact, a published eligibility list, and an application window that opens and closes on a schedule. The highest-effort, most targeted end of the rung: closer to a small grants program than a ticket tier.

Write these before publishing the route:

- who reads applications
- how fast they promise to answer
- whether the window closes
- what the applicant receives: a code, a free pass, or a price

An unanswered application is worse than no route at all - the applicant has disclosed hardship to a silent inbox.

## Eligibility framing

- Auckland's published list - LGBTQ, women, people of colour, disabled people, students - is the one example of an explicit eligibility enumeration. Other pages frame on need ("financial hardship") rather than identity, or combine a need route with a separate underrepresentation route.
- Framing on need reaches cases an identity list omits. Framing on identity is legible and reduces reviewer judgment. Several events publish both, separately, rather than merging them into one paragraph.
- Do not copy an eligibility list across jurisdictions without checking it locally. How these categories interact with local law is a local question, and this skill does not give legal advice.

## Group orders and invoicing

- **Threshold and percentage**: published thresholds run from five tickets (15% off, one community event) to a 5-9 / 10+ step at 5% and 10% (GopherCon). Pick one threshold and one percentage; a multi-step table for an event selling a few hundred seats is machinery with nothing to do.
- **Same-order requirement**: the published wording is "in the same order". Without it, a group discount becomes a code that circulates.
- **Tier exclusion**: GopherCon applies group discounts only to its standard tier, never to early or late tiers. Say which tiers the discount may touch, on the page, before anyone asks.
- **Invoicing is a rail, not a rate**: a company that cannot pay by card at checkout buys nothing without it. Offer it at full price, on request, and only if an entity can actually issue the invoice and reconcile the payment. An event that cannot process money in the form its buyers need has an upstream problem - one organizer guide states plainly that discovering this late has rescheduled and cancelled events.

## What this rung structure does not cover

- **Regional or currency price parity** for one event run in several countries: no established method. Say so rather than inventing a purchasing-power formula.
- **Comp allocation as a share of capacity**: practice subtracts sponsor, speaker, organizer, and volunteer comps from expected sales, but sets no percentage. Count the seats you have actually committed.
- **Deposit-hold tickets** (a refundable deposit returned on attendance): untested for developer events specifically. Eventtia's 2024 managed-conference data reports a $3 refundable deposit cutting a previously-free event's no-show rate from 38% to 14% - general-conference evidence, not developer-event-specific.
