## What every brief carries

A supplier cannot quote what it has not been told.

1. Event name, date, exact address, and the access hours the venue gave you.
2. Headcount, and how firm it is: the number you will pay for, the date it locks, and the direction it is likely to move.
3. What you need delivered or performed, in the supplier's own units - plates, cameras, guards, garments, hours.
4. The window: when they may arrive, when the thing must be ready, when they may break down and leave.
5. What the venue supplies and what it forbids. Suppliers price around a venue's restrictions and cannot guess them.
6. Who on your side owns this supplier: one name, one number, one backup number.
7. What you want priced separately rather than bundled - delivery, staff, equipment, overtime - so the quotes arrive comparable.
8. The answer deadline, and the date you commit.

## Catering

A US student-hackathon organizer network publishes a guide on catering process. That process transfers well; its per-person prices do not.

Brief contents beyond the shared list:

- **The exclusivity answer first.** Does the venue mandate its own caterer, permit an outside one, or charge to allow one? Venues, "especially universities", often require their own catering service. Comparing outside caterers before this is answered is effort spent on suppliers you may not be allowed to hire.
- **Service style**: plated or individual meals versus family-style shared platters. This is not a presentation choice - it changes the delivery buffer, the equipment, the staffing and the fire-code exposure.
- **Meals and timings**, one row per meal, with the serving time rather than the delivery time. The supplier works back to a delivery time; you check it against the buffer.
- **Dietary requirements as counts, not as a note.** Ask every person who will be fed, including mentors, volunteers, speakers and organizers - not only registered attendees - before the order is placed. The categories to ask about:
  - Vegetarian.
  - Vegan.
  - Gluten-free or celiac.
  - Common allergies (lactose and peanut named explicitly).
  - Kosher.
  - Halal.

  What the event commits to meeting is `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-accessibility-inclusion`'s decision; this brief carries that decision to the supplier.

- **Who serves.** Some suppliers, university catering especially, require their own staff to serve. Ask before planning a volunteer rota around it.
- **What comes back, and when.** Chafing dishes, tablecloths and other reusable equipment need an agreed pickup time and a named responsible person - leaving it open-ended is how it becomes the organizers' problem at midnight.
- **Allergen information in a form you can post.** The guide's own practice is a menu posted at check-in with allergen detail so attendees can plan before the line forms.

Select on:

- Whether they will commit in writing to the delivery window for your service style - it is the failure that lands on the day.
- Whether they can meet the dietary counts within the standard order, or whether accommodation means a separate small order - a real cost and staffing difference. The guide's two paths:
  - A one-off addition to the standing order.
  - Where the supplier genuinely cannot cover a restriction: telling the affected attendee in advance and giving them a delivery gift card so they can plan.
- Whether their price moves with the headcount and until when.
- Whether they have served this venue before. A caterer who knows the loading dock, the kitchen access and the fire rules removes a class of surprise.

## Swag and print

A hackathon organizer network and a long-running community conference network each publish organizing guides covering apparel and print.

Brief contents beyond the shared list:

- **Quantity by size, not a total.** Apparel is ordered per size and style. A total headcount is not an order.
- **The size range required, stated as a requirement.** Some apparel lines stop at 2XL while real demand exists at 3XL-5XL - that is a supplier-selection criterion, not a size-chart footnote. A supplier who cannot cover the range does not meet the brief.
- **Fit options.** Where a fitted cut is offered alongside a unisex one, it should not differ sharply in colour or design from the standard line.
- **Fabric.** A tri-blend is named as more comfortable across a wide range of body types than 100% cotton.
- **The artwork specification and who supplies it** - file formats, colours, print positions, and whether the supplier does any design work.
- **A hard in-hand date, not a ship date** - the backorder risk below is what makes the distinction load-bearing.
- **Whether unopened surplus can be returned** (from the prize side of the same guide: keep every receipt, because unclaimed items can be returned, raffled or rolled into the next edition rather than written off).

Select on:

- Whether they will confirm current stock and lead time for the specific items and sizes, in writing, before you commit. Interesting items are frequently backordered, and stock availability - not shipping - is the real lead-time driver.
- Whether the full size range is genuinely in their catalogue.
- Turnaround time, priced against how late your order will be placed (one guide names quick turnaround as its main reason for preferring a particular print supplier).
- Whether they will hold or ship to the venue, and whether the venue accepts and charges for received shipments - a venue question, but it belongs in the supplier brief because it changes their job.

## AV and production

Community conference organizers expect the AV supplier to "provide a camera or cameras and record the feed off the board into a computer". Everything below is a working default; offer it as a starting point and say so.

The brief is not written here. `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-production` authors the technical specification - signal path, camera positions, recording and streaming requirements, redundancy, bandwidth - and this skill contracts a supplier against it. Attach that specification to the brief unchanged.

What this skill adds on top of the specification:

- The venue's own answers about what it already provides: in-house AV equipment, an in-house AV team, whether an outside supplier is permitted, and what it charges to allow one.
- Load-in and load-out windows from the venue contract, and whether an overnight secure store exists for their equipment.
- Which parts you expect priced separately: equipment, crew, crew overtime, transport, rehearsal time, and post-event deliverables such as edited recordings.
- Who on your side directs them on the day, and where their brief stops and `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-production`'s live direction starts.

A published AV-industry selection process shortlists before it formally requests proposals: identify up to five candidates through referral and reputation, interview them like a hire rather than a vendor - involving the people who will actually work with them on the day, not only procurement - then narrow to two finalists and send only those two the full RFP against an identical scope. This turns the quote-comparison grid in `references/quote-comparison-and-contract-terms.md` into a real like-for-like comparison instead of several differently-shaped bids.

State a budget range inside the RFP rather than withholding it. A published AV-RFP guide argues that withholding the number does not protect the organizer - it widens the spread between bids and filters capable suppliers out on guesswork instead of filtering on fit.

Selection criteria to offer as a starting point:

- Whether they will quote against the written specification without substituting their own package.
- Whether crew hours are quoted with a stated window and a named overtime rate, rather than "for the day".
- What the remedy is if a recording fails: this is the only category where the failure is invisible until after the event is over, so a supplier's answer here is worth more than their equipment list.
- Whether a rehearsal or technical check is inside the quote or outside it.
- Whether the crew hold CTS (Certified Technology Specialist) credentials or the equivalent from the AV trade association covering the event's country - a published, checkable qualification rather than a supplier's own claim of experience.

## Security

Start from the venue-checklist question: does the venue provide its own security, or must the organizer source it separately? Choosing a specific firm, and the licensing and coverage level required, still turn on local knowledge no single source resolves.

A published US standard describes what a professional security provider does and gives selection-relevant criteria to check a firm's proposal against: ANSI ES1.40-2023, _Event Safety - Event Security_ (Entertainment Services and Technology Association and the Event Safety Alliance). Treat it as a vetting checklist, not a licensing bar - the standard itself says there is no one correct number of guards and no single best practice for staffing a given event.

1. Ask the venue first. Many venues include security, mandate their own, or will only permit firms they already work with - and if they will not permit an outside firm, the category closes.
2. Ask the venue and peer organizers in the same city for firms that have worked that building. This is the same referred-single-source posture the main menu defaults to, and here it is close to the only responsible one.
3. Ask the venue what the building actually requires - guard posts, hours, whether a fire marshal is a separate paid role - rather than deciding a coverage level yourself.
4. Treat licensing as jurisdiction-specific and unresolvable here. Ask the firm what licenses and insurance it and its staff hold, and ask the venue what it requires; do not assert a standard. Whether a license is even required can turn on whether the guards are the firm's own employees or subcontracted from elsewhere - ask which.
5. Never substitute volunteers for licensed security work. Volunteers can staff a door, direct people, and watch a room; they cannot carry the obligation a licensed guard carries, and no briefing transfers it.

Select on:

- Whether the firm will produce a written post order before the event: a map or table naming each post, how many guards staff it, and their hours. A firm that cannot produce one has priced a headcount, not planned a deployment.
- Whether each guard meets baseline qualifications: at least 18 years old, no other duties that interfere with the security role, identifiable by uniform or visible credential, and trained for the specific tasks the event needs, such as crowd management, de-escalation, or emergency evacuation direction.
- Whether the proposed headcount is defensible. US fire and life-safety codes set a 1:250 ratio of trained crowd managers to occupants as a floor for that specific credential; a firm's total security number sitting far below it is worth a direct question, not a reason to reject the quote outright.
- Whether the firm has an incident-report practice: what a report captures, who writes it, and how long records are kept. A firm with no answer has no institutional memory to draw on when something goes wrong.

Tell the user plainly that licensing and coverage level still turn on local knowledge, so they weight the venue's and firm's own answers accordingly.

## Insurance broker

The coverage side belongs to `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-risk-management` - which policies exist, what each covers, what the standard exclusions are. Selecting and vetting the broker who sells them has published vetting criteria; picking one specific broker over another for a given jurisdiction still turns on local knowledge no single source resolves.

What holds regardless:

1. The coverage decision precedes the broker conversation. Arrive with `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-risk-management`'s answer about what the event needs and what the venue's contract requires, or the broker decides it for you.
2. Two requirements usually arrive from contracts rather than from choice: what the venue requires of the organizer, and what the organizer therefore requires of sponsors and suppliers. Extract both verbatim before approaching a broker.
3. Some policies must be bound well ahead of the event, and some cover is only available at all before a known circumstance exists - so the binding date is part of the lead-time work-back, not an administrative afterthought.

Select on:

- Licensing: confirm the broker's license is active in the state or country where the event happens, through the regulator's own public lookup rather than the broker's own claim.
- Experience placing event coverage specifically, not general home or auto lines, with a track record at a comparable event size and scope.
- Carrier quality: a broker who places with A-rated (AM Best) carriers rather than routing to the cheapest underwriter regardless of rating.
- The broker's own standing: current errors-and-omissions cover, and no unresolved solvency or disciplinary flags on the regulator's public record.
- Claims-handling reputation, checked at the carrier level: AM Best's financial-strength rating plus the complaint-index tools state insurance regulators and the NAIC publish, since brokers themselves are not carrier-rated - only the underwriters they place with are.

Named insurers who specialize in event liability exist, unlike the broker-selection playbook above. K&K Insurance runs dedicated event-industry programs (short-term events under 12,000 attendees, vendors, performers). Direct Event Insurance places corporate-conference liability at $1M/$2M limits and is licensed in all 50 US states. Hiscox sells conference, exhibition and exhibitor cover in the UK. Treat named insurers as a shortlist to request quotes from, never as an endorsement of one over the others, and still compare at least two - direct-to-carrier or through a broker.

Do not benchmark a premium from a single figure; see the price-benchmark section of `references/quote-comparison-and-contract-terms.md` for the one reachable floor and why it is not a quote.

## On named-vendor lists you inherit

Published organizer guides sometimes name specific suppliers - one names an apparel supplier and a print supplier, and is explicit that these are _personal_ recommendations from past organizer experience, not a vetted or exclusive list.

Carry that framing whenever you pass such a list on. A named supplier in a community guide is one organizer's good experience in one city in one year, which is genuinely useful as a starting point and is not an endorsement, a benchmark, or evidence that they are still good. Never restate an inherited recommendation as this skill's own pick, and never let a named supplier stand in for the brief.
