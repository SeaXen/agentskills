## The price-benchmark refusal

No reachable public source benchmarks most event-supplier prices:

- Not catering per plate.
- Not AV day rates for a standalone mid-size tech conference or hackathon. Published AV-production ranges exist for weddings, hotel ballrooms and generic corporate events, not this format specifically.
- Not security hourly rates.
- Not insurance-broker commission.

This is the same gap `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-venue-sourcing` states for venue pricing, and it is stated the same way here: say it plainly rather than producing a figure.

One partial exception on insurance: a named insurer (Markel) publishes event-liability policies "as low as $75" per event, with its own published footnote that the real price moves with the coverage selected and the policyholder's state. A floor from one insurer is not a market rate, and no source prices a standalone 200-1,000-person tech conference or hackathon's premium specifically - treat the figure as evidence that a floor exists, never as a number to budget a specific event against.

The other exception is a set of per-person figures published by a US student-hackathon organizer network for its own events - food per person per meal, snacks per person, shirts per person, and coach hire. They are real, and they are true for US student events in the year they were published.

They are not a rate card for a European conference, a corporate event, or an audience that is not students. Treat them as evidence that an organizer network can collect and publish such figures, and as a prompt to collect your own, never as the number to budget against or to quote at a supplier.

What is true for this city, this size and this year is three all-in quotes on one identical brief. That is the benchmark, and producing it is cheaper than being wrong in front of a supplier who prices this market every week.

Where a source publishes price bands for items rather than services - a prize ladder, for instance - convert them to relative tiers (entry, mid, flagship) rather than restating the currency amounts. The ratio between the tiers survives a change of country and year; the numbers do not.

## The all-in comparison grid

One row per candidate, one column per cost component, plus the terms that decide what the price is actually worth. Quotes arrive shaped differently on purpose; normalizing them is the step that reorders the candidates.

Cost components to force onto every row:

- Unit price and unit (per plate, per guard-hour, per garment, per crew-day).
- Minimum order or minimum call-out.
- Delivery, travel or transport.
- Staff: how many, for how long, and whether they are inside the unit price.
- Service charge or gratuity, and whether it applies to the whole invoice or to labour only.
- Equipment rental, and what happens if it is damaged.
- Setup and teardown, and whether they are inside the stated window.
- Overtime rate beyond the window, and when the window starts.
- Taxes.
- Anything you are expected to return, and the cost if you do not.

Terms to force onto every row:

- Deposit: amount, due date, refundable or not.
- Headcount change: until when, in which direction, at what cost.
- Cancellation: the schedule, not "cancellation fees may apply".
- Late or short delivery: the actual remedy, in the actual contract.
- Substitution: what they may change without telling you.
- Proof of insurance: what they carry, and what they will name you on.
- Reference: one event they served, with a contact.

Then compute the all-in total for a realistic headcount, and a second one for the headcount moved in the direction it is likely to move. A supplier who is cheapest at the planned number and most expensive at the likely number is not the cheapest supplier.

## Worked example: three catering quotes

A one-day conference, 200 attendees, lunch, one venue that permits outside caterers.

|                      | Supplier A                                                  | Supplier B                         | Supplier C                           |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Quoted headline      | per-plate, lowest of the three                              | per-plate, mid                     | per-head "all inclusive", highest    |
| Minimum order        | 150 covers                                                  | none                               | 100 covers                           |
| Delivery             | charged separately                                          | included                           | included                             |
| Serving staff        | not included - their staff required, charged per staff-hour | not included, volunteers permitted | 4 staff included for 3 hours         |
| Service charge       | 18% on the whole invoice                                    | none                               | included                             |
| Equipment            | chafing dishes rented, returned next day                    | disposables included               | included                             |
| Setup / teardown     | teardown charged                                            | teardown by us                     | included                             |
| Headcount lock       | 10 days out, downward moves not credited                    | 5 days out, ±10% free              | 14 days out, no changes              |
| Late delivery remedy | none stated                                                 | none stated                        | credit against invoice, stated       |
| Returns              | dishes collected next morning, fee if missing               | nothing to return                  | nothing to return                    |
| **All-in at 200**    | highest of the three                                        | lowest                             | middle                               |
| **All-in at 170**    | highest, minimum order bites                                | lowest                             | middle, but no downward move allowed |

The read: A's headline price was the lowest and its all-in total the highest, because required staff, an 18% service charge on the whole invoice, separate delivery and charged teardown all sat outside the quoted number. C looked most expensive and was defensible - it is the only one with a stated remedy for late delivery - but its 14-day hard lock is the wrong shape for an event whose numbers are still moving. B wins on total and on flexibility, and its gap is the missing late-delivery remedy; that is the question to ask B before signing, and the reason to reference-check them.

Note what the grid did: it changed the answer. The candidate with the lowest headline price finished last.

## Negative counterpart: the same three quotes compared wrong

> "Got three catering quotes. A is €X/head, B is €Y/head, C is €Z/head. Going with A, it's the cheapest - will confirm final numbers closer to the date."

Four failures in two sentences:

1. It compares headline per-head prices that cover different things.
2. It does not notice that A's number excludes the staff A requires.
3. It does not check whether "closer to the date" is inside A's 10-day lock.
4. It has read no failure terms at all, so nobody knows that A owes nothing if lunch arrives an hour late.

The organizer will discover all four after the deposit is paid.

## Contract terms to read before committing

The clause list below is a structural checklist borrowed from generic contract-review practice - the _shape_ is reusable, the content is this skill's, written for suppliers rather than for software agreements. Reading them is not legal advice, and a material commitment deserves a real reviewer.

1. **Scope and deliverables.** What exactly they deliver, in units, at what time. If the brief is not attached or restated, the contract is not about the brief.
2. **Price and what sits outside it.** Every component from the grid above, with the ones excluded named explicitly.
3. **Deposit and payment schedule.** Amount, dates, refundability, and what triggers the balance.
4. **Headcount or quantity changes.** The deadline, the permitted range, the cost of moving outside it, and whether downward moves are credited at all.
5. **Cancellation, both directions.** Yours: the schedule of what you owe as the date approaches. Theirs: what happens if they cancel, which is usually far weaker than what happens if you do - worth naming out loud.
6. **Substitution.** What they may swap without asking: a dish, an equipment model, a garment fabric, a crew member's role. Unconstrained substitution rights make the specification decorative.
7. **Remedy for failure.** Late, short, or not at all. Most supplier contracts offered to small events say nothing here; the absence is the finding, and it is what makes a reference check worth the call.
8. **Liability and indemnity.** Who carries what when someone is hurt or something is damaged. Expect the supplier's draft to be one-sided in their favour; mutual is a legitimate ask.
9. **Insurance.** See below.
10. **Force majeure.** What happens to your money when neither side can perform.
11. **Equipment and returns.** What is theirs, when it comes back, and what a missing item costs.
12. **Access, timing and the venue's own rules.** Load-in window, access hours, and any venue restriction the supplier must work inside. A supplier who has not been told the venue's rules will breach them on your account.
13. **Confidentiality, data and attendee information.** Relevant wherever a supplier touches an attendee list - a caterer receiving dietary requirements, a print supplier receiving names.

## The certificate-of-insurance ask

Two directions, and they are frequently confused:

- **What the supplier carries.** Ask for evidence of their own liability cover, and ask to be named as an additional insured where the venue or the risk register calls for it. A supplier who cannot produce a certificate is a supplier whose failure lands entirely on you.
- **What the venue requires of you.** The venue contract states this; `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-venue-sourcing` extracts it verbatim. It flows down into what you ask of suppliers and sponsors alike.

Gate the requirement on scale rather than applying it uniformly. Exhibitor-hall and convention-centre norms sit at a defined liability minimum with the organizer named as additional insured and the certificate due before move-in; small community events and hackathons frequently require no supplier-carried cover at all, because the venue's or organizer's own policy covers the space.

Requiring an enterprise-shaped certificate from a two-person catering business for a 60-person meetup mostly costs you the supplier. Keep the supplier-side ask consistent with the sponsor-side ask in `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-sponsor-agreement`, and let `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-risk-management` own which cover the event itself buys.

## Negotiation

**A worked example:** A hackathon organizer network publishes a worked example of pushing back on a catering quote that exceeds budget. Its shape is a specific target and a request for the trade-off that reaches it, rather than a blanket ask for a discount:

> "Is there another meal we could provide that would bring down the cost to [the target]? Are there changes we can make to other meals to save us money?"

Two things make it work:

1. It names a number, so the supplier is solving a defined problem rather than guessing how much cheaper is cheap enough.
2. It puts the trade-off in the supplier's hands, where the knowledge of what is expensive actually lives - a caterer knows which dish costs them the most; you do not.

**Negotiation tactics for live conversations:**

- **Mirroring** - repeat the last few words the supplier said, with a curious upward inflection, then stop talking. Draws out the constraint behind the statement.
- **Labelling** - name their constraint neutrally: "It sounds like the staffing charge is fixed at this size." Validates without conceding, and a wrong label produces a useful correction.
- **Calibrated questions** - "How" and "What", never "Why". "How am I supposed to lock a number 14 days out when registration is still open?" is pushback that does not refuse.
- **Accusation audit** - pre-empt the objection a supplier has about a small, new, community-run event before they raise it.
- **Decreasing-increment concessions** - open below target and move in shrinking steps to a precise, non-round final number, with a non-monetary ask attached. For suppliers the non-monetary ask is the valuable half: a later headcount deadline, waived delivery, teardown included, an extra staff-hour, a longer return window.
- **Real alternative** - every technique above draws its force from a genuine second candidate. This is why the multi-quote rung exists at all: without it, a negotiation is a request.

Do not use these tactics in an email exchange, and do not use any of it on a supplier the venue mandates - there is no alternative there, and the leverage they assume does not exist.
