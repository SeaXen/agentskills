## Sourcing channels, in detail

The sourcing ladder prioritizes free space first, then progressively moves to paid options only when free alternatives cannot meet the requirements.

For conference-scale events, the channel order is: free innovation centres or educational venues, local companies willing to host in their own facility, and a host sponsor who pays for a venue on the organizer's behalf.

For hackathon-scale events, "your venue should be free", and securing the venue early is proof to sponsors and attendees that the event is real. The channel order:

1. **On-campus** - usually free, accessible to the audience, and campus staff already know the institutional booking process. Research past events at the same school to learn a room's real capabilities before pitching it.
2. **A campus advocate** - a professor, dean or staff sponsor who can fast-track institutional booking. MLH publishes ready-made pitch material for administrators, faculty and parents, so the persuasion asset does not have to be written from scratch. Its framing device: pitch the hackathon as "a recruiting event with a twist", anchored to the career-fair vocabulary administrators already value.
3. **Off-campus company space** - companies that recruit developers and benefit from hosting. Named fit: libraries with a makerspace, for hardware-focused events.
4. **Paid rental, explicitly a last resort** - hidden and additional charges can double the total cost, and a paid bare venue shifts infrastructure onto the organizer (internet wiring, tables and chairs, a security team) that a sponsored space would absorb. Where a free venue genuinely cannot be found, a coworking space with that infrastructure already built in is the named fallback, cheaper than a bare rental plus build-out.

The free rungs are not charity and are not free of terms. A company-hosted venue may limit which other sponsors appear or claim category exclusivity - clarify in writing before selling a category. A campus venue brings institutional booking bureaucracy, an academic calendar that constrains dates, and its own security and fire-code regime.

## The site visit

A site visit answers what a floor plan cannot: whether the WiFi holds, where the load-in actually goes, how loud the partition-split rooms really are, and whether the accessible route is the same route everyone else takes or a service corridor round the back.

Checklist, walked in this order because it follows the day:

1. **Arrive the way an attendee will** - from the transit stop or the car park, not from the staff entrance. Time it. Note signage, or the absence of it.
2. **Walk the accessible route** in parallel. If it is longer, separate, or goes through a loading bay, that is a finding, and it belongs in the report.
3. **Main room** - check sight lines from the back and the far edges, and listen to the acoustics with the room empty (it will sound different full, but a bad empty room is never a good full one). Note where the stage can go, where power reaches, where the projector throws, and whether daylight washes it out.
4. **Breakouts** - walk the distance between them and time it against the session turnaround. Check whether chairs actually move into a circle. If rooms are partition-split, ask them to close the partition and listen.
5. **Circulation and hallway space** - stand where the coffee will be and ask whether 80 people can hold a conversation there without blocking the room doors.
6. **Sponsor tables** - check the placement against the food and hangout circulation path, and check where power and network reach them.
7. **Test the WiFi yourself**, on the day, in the rooms, and ask what the access-point layout and the uplink actually are. A verbal "the WiFi is fine" is not a requirement you can hold anyone to.
8. **Count outlets** in the rooms where people will work, against the density ratio in the requirements sheet.
9. **Load-in** - walk from the dock or the street door to each room. Note doorway widths, lifts, stairs, and whether the route crosses attendee space.
10. **Storage** - see the actual lockable room, not a described one.
11. **Toilets and quiet space** - count them against headcount; find the quiet room.
12. **Meet the person who will be on site on the day**, not only the sales contact. They are the one who knows what is actually possible.

Bring the requirements sheet and mark each line met, not met, or needs-an-answer-in-writing. A visit that produces a good feeling and no marked sheet has not produced a comparison.

## The options approach

Do not fix one date and then ask the venue. Instead, use **"Get Options"**: carry several candidate windows into the venue conversation, because availability is uneven across a week and a month - a venue "can accommodate you better during some parts of the week than others". Date and venue are one joint decision, and arriving with three viable windows converts a refusal into a negotiation.

Extend the same logic across venues. Run a grid, not a queue:

|          | Venue A            | Venue B            | Venue C            |
| -------- | ------------------ | ------------------ | ------------------ |
| Window 1 | available / quoted | not available      | available / quoted |
| Window 2 | not available      | available / quoted | available / quoted |
| Window 3 | available / quoted | available / quoted | not available      |

Fill it before choosing anything. The grid does three jobs at once: it lets availability rank the date windows, which is the job `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-date-selection` explicitly hands over; it produces comparable quotes on identical written requirements; and it is the alternative that gives every negotiation tactic its force. A single cell is a request. A filled grid is a negotiation.

Sequence it so the grid stays cheap: desk-check every candidate first, visit only the ones that survive, and get written quotes on the same requirements sheet from all survivors before opening a serious price conversation with any of them.

## The handoff pack

Venue work does not end at signature. One local organizer is named as the venue's primary operational contact for chair layout, table arrangement, A/V detail and the rest - an explicit role, not an assumption that whoever signed the contract owns it. Finalising venue details is an ongoing task through the final weeks, alongside check-in setup, signage and badges.

Hand over, in writing:

- **The signed contract**, with the cancellation-fee dates and percentages called out separately, and the insurance requirement and its amounts called out separately.
- **The catering headcount deadline** - usually about two weeks before the event for the venue or its mandated caterer, a date that belongs on the work-back schedule and is distinct from the ticket-sales deadline.
- **Shipping details** - earliest accepted date, exact address and routing, receiving fees.
- **Access hours** for the team, including the setup day negotiated before the event.
- **Storage** - where it is, who has the key, and whether it covers sponsor equipment.
- **The named venue contact** on both sides.
- **Every requirement marked "not met"** on the sheet, with whatever workaround was agreed. The unmet lines are what production has to solve, and they are the ones most likely to be forgotten between signature and load-in.
- **The room-block group rate code and its expiry date**, if a block exists.

## Worked shortlist

Illustrative, using the fictional event from the requirements sheet. No prices appear, deliberately: quotes are the only price this skill trusts.

```
CANDIDATE A  - City university, lecture theatre + 2 seminar rooms
  Channel: educational venue (free rung).  Windows: 1 and 3 available.
  Met: main room 200, breakouts 20 and 40, step-free throughout, outside
       catering permitted, dock at rear.
  Not met: WiFi capacity unstated  - campus IT to confirm; no storage offered
       overnight; hallway space is a corridor, too narrow for 80.
  Written answers outstanding: insurance required of us; setup-day access.

CANDIDATE B  - Company office, sponsor-hosted
  Channel: company host.  Windows: 2 only.
  Met: WiFi and power good, storage secure, hallway space excellent.
  Not met: main room seats 120, short of 180; no dock, street load-in only.
  Flag: host asks that no direct competitor sponsors the event  - GET IN
       WRITING before selling any sponsor category, and check it against
       the categories already sold.

CANDIDATE C  - Conference centre, paid rental
  Channel: paid (last rung).  Windows: 1, 2 and 3 all available.
  Met: every must-have line.
  Not met: catering mandated in-house; fire marshal and janitorial charged
       separately; quote not yet confirmed as a ceiling.
  Flag: only candidate with a cancellation-fee schedule and a deposit  -
       extract dates and percentages verbatim for the risk register.

READ: Window 1 survives at A and C; window 2 only at B and C. B fails on
capacity and cannot be fixed by layout. Visit A and C; ask A whether campus
IT can fortify WiFi and whether a nearby room can serve as storage, since
both gaps are institution-patchable. Hold C as the real alternative  - its
existence is what makes the conversation with A a negotiation.
```

## Negative example: how the shortlist collapses

```
The university said yes for 15-16 April. Booked it. We'll sort out the WiFi
and the storage nearer the time.
```

The alternative is gone before any term was tested, so nothing about WiFi, storage or the hallway problem is negotiable any more - each is now the organizer's own problem to solve at their own cost. Nobody asked what insurance the university requires, which surfaces later as a condition of the doors opening and, by then, as an input the sponsor conversations already needed. And the two other windows were discarded without ever being priced, so the event will never know whether a different week bought a better room.
