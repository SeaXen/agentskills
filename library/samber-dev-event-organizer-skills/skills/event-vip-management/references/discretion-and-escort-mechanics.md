# Discretion and escort mechanics

Every line of quoted copy in this file is written here as a model to adapt, never transcribed from a real briefing or a real event.

## What each visibility rung looks like on the day

**Invisible handling.** The guest uses the same entrance, carries the same badge, sits where they like. One organizer knows the arrangements; nobody else on the team does. The guest's arrival then depends entirely on that organizer being reachable and awake at the right moment. That is this rung's weakness, not its cost.

**Staff-only signal.** Publicly identical to the rung above. Internally, three groups know before doors:

- The **door or registration lead** gets a name and an arrival window. Nothing else.
- The **room leads** for wherever the guest will be get the name and a single instruction: no announcement, no reserved seat, route any question to the named organizer.
- The **code-of-conduct responders** get the name, because they are the people who most need to know who is in the room before an incident, not after one.

Say the reason out loud in the briefing, because a team told to keep a name quiet without a reason will invent one: _"She has asked not to be singled out. That is the whole instruction. If you see her, she is an attendee."_

**Visible marker.** A ribbon, lanyard colour, badge variant or reserved row. Two things have to be true before this is built:

- The guest asked to be identifiable.
- The print run is still open.

A marker's real cost is not printing it - it is that it must then be explained. Attendees read a second badge colour as a hierarchy on sight, and the only thing that makes it read otherwise is a public, stated function ("green ribbon = organizer, ask me anything"). If you cannot write that function in five words, you have built a tier.

One published vendor-pole VIP program shows this rung's ceiling: an application-gated badge issued from its own desk, a single named VIP Experience Manager as point of contact, a lounge for orientation and coffee, and reserved front-row keynote seating, run as one recognized program rather than assembled item by item. That is the whole rung fully built out at the vendor pole. Most guests this skill will ever see need one piece of it, not the whole program.

**Public marker + recognition moment.** An announcement, a stage acknowledgement, a photograph. Three checks before it goes in the run of show:

- The guest's office asked for it in as many words.
- Nothing about their movements is being published that their security posture forbids.
- The run of show can absorb it without pushing a talk, which is `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-run-of-show`'s call and not yours to make on their behalf.

## Greeter brief

One page, handed to whoever meets the guest at the door. Written by the named organizer, never improvised on the morning. The item list below is this skill's own construction.

- **Who you are meeting**, name and photograph if the guest's office supplied one - and if they did not, ask, rather than searching for one.
- **When and where**, with a fallback if they are late and a phone number that will be answered.
- **The route**: door, cloakroom, coffee, the room they are heading for, the toilets, and the exit they will leave by. Walk it once yourself before doors.
- **What they asked for**, quoted, and **what was refused**, quoted.
- **The visibility instruction in one line**, in plain words.
- **Your handoff**: the name of the person you hand them to, and what you do if that person is not there.

What not to say, and why each one matters:

- Never introduce the guest to anyone who has not been told they are coming. An unplanned introduction is a status announcement made by accident.
- Never say "VIP", to the guest or to anyone else, including on the radio. The word is the tier; once it is in the team's vocabulary the treatment follows it.
- Never explain the arrangements to a curious attendee. "I'm just showing someone where the room is" is complete and true.
- Never volunteer a departure time to anyone.

## Declining a photograph on the guest's behalf

Decide in advance who holds this, per step 8 of the workflow, because it always happens at the worst moment. The person holding it needs two lines, both refusing without making the guest the reason:

- To an attendee: _"Not right now, sorry - we're on a tight schedule."_ Short, cheerful, no explanation. An explanation invites a negotiation.
- To a photographer or someone filming: _"No photos of this group, please."_ Address the frame, not the person, so the guest is not marked by the refusal itself.

Consent to a photograph is never implied by attendance, and it is not the organizer's to give. Where an image has already been taken and the guest's posture forbids publication, the ask goes to the person who took it, in private, immediately - not to a moderation queue afterwards.

## Liaison intake with an external detail

Run this the moment Q4 confirms a guest arrives with a protective detail, protocol officer or advance team. The point is to _receive_ a plan, not to build one.

Two things this intake is not:

- A negotiation about status. Nobody in it is asking for recognition, and reading it that way is how a security arrangement turns into a VIP lounge.
- The moment to promote every other menu. A guest with a protective detail still gets the visibility posture their office asked for, which is very often the least visible one on the menu.

Start as early as you can: a published run of show that lists a name, a session, a room and a time hands anyone with bad intent a precise plan of where the guest will be, so the contact that matters happens before the run of show goes public, not after.

The sequence below carries over the shape of government and corporate protocol practice - one counterpart, one written requirements exchange, one walked route - and is not measured against event data.

1. **Name one counterpart on your side** and give them a number that will be answered from the day before until the guest leaves. One name - not a team address, not a shared inbox.
2. **Ask what they need, and write it down verbatim.** Entrance, route, a room to hold in, timings, who may approach, what may be published and when. Do not paraphrase into your own words; a paraphrase is where a requirement gets softened.
3. **Answer each item in writing with yes, no, or a substitute**, before the day. A venue with one entrance has one entrance, and saying so three weeks out is a fact they can plan around. Saying so at the door is a problem you created.
4. **Walk the route with them if they ask.** They will usually ask, and the walk surfaces more than the exchange did.
5. **State the code of conduct applies to them**, in the same message, without ceremony. They are a third party in your venue like any contractor - `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-code-of-conduct`'s scope clause names contractors and press, and its wording is the place this gets written properly.
6. **Tell your own team what changes and nothing more.** The door lead needs a time and a name. Sharing the security plan more widely is not transparency; it is the plan's failure mode.
