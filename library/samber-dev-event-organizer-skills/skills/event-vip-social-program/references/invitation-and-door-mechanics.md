# Invitation and door mechanics

Do not invent quantities in any field below. No seat count, no lead time, no cap, no headcount - each reads as a benchmark once invented.

The field lists, the door page and every line of sample copy here are this skill's own construction, not wording lifted from an event. Keep the shape; rewrite the words. One principle shapes all of them: nothing in this file should outlive the event it serves unless the organizer chose to keep it.

## The seat list

Build it only once Menu A has landed above the fold. The fold deliberately has no list, which is both its cheapness and the reason every invitation it issues is verbal and unrecorded.

Fields, and nothing beyond them:

- **Name**, as the person or their office writes it.
- **Why this seat exists**, in one sentence. Not a rank, not a title, not "important to us". A row whose sentence has to be invented is a row that comes off the list.
- **Who put the name forward** - the host, a sponsor, a speaker, a team member - and the date. This is the field that makes a refusal defensible later, because it records which asks were made and which rule was applied to each.
- **Answer given**, and the date it was given. A seat offered and a seat confirmed are different states and conflating them is how a room ends up over-promised.
- **Access requirements**, only where the person supplied one: a fixed window, a route, a dietary need, a discretion request quoted from whoever stated it.

Three rules govern the artifact itself:

- **The deletion date is set when the list is created**, by the named owner, in the same pass. Default: the day the post-event follow-ups go out. A seat list carrying dietary answers and quoted requirements is a named-person record, and it is the artifact hardest to justify holding and easiest to forget.
- **The list never records a judgment about a person.** Every field is either a fact somebody supplied or a decision you made and can defend. This is what keeps it survivable if it leaks.
- **Circulation is by need.** Whoever works the door needs names and nothing else - not the reasons, not who nominated whom, and never the refusals.

## Issuing the invitation

One rule does most of the work: **the person who holds the list is the person who invites.** An invitation issued by anyone else is a seat allocated by someone who cannot see how many are left, and it is the single most common way a room gets over-promised.

Send it individually and privately. A group thread turns the guest list into a published document, which is the Menu B decision made by accident in the direction that menu exists to avoid.

What the message carries:

- **What the gathering is**, in plain words, without the word "VIP" and without a superlative. Illustrative: "A small room over the lunch break, so you can talk to the people you came to talk to" is complete.
- **When and where**, with the room named and the way in described - because the alternative is a guest arriving at a door they cannot identify and asking a general attendee about it.
- **Who else will be there**, in shape rather than by name unless every person on the list has agreed to be named. Some guests decide on this fact alone; some would decline to be listed to anyone.
- **What it costs them**, meaning what they will miss on the public programme.
- **How to say no**, made easy and made explicit. A guest who cannot decline cheaply accepts and does not come, which is worse than a decline.
- **One name and one way to reach it**, answered from the day before until the gathering ends.

Never publish the wording anywhere the general audience reads. The listing rung on Menu B is a deliberate decision with its own promotion condition, not something an invitation drifts into.

## Refusing an ask

Every asking party gets an answer, and the same rule is cited to each. That sentence is the whole of the defensibility this skill is built to buy, and it is cheap only if the rule was written before the first ask arrived. A conference-dinner host describes this exact review-and-refuse process: he manually reviews every RSVP against the room's purpose, and where someone does not fit, their access is respectfully declined. The two lines below standardize that discipline rather than leaving it to whoever is on the door that day.

Two illustrative lines, both refusing without making the refused person the reason:

- To a party who nominated somebody: _"The room seats what it seats, and we allocated it on <the rule>. I can't add a seat, but here is what I can do instead."_ Name the rule out loud. A refusal with no rule behind it reads as a judgment about the person nominated, and it is the version that generates a grievance.
- To a party who was already told yes by somebody else on your team: honour it, then fix the process rather than the guest. Withdrawing an accepted invitation costs more than the seat did, and the reversibility was spent the moment the yes was sent.

Always pair the refusal with the alternative that is genuinely available - the general evening, an introduction made during a break, a scheduled conversation. A refusal with nothing attached is where a sponsor relationship gets damaged over a seat nobody would have remembered.

Where the seats were **sold** rather than requested, this is not a refusal at all. A gathering promised inside a signed agreement is a deliverable, and it belongs to `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-sponsor-fulfillment` against that agreement, not to this skill's allocation rule.

## Working the door

One person, one list, one printed page. Written by the named owner, never improvised on the day.

- **The names**, alphabetically, and nothing else on the page.
- **What to do with a name that is not on it**: take the name, say the owner will come, and go and get them. Never turn somebody away with an explanation, and never let the door-holder adjudicate - that is the owner's decision and it needs the list to make.
- **What to say to a curious attendee**, illustrative: _"It's a private meeting, it'll be done shortly."_ Short, true, complete. An explanation invites a negotiation about a seat.
- **Never say "VIP"**, to a guest or to anyone else, including on the radio. The word is the tier; once it is in the team's vocabulary the treatment follows it.
- **Never read the list aloud** or hold it face-out. The list working correctly is a list nobody else sees.
- **What happens at the end**: the door-holder hands the page back to the owner. It does not go in a bin in a public room.

## The fold, done properly

Menu A's default is that no separate gathering exists. That is a decision with its own mechanics, not an absence, and doing it badly is how it acquires the reputation of being the lazy answer.

- **Name the person who will be standing with the guest** when they walk into the general evening, and for how long. The failure mode of the fold is a guest left holding a drink at the edge of a room, which is worse than not inviting them.
- **Make the introductions the gathering would have made.** Write down, before the evening, which conversations were the actual point - name them individually rather than as a goal, and make those happen by walking up to people. This is the whole of what a private room buys, and most of it carries over.
- **Give them a way out**: a stated time you will walk them to the door, so leaving needs no excuse.
- **Say nothing about any of it.** The fold's advantage over every other rung is that it produces no artifact, no door and no list - and announcing that a guest is being looked after re-creates the tier for free.
