# The retained volunteer list and its handoff

Everything in this file is this skill's own construction, not a published standard: the field set, the owner-and-deletion-date rule, the handoff steps and the flag criteria. The one exception is the vouching questions at the end, which carry a named source.

## Why the list is the load-bearing rung

`samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-volunteers` ranks a past-volunteer call-back second on efficiency among its recruitment channels, and names it in its own stated default. That channel cannot run without a list somebody kept. Everything else in this skill's closure menu is optional. The list is what next edition's recruitment structurally depends on.

That is a dependency on a sibling's published default, not external evidence. It says the list matters. It does not say anyone measured the payoff.

## What the list holds

Keep the smallest record that makes a call-back possible. Every extra field is a field you now have to justify holding for a year.

- Name, and the contact address the volunteer used to sign up.
- Which posts they staffed, and how many shifts.
- Whether they said yes to being contacted about the next edition, recorded as their answer rather than as an assumption.
- Whether they agreed to be named publicly (see the consent ask in the closure-messages reference).
- One free-text line from the coordinator: what they were good at, and anything that should change about their assignment next time.

Do not carry over dietary answers, phone numbers, t-shirt sizes, emergency contacts, or accessibility requests. Those were collected to run one edition and have no role in recruiting the next one. Deleting them at closure is the cheapest privacy decision available to you.

## The owner and the deletion date

Set both on the day the list is created, not when someone remembers.

- **Named owner** - a person, not "the organizers". A list with no owner is a file that survives every handover and is reviewed by nobody.
- **Deletion date** - an actual date, usually tied to the next edition's recruitment close. Renew it deliberately by asking again, never by letting it lapse into permanence.
- **Renewal ask** - when the date approaches, ask the volunteer whether they still want to be on the list. A one-line email most people ignore still beats silence, because the ones who reply "remove me" are the ones the list was wrong about.

`samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-volunteers` already flags the retention duty on its own sign-up channels. Apply that same duty to a record that now outlives the edition it was collected for, rather than deriving a second one.

## The handoff

A list nobody inherits is the same as no list. Make the transfer an event with a date on it.

1. Name who receives it: whoever will run recruitment for the next edition, by name, not by role.
2. Hand it over with the owner and the deletion date attached, so both travel with the file rather than staying in one person's memory.
3. State what was already asked: who consented to contact, who asked to be removed, who was thanked publicly.
4. Say what changed this edition - posts that ran short, roles that were hard to fill - so the call-back is targeted rather than a blanket message.

## The candidate flag

A volunteer who ran an area, fixed something nobody asked them to fix, or was the person other volunteers went to, is a candidate for a named role on the standing team.

- Raise it as a flag: a name, what they did, and who noticed. Nothing more.
- Hand it to `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-team-structure`, which owns the role taxonomy, the charter and the conversation that follows.
- Never write the role definition here, and never tell the volunteer a role exists before the standing team has agreed one does.

Borrow a pattern rather than reinventing one. DEF CON's own volunteer corps recruits into its standing team by vouching, and its own community discussion of that process converges on four questions:

1. Does the team have a real opening?
2. Does the candidate carry the skill that opening needs?
3. Does someone already on the team trust them enough to answer for them?
4. Will they hold up doing the same repetitive work reliably for days in a row?

Most people who reach that conversation have already been helping informally, sometimes for more than a year, before anyone raises it. Use the four questions to sharpen what you flag. They do not replace the standing team's own conversation, and they carry no promise that this edition's flag moves as fast as DEF CON's multi-year path.
