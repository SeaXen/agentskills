## Marking eligibility without forking the grid

Add a column to the published grid. Never build a "remote schedule" as a separate artifact.

A separate remote schedule is a second document with a second update path, and the update that gets missed is always the one on the document fewer people open. A documented failure has exactly that shape: a session slips in the room and the remote side is never told. One document with a column cannot slip that way, because the person changing the row is looking at both audiences at once.

What the column holds per session, and nothing more:

- **Eligible / not eligible.** A binary, not a plan.
- **The return path for that session**, if it differs from the event default - some sessions can carry live Q&A and some cannot.
- **The named person on the remote side for that session**, or an explicit "closed" where nobody is available.

An explicit "closed" is the load-bearing entry. It separates "no remote return path here, deliberately" from "nobody has looked at this row", which is exactly what a rushed grid hides.

## What each parity rung looks like inside a session

### Feed only

Remote sees and hears the session. Nothing comes back. Leave the speaker untold that remote exists, because telling them invites an interaction the room cannot deliver.

Publish one line in advance where remote attendees will already be looking: what the feed carries, that questions are not taken live, and where the recordings will appear afterwards if they will. That sentence is the entire cost of the rung, and it removes the worst minute of a one-way feed, the one where somebody types a question and watches nothing happen.

### An asynchronous written return path

Questions are collected during the session and answered afterwards. The rung works only if three things are stated up front:

- **Who answers** - the speaker, an organizer, or a subject-matter volunteer. Unstated means nobody.
- **By when** - a real window somebody has agreed to.
- **Where the answer appears** - the same channel the question was asked in, so the asker does not have to go looking.

Without those three, this is the deleted rung wearing a promise. With them it is honest and cheap, and it suits a programme whose sessions are dense enough that live Q&A was never the point.

### Live relay by a named person

The mechanic, in the order it happens:

1. **Before the session**, the relay person tells the speaker two things: remote is watching, and questions will come through this person rather than from the floor. A speaker who does not know this addresses the room only, and the remote audience watches a talk given past them.
2. **During the talk**, the relay person reads the remote channel and selects. Selection is the job: an unfiltered read-aloud of everything typed is dictation, not relay, and it burns the room's Q&A time on the first question rather than the best one.
3. **At Q&A**, the relay person says who is asking and where from, then reads the question. Naming the asker is what makes the remote audience a set of people rather than a source of text.
4. **After the answer**, the relay person confirms in the channel that the question was asked. The remote asker cannot always tell that the question just answered was theirs.

Step 4 is the one that gets dropped and the one remote attendees notice.

### Reserved floor time for remote questions

The relay mechanic above, plus a rule the moderator holds. Write the rule as a sequence rather than a share - for example, remote takes the first question of every Q&A, or the room and remote alternate until time runs out.

Prefer a sequence rule over a proportion. Someone counting hands must compute a proportion under time pressure, and it fails silently when Q&A is cut short. A sequence rule survives being cut short, because whatever fraction of it ran, remote got its turn first.

Brief the moderator on the rule before the day and put it on the cue sheet row, not in a briefing document. A rule that lives only in someone's memory loses to a room full of raised hands.

### Remote participants appear in the room

The remote questioner's audio, and sometimes video, is played into the room so the speaker answers a person. Two things change and both are somebody else's to build:

- **The return path.** Audio going _into_ the room's system is a different signal path from audio coming out of it. Specify that you need it. Let the production sibling decide how.
- **The consent envelope.** A remote participant is now inside the room's captured output. Collect their agreement before they go on air, and note that the room's own recording now contains a person who was never in the room.

Run this rung with a queue rather than an open microphone: the relay person takes the question in text first, picks it, then brings that person on. An open path into a room's PA is a conduct surface with no door on it.

## The consent questions a live feed adds

Put these to whoever holds the consent record before eligibility is marked, not after. A live feed publishes before anyone can check it, which is why these cannot be settled in an edit.

- Does each eligible speaker's consent cover a **live** feed, or only a recording? These are different permissions and a record built for recording may not carry the first.
- What happens to the feed when an in-room questioner steps to a microphone? Are audience questions on camera, off camera, or audio only?
- What is the visible sign that tells an in-room attendee this session is going out live, and where is it placed so they see it before they speak?
- Where is the boundary of the session - does the feed cut at the last word, or does it carry the room emptying?
- If a speaker withdraws consent on the day, what happens to the feed, and who has the authority to cut it?

The last question has no standard answer in the literature. Decide it in advance and name the person who holds the authority.

## A positive and negative pair

### Negative

A three-room day. The grid says "all talks streamed". Remote attendees are pointed at a chat channel with no stated purpose. The AV volunteer in each room is told to "keep an eye on the chat too".

By mid-morning the channel has questions in it. The volunteer in room one is re-seating a speaker whose laptop will not display, and never looks at the channel. In room two the volunteer reads a question at Q&A but the speaker, who was never told remote existed, answers it to the front row and moves on.

A remote attendee asks whether the afternoon is delayed. The room was told out loud earlier and nobody typed it anywhere. At the close, someone posts "thanks everyone" into a channel of unanswered questions.

Nothing here is an equipment failure. Every one of them is a person looking at the room.

### Positive

The same three-room day. One room is the remote room for the whole day, chosen in advance with the schedule owner so the sessions in it hold together. The grid's remote column says eligible for every session in that room, closed everywhere else, with a named person per session and one explicit "closed" against the after-lunch slot where nobody was available.

Remote attendees are told in advance: this room, live questions taken through this channel, other rooms not carried. The relay person briefs each speaker just before they start.

At Q&A they name the asker and their city, read the question, and afterwards post in the channel that it was asked. During the after-lunch slot the channel carries a pinned line saying questions are not being taken this hour and why.

When the schedule slips, the person who changes the row on the cue sheet changes both columns, and the remote channel hears it at the same moment the room does.

The second day costs one more person than the first and delivers an audience that was actually present.
