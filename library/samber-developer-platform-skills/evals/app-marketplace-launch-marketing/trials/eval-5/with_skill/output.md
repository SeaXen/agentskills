# Verdict: the plan fails on its central design choice

The "no badge, no disclosure, presented as our editorial pick" part is what breaks it. The skill lists this exact pattern as a named failure mode: *"A paid slot that reads as an editorial pick spends the trust the review gate bought. Label paid placement as paid."* Selling the slot is fine. Hiding that it's sold is not.

I didn't run the skill's interview, so some answers below are assumptions. The open questions are at the end.

## Holes, ranked by damage

**1. The disclosure is what makes the product work, and it can't survive.**
- The slot is worth more because buyers read it as your judgment. That is the thing you're selling, and you'd be selling it by deceiving the buyers.
- It only works while nobody knows. Eight partners bid every month, so at least eight companies know exactly how it works. Seven of them lose each round and have a reason to talk.
- Assume it leaks within a quarter, through a screenshot, a Slack thread or a LinkedIn post.
- Once it leaks, every editorial surface you run is tainted, not just this slot.
- That includes Staff Picks-style rotations, collections and badges. The review gate's credibility goes with it.

**2. It's probably a legal problem, not only a trust problem.** This is not legal advice, so get counsel to confirm.
- In the US, the FTC treats undisclosed paid placement presented as independent selection as deceptive. Its native-advertising and endorsement guidance covers this.
- In the EU, the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive requires disclosing paid placement that influences ranking. The Digital Services Act adds ad-labeling duties for platforms.
- Buyers are businesses, not consumers. That softens some rules but doesn't remove the deception exposure.
- Partners are exposed too. If a partner's contract or a buyer's procurement policy treats the placement as an endorsement, the partner inherits the problem.

**3. A pure highest-bid auction picks the wrong winners.**
- The top 8 partners are your largest and best-funded. They win by wallet, not by fit or quality.
- New and small apps are structurally shut out. The skill's guidance is that rotations exist to surface them, which Shopify's Staff Picks does deliberately.
- Money doesn't filter for quality. If a weak app wins, buyers get a bad "pick" and blame you.
- There is no review-gate check built in. A bid can win a slot that the vetting process would not have endorsed.

**4. Seven of eight losers each month are your best partners.**
- Your biggest accounts spend money, lose, and watch a competitor take the hero slot with your implied endorsement.
- Without a published rule, the grant reads as favoritism. The skill calls "unpublished rationing" a failure mode on its own.
- Bidding wars also escalate. Your top partners learn that the slot is a budget line, then ask for the same treatment elsewhere.
- Pricing is another trap. A pure auction gives you no price floor you can defend, and a collusion risk where the top 8 agree to rotate.

**5. A two-person partner marketing team can't run this.**
- Each month needs: bid intake, eligibility checks, a decision, creative approval, a placement, a measurement report, and a "you lost" conversation with seven partners.
- Add the dispute handling when someone asks why they lost.
- That is the "menu-priced paid catalog" work. The skill ranks it highest in effort and says it starves otherwise. Salesforce runs it with dedicated fulfillment machinery.
- Two people doing this means the baseline demand-gen work slips, which is the non-rival work that helps every partner.

**6. Revenue is unverified, and the sizing is probably thin.**
- One slot a month is 12 sales a year. Even a generous bid, times 12, may be small against the revenue pressure.
- You have no price anchor. Salesforce's published AMP points (~$4K to ~$14K per item, operator-published) are the closest reference, and they come from a very different volume of buyers.
- Nobody has shown that a hero slot converts. The only lift figures in the skill's sources are vendor-reported or contested, for example Shippo's "3x signups" or Shopify's 49% (operator-published, contested on rank).
- Without baselines you can't show a partner what they paid for, so renewals fall.

**7. It cannibalizes the editorial credibility you'd need to charge more later.** An honest sponsored slot gets weaker than an honest editorial pick, but it's a durable product. A fake editorial pick is worth more for a few months and then is worth zero or negative.

## What to do instead

The skill's rung for this is a **disclosed, published, menu-priced paid placement**, kept apart from any editorial surface.

1. **Label it.** "Sponsored" or "Promoted" on the slot. Keep the format a hero slot if you want, but visibly paid. Expect a lower conversion than a disguised pick, but it holds up and keeps working.
2. **Keep editorial separate.** Run a free, time-boxed editorial rotation (a "Staff Picks" equivalent) that money cannot buy. It protects the credibility the paid slot borrows from, and it surfaces new apps.
3. **Publish the rule.** Gate eligibility on review-gate status plus a tier or quality threshold, then sell on a published price, not a blind auction. A partner should be able to self-assess before applying.
4. **Replace the auction with one of these:**
   - A fixed price list, with first-come booking for a calendar slot.
   - A lottery among eligible applicants.
   - Quarterly slots rather than monthly, if the team is small.
   Any of these reduces the "my budget lost" resentment and the dispute load.
5. **Cap and sequence.** Limit the paid slots to one a month. Sell them 1 to 2 quarters ahead so the calendar is predictable and you can plan the work.
6. **Fix the labor first.** With two people, productize it: one intake form, one creative spec, one report template. If that doesn't fit, defer the paid slot and make the editorial rotation the first launch.
7. **Measure honestly.** Baseline each app's installs for the prior fortnight, report lift during and after the window, and label it as vendor-reported or measured. Don't promise a rank effect.

## Questions I'd have asked, and what changes the answer

1. **Is there a live review gate?** If not, "featured" can't honestly mean vetted, and the paid slot has even less to stand on.
2. **Is there a partner-tier program?** If yes, gate eligibility on it. If no, the tier design belongs to the marketplace-strategy sibling skill.
3. **What does the revenue need to be, and by when?** A hard date favors a simple fixed-price list. An open horizon favors building the editorial rotation first.
4. **How many hours per week can the two people commit to this?** Under about 4, defer the paid slot.
5. **Does legal accept the disclosure risk in your markets?** Ask counsel before anything ships, whichever design you pick.
