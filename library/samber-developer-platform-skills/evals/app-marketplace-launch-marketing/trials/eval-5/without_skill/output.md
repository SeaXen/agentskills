# Poking holes in the undisclosed "Featured App of the Month" auction

**Verdict: ❌ don't ship it as designed.** The undisclosed part makes it a legal problem, a trust problem, and a partner-relations problem. The auction part is fine. Disclosure is the fix, and the cost is lower conversion on that slot. Details below.

## 1. Legal exposure 🔴

I'm not a lawyer, so get counsel to confirm. A paid placement presented as an editorial pick is the textbook case that advertising rules target.

- **US (FTC):** The FTC treats native ads that look like editorial content as deceptive under Section 5. Disclosure must be clear and close to the content, not buried in terms.
- **EU/UK:** Paid editorial without clear disclosure is on the unfair-practices blacklist (UCPD Annex I, point 11). If you serve EU buyers, the DSA (Art. 26) also requires online platforms to label ads and say who paid. The P2B Regulation requires disclosing paid ranking influence to business users, meaning your partners.
- **Contracts:** Check your partner program terms, marketplace terms, and any public "how we curate" page. If any of them promise editorial independence, you would be breaching your own paper.
- **Liability:** Calling something your pick implies you vetted it. If the winning app has a security incident or fails buyers, "we endorsed it" is a bad position, and you didn't vet it because the highest bid won.

## 2. The secret won't hold 🔴

- The winner will want to say "Featured by Brimhollow" in their own marketing. Either they publicize it, which hints at the pay-to-play, or you forbid it, which cuts the value they're paying for.
- At least 8 partner companies know the auction exists. Losing bidders talk to each other, and ex-employees and journalists can find out too.
- Money trails exist: invoices, bid records, and finance and legal staff who know what the revenue line is.
- One leak doesn't only expose this slot. It retroactively poisons every editorial pick you've made and every one you'll make.

## 3. Your whole curation signal gets devalued 🟠

Buyers trust the marketplace because it filters. Once the hero slot is bought, the question becomes "which other placements are for sale?" Search rankings, category leaders, and "recommended for you" all inherit the doubt. You are trading the asset that makes the marketplace worth visiting for one slot's revenue.

## 4. Partner dynamics 🟠

- **Rich-get-richer:** The biggest partners win most months. Small, innovative apps never get the slot, and the best new ones may leave.
- **Losers learn the game:** With 8 bidders, up to 7 lose each month. They quickly work out that "editorial" meant "paid", and some will demand to know whether other placements are also sold.
- **Arms race, then collusion:** Eight repeat bidders are a small group. They can rotate wins, bid the reserve, and collapse your price. Revenue is probably lower and less predictable than the pitch implies.
- **Existing commitments:** Partners may already have co-marketing deals, MDF, or promises of featured placement. Check for conflicts.
- **Complaints:** An undisclosed auction has no clean answer to "why did we lose?" without revealing what it is.

## 5. Buyer harm 🟠

Buyers weight the slot more because they think it's independent. If they pick on that basis and the app is a poor fit, you get refunds, support load, churn, and angry reviews that name Brimhollow. The highest bidder is the partner with the most marketing budget, which doesn't correlate with the best fit or the safest app.

## 6. Operations: partner marketing is two people 🟠

Every month someone has to:

1. Write and run auction rules, including a bid window and a tie-break.
2. Answer questions from up to 8 partners.
3. Run a quality or security check on the winner, so you aren't endorsing something unsafe.
4. Invoice, collect, and handle disputes.
5. Write "editorial" copy for an app you chose by price. That is itself a fabricated endorsement.
6. Debrief the losers.

That's 12 cycles a year on a team of 2, plus whatever they already do. The cost may eat much of the revenue. It also means one person may be fielding partner complaints about a scheme they know is deceptive. That's an internal morale and retention risk.

## 7. Reputational downside is asymmetric 🔴

The upside is one slot's revenue per month. The downside is a story like "Brimhollow sells fake endorsements," which is a news cycle, a partner trust crisis, and possibly a regulator letter. A scheme that works only if nobody ever finds out is a poor trade for that downside.

## 8. Numbers you need before deciding ⏳

I don't have your figures, so these are questions, not estimates:

- What's the realistic clearing price? Ask partners informally what a disclosed slot is worth, then discount for collusion.
- How much of marketplace revenue or partner-sourced revenue is at risk if trust drops 5%?
- What does a month of the 2-person team's time cost?
- What conversion lift does the hero slot give today, and how much of that comes from the "editorial" framing?

## What I'd do instead ✅

1. **Disclose, and still sell it.** Rename it "Sponsored Spotlight" (or similar) with a visible label. Partners still pay because the slot is still prime real estate, and no one is being lied to. The honest cost is that it converts less than a fake endorsement would. That gap is the part of the value that came from deception.
2. **Keep a separate, unpaid editorial pick.** It stays credible, and it makes the sponsored slot easier for buyers to accept.
3. **Prefer fixed pricing over a bid auction.** Sell 12 monthly slots at set prices, first-come or with a lottery among eligible partners. That cuts ops work, kills collusion dynamics, and makes revenue predictable.
4. **Set a quality gate.** Sponsored apps must pass a security and listing-quality check. Publish the rule.
5. **Publish the rules.** Eligibility, price tiers, how editorial picks are chosen. Partners can plan, and losers have nothing to resent.
6. **Pilot it for one quarter.** Measure revenue, buyer conversion and refund rate on the slot, partner NPS, and ops hours. Decide on the data.
7. **Look at other revenue levers** if pressure is high: premium listing tiers, co-marketing packages, lead-gen fees, take-rate changes. None of them put your credibility at risk.
