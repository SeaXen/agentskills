## 1. The benchmark table

**Pull it.** The comparison fails for three reasons.

1. **Wrong age.** AppExchange launched in 2005 and Atlassian's marketplace in about 2012. Both have had ten years or more of compounding. Comparing your week 6 to their year 15 shows age, not performance.
2. **Wrong unit.** "575 apps, 250 ISVs" and "1,000 add-ons" are listing counts from different dates and sources. Each marketplace counts differently. Atlassian add-ons include many free, tiny, and abandoned ones. I can't verify the analyst's figures from here, and she should cite a source and date for each. If she can't, drop them.
3. **Wrong metric.** App count measures supply. Your board cares whether the marketplace creates value for customers and partners. A 1,000-app catalog with a long tail of dead listings is not a better marketplace than 28 apps people use.

**Replace it with:**

- **Your own trajectory.** Weekly installs, active installs, and apps listed, from launch to week 6.
- **A day-one versus week-6 retention view.** The 2,100 installs are day one, so they mostly measure launch buzz. Show what happened after.
- **Early-stage reference points only if you can source them.** Use the first 6 to 12 months of comparable marketplaces, not their current size. If you can't get that data, say "no like-for-like benchmark exists" and move on. That is a more honest line than a bad table.

If a board member asks about AppExchange, the answer is: "They have a 20-year head start. We track our own cohort curve, and here it is."

## 2. What the readout should say

**Headline:** Launch worked on supply and demand signals. Whether it creates durable value is still unproven at 6 weeks, and here is how we will find out.

Cover these items in this order:

1. **Supply:** 28 apps live. Say how many were launch partners versus organic submissions. Say how many have more than N installs.
2. **Demand:** 2,100 day-one installs. Say how many are unique customers, since one customer installing 5 apps counts as 5. Say what share were installed in the first 48 hours.
3. **Retention, the number that matters most:** the share of day-one installs still active at day 7, 14, and 42. If you don't have it, say so and make it the first thing you build.
4. **Concentration:** what share of installs come from the top 3 apps. If it is above about 50%, the "28 apps" figure overstates breadth.
5. **Revenue or business impact,** if any, with the attribution method stated.
6. **What we don't know yet:** causal effect of featuring, install-to-active conversion, partner payback.
7. **Next 6 weeks:** the metrics to track and the decision each one informs.

## 3. The "doubles installs" claim

**Don't publish it.** One great week after being featured doesn't support a causal claim. The problems:

- **Single data point.** One partner, one week. No baseline variance.
- **Confounds.** Was the feature timed with their own email blast, a product release, a webinar, or a seasonal bump? Any of these could explain a spike.
- **Regression to the mean.** Partners get featured after a good stretch, so the next week often looks like a drop anyway.
- **No control.** You have no comparable unfeatured app to compare against.
- **"Doubles" is a promise.** Other partners will read it as a guarantee, expect it, and be disappointed. Your own credibility is the cost.
- **Possible regulatory or contractual exposure** if the claim is used in marketing. Check with legal before anything goes out.

**What you can do:**

1. **Publish the observation, not the law.** For example: "Partner X saw installs rise from A to B in the week after being featured." State the numbers, the dates, and that it is one case.
2. **Check the basics first.** Compare the featured week to the partner's prior 4 weeks. Strip out installs from their own campaigns using UTM or referral source. Look at whether the lift persisted in weeks 2 and 3.
3. **Turn it into a test.** Feature partners on a rotation and hold out a comparable set. A few rotations give you a real lift estimate with a range. Then you can say "featured apps see roughly X to Y times baseline installs," with the sample size.
4. **Offer the partner something they can use.** A case study with the real numbers and an honest caveat is more credible than "doubles." You can give them a quote or a data card.

## 4. Suggested wording for the board deck
