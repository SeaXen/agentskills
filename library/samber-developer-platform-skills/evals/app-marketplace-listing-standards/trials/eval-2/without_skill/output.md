# Parcelwise App Directory: Description Quality Bar and Rejection Triggers

## Your three open calls

| Question | Decision |
|---|---|
| Name "Parcelwise Label Printer" | ❌ Reject as submitted. Accept "Label Printer for Parcelwise" or a vendor-brand name. |
| Opener (a) vs (b) | ✅ Accept (b). ❌ Reject (a) as an opener. |
| Drive PDF + "warehouse manager in Ohio" quote | ❌ Reject the support surface. ❌ Reject the testimonial. Both are returnable for fixes. |

### Why the name fails
- "Parcelwise" is your platform's mark, and a leading platform name reads as first-party or endorsed.
- Merchants will assume you built it, support it, or vouch for it.
- The rule is that the vendor's own brand leads. Your name may appear only as a compatibility reference ("for Parcelwise", "works with Parcelwise").
- Exception: a signed partner or trademark license on file. I don't know whether this vendor has one, so check.
- "Label Printer" alone is generic. The name must also be distinguishable from other listings, so a vendor-brand name or "VendorCo Label Printer for Parcelwise" works.

### Why (b) wins
- (b) opens on the merchant's problem and outcome, in the merchant's words. (a) opens on the vendor's architecture, which is a "how" that belongs lower down.
- (b) still has to be true. The listing must show the app actually detects stuck shipments, which a reviewer can confirm in the feature list or a demo.
- (a)'s detail isn't banned, only relocated. "Sub-second latency" is a performance claim and needs a stated measurement basis wherever it appears.

## Description quality bar

A description must meet all of these to publish.

**Structure**
1. The first sentence states the merchant's job or outcome. It is at most 25 words, with no architecture, protocol, or acronym jargon.
2. The next 2–3 sentences say who the app is for and what it does, using concrete nouns (labels, manifests, carriers, exceptions).
3. A separate "How it works" or "Technical details" section holds implementation detail such as protocols, sync mechanics, and latency.
4. Length is 80–300 words in the main description. Bullets are fine.

**Truthfulness**
5. Every capability described exists in the current released version. "Coming soon" features go in a labeled roadmap line or are cut.
6. Every named integration (carrier, ERP, printer) is actually supported. Third-party names appear only in a nominative, factual way ("works with X"), with no implied partnership.
7. Quantitative claims (speed, savings, accuracy) state baseline, timeframe, and source. Without these, remove them or soften to non-numeric language.
8. Superlatives ("fastest", "#1", "best") need a cited, dated comparison. Otherwise they are removed.
9. Required setup, plan tier, or extra cost needed for a described feature is stated.

**Trust and consistency**
10. The data the app reads and writes, and its permission scopes, are plainly stated and match what the app actually requests at install.
11. Pricing is described, or the listing links to a pricing page on the vendor's domain. There are no "free" claims that hide a paywall.
12. Testimonials need a named person, role, and company, plus consent on file. An anonymized quote is allowed only if a reviewer has verified it and the descriptor is real (industry and company size, not just a region).
13. A single-customer result is labeled as that customer's result, never as typical.
14. No endorsement implication. Words such as "official", "certified", "recommended by Parcelwise", and "verified" are reserved for badges you award.

**Support surface**
15. At least one monitored channel (email, help desk, or form) on a vendor-controlled domain, with a stated response time.
16. A documentation or help URL that is a stable HTML page on the vendor's domain. A PDF may supplement it but never be the only surface.
17. A reviewer sends a test message during review, and a reply within the stated window is part of approval.

**Language and form**
18. English, readable by a non-specialist, no keyword stuffing, no ALL-CAPS runs, no emoji strings.
19. No phone numbers, personal emails, or off-platform purchase links in the description body.
20. No disparagement of named competitors.

## Rejection triggers

### Hard reject (no resubmission until fixed)
- **R1.** The listing name contains "Parcelwise" in lead position without a license on file.
- **R2.** The listing implies platform endorsement, certification, or partnership (rule 14).
- **R3.** The only support surface is a file-sharing link (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, a public personal doc). These can be revoked, aren't versioned, and often expose a personal account.
- **R4.** The listing has no monitored support channel, or the reviewer test message goes unanswered.
- **R5.** A testimonial is anonymous or unverifiable, or the quoted person doesn't exist or didn't consent.
- **R6.** An unsubstantiated numeric claim is presented as a general result ("cut costs 40%" with no baseline, period, or source).
- **R7.** Described features don't exist in the released app, or a listed integration isn't supported.
- **R8.** Permissions or data use in the description contradict the actual install scopes.
- **R9.** The description discloses nothing about what data is accessed.

### Return for edit (fixable in a single resubmission)
- **E1.** The opener is implementation-first (as in candidate (a)). Rewrite it outcome-first.
- **E2.** Superlatives are present without a cited comparison.
- **E3.** A "coming soon" feature is mixed into the capability list.
- **E4.** Pricing or plan requirements are missing.
- **E5.** The text is over 300 words, or is jargon-heavy or keyword-stuffed.
- **E6.** A testimonial is real but lacks full attribution. Add name, role, and company.

## Two things to confirm before you publish the rulebook
1. **Partner or license status for this vendor.** It decides whether R1 can ever be waived, and what the waiver process looks like.
2. **Whether your lawyers want a jurisdiction-specific testimonial rule.** US endorsement rules treat undisclosed material connections and atypical results differently from EU rules. Rules 12–13 are written conservatively, but this is a product-policy draft, not legal advice.
