## Verdict

Don't publish monthly ARR. Give the founder the growth story he wants through a route that doesn't backfire on the exact buyer you're selling to.

## Why this specific number, on this specific audience, backfires

Your buyer is a platform engineering lead who has to carry your tool through procurement and a security review at an insurance company or a bank. That's the "company adoption" reader, not an indie-hacker audience — they're building a case to an approver, and the evidence that wins that case is release cadence, response times, security posture, and proof you can be maintained. Revenue is not on that checklist. It shows up on a *different* checklist: vendor-risk / financial-viability review, which regulated-industry procurement runs privately, under NDA, off real financials — not off a number on your marketing site.

So a public ARR number does nothing for the review that actually gates your deals. It does something to the *other* side of the table:

- **$610k ARR next to the incumbents you're actually competing against in insurance/banking RFPs (Collibra, Alation, Manta, IBM) reads as small, not fast-growing.** A prospect's procurement team doesn't see "154% growth," they see "will this vendor still exist to support our data lineage program in three years" — the standard vendor-continuity objection, and you'd be handing it to them for free instead of making them dig for it.
- **Your two numbers together leak a third one.** $610k ARR ÷ ~$48k average contract ≈ 13 customers. That's a real number a competitor's sales rep will say out loud in your next competitive bake-off: "they have about a dozen customers total." You'd be giving away customer-count intelligence you never decided to disclose — nobody asked for that, it just falls out of the two figures you're both agreeing to publish monthly.
- **Monthly is a standing commitment, not a post.** The day a bank contract doesn't renew, one $48k account is 8% of ARR — a visible, dated dip, live on your site, mid-cycle with some other bank's security review. Silence or a flat month reads as decline whether or not it is one. You can't publish this selectively; once it's monthly, it's monthly through the bad months too.
- **Climbing down costs more than never climbing.** If this gets uncomfortable in twelve months, deleting the page is its own announcement — "why did the number disappear" is a worse conversation than the one you're avoiding now.

Run it through the four-question pre-publish check and it fails on its own: *would this sentence be a problem if a competitor quoted it alone, in front of your prospect?* Yes — "13 customers, $610k ARR" is a gift to whoever you're up against in the next bank RFP.

One thing to check before this goes further, not a marketing call to make alone: does Northgate have outside investors? If so, this stops being your and the founder's decision at all — investor rights agreements typically make financial disclosure a legal question first. Confirm that before this conversation goes any further with him.

## What actually gets the founder what he wants

He wants "prove we're real and growing fast." That's a legitimate goal — just aim it at signals your buyer's security/procurement committee actually checks, and that a bad quarter can't turn against you:

- **Customer proof, with consent.** Named logos or case studies from an insurance or banking customer, if you can get sign-off — "how [Bank] cut lineage audit prep from 3 weeks to 2 days" does more for a platform engineering lead's internal pitch than any ARR figure ever will.
- **Security and compliance posture.** SOC 2 / ISO 27001 status, pen-test cadence, uptime history. This is literally what the security review is scoring you against — publish it prominently, it's free credibility that doesn't decay.
- **Release cadence and response times.** A changelog that ships regularly and a stated support SLA are exactly the "proof of life" signal he's after, and they cost you nothing to keep publishing since the material already exists from shipping.
- **Growth, told once a year, as a narrative, not a dashboard.** If he wants the 240k → 610k story out there, write it as a single annual post — "how we grew in [year], what worked, what we're fixing" — the way Plausible discloses milestones without running a live financial feed. One post, a specific mechanism behind the number, tied to something concrete (a cert earned, a segment won), not a running total anyone can subtract month-to-month.

That gets him a growth story that's true, told once a year at a moment he controls, without arming your competitors' sales decks every 30 days.

## The one-sentence version for the founder

"A growing number proves you're real to somebody deciding for themselves — it proves the opposite to a bank's procurement team sizing you up against Collibra. Tell the growth story once a year, in words; tell the trust story every day, in certs and case studies — that's the one they're actually scoring you on."
