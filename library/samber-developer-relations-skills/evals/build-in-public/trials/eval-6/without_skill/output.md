**Recommendation: don't publish monthly ARR. Give him the growth story a different way — annually, aggregated, on a trust page, next to the things this buyer actually checks.**

**Why monthly ARR is the wrong instrument here**

The "publish your numbers" playbook (Buffer, Baremetrics, the indie-SaaS transparency crowd) works when you have thousands of small, homogeneous subscribers and the audience is other founders or a consumer/prosumer crowd who read growth as proof of product-market fit. None of that applies to you:

- At $48k average contract, $610k ARR is roughly 13 customers. $240k was roughly 5. Every one of those logos is worth ~8% of total ARR. A monthly chart isn't a growth trend at this scale — it's a deal ticker. A competitor, or a prospect's procurement team, can read a jump and know you closed a large regulated account that month. A flat or down month reads as a churned bank or insurer. You'd effectively be disclosing customer-level events without naming anyone, which for this buyer is often just as sensitive as naming them.
- Insurance and banking companies routinely restrict vendors from doing anything that lets a third party infer they're a customer — sometimes contractually, always culturally. A number that visibly steps up in lockstep with a known signing is close enough to identification to create exactly the kind of confidentiality problem your buyers are the most sensitive to. You'd be handing your own customers a reason to be annoyed with you.
- The audience that actually gates your deal — platform engineering lead, then procurement, then security review — doesn't buy on a public ARR ticker. They buy on SOC 2 Type II report, pen test results, references from similar regulated companies, data handling docs, and a security questionnaire. A self-reported, unaudited number on your marketing site carries close to zero evidentiary weight with a bank's vendor risk team; it's not the kind of proof their process is built to accept.
- Monthly granularity also undersells you. Enterprise sales in regulated industries is lumpy — deals slip a quarter for budget cycles or a re-signed vendor risk assessment, not because you did anything wrong. A monthly view will show plateaus and dips that the annual number (240k → 610k, +154%) completely hides. You'd be trading your best statistic for a noisier, worse-looking one.

**What actually moves this buyer**

Vendor viability — "will this company still exist to support us in three years" — is a legitimate line item in bank/insurer procurement. So the founder's underlying instinct (prove we're real, prove we're not going away) is correct. It's the format that's wrong.

Give him this instead:

- One aggregate growth stat, updated annually (or at a real milestone), on an About/Trust page: "$610k ARR in 2025, up from $240k in 2024." Same proof point, none of the deal-level leakage.
- Put it next to what procurement actually reads: SOC 2 report link, security/data-handling page, customer count by sector ("13 enterprise customers across banking and insurance") without naming names, and anonymized case studies if you can get sign-off ("A top-20 US insurer cut lineage audit prep from X weeks to Y days").
- If you want a bigger single moment, do a press release or a "state of the company" blog post at year-end — one clean, controlled disclosure instead of a live feed a competitor can watch.

**How to pitch the "no" to the founder**

Frame it as protecting the deals, not gatekeeping his idea: "Monthly ARR tells our competitors and our customers' competitors exactly when we close a bank or insurer — at our size, one deal is 8% of the number. I want the growth story to help us close the next one, not get read out loud to the account we just signed. Let's put the same 154% growth number on a trust page next to our SOC 2 report, updated once a year — same proof, zero risk to the customers we're trying to protect." That gives him the "prove we're real" outcome he's after without the exposure.
