## Should you scrub Quillstack out entirely? No.

That instinct is right for a talk at a neutral, non-Quillstack conference — there, naming your own product reads as an ad. QuillCon is different: everyone in the room is already a Quillstack customer or a prospect evaluating Quillstack. A talk that hides the product would feel evasive, and it throws away the thing this audience actually came for — proof of how the platform holds up at real scale.

The pitch risk at your own user conference isn't *mentioning* the product. It's *sounding like marketing instead of an operations story*. Concrete test: if a sentence would need editing to also run as a case study on a competitor's blog, cut it — it's doing brand work, not storytelling. If it only makes sense because it's Quillstack-specific detail (which workflow patterns Harbor Dental used, what broke, what they changed), keep it plain and leave it in.

So: keep the product name and the specific mechanics. Cut superlatives, roadmap teases, and anything that reads like "why Quillstack is the best." Center Harbor Dental's ops team as the protagonist — ideally have their ops lead present or co-present, with Quillstack DevRel in a moderator/interviewer role rather than the narrator.

### Abstract (~200 words)

Harbor Dental Group runs 90 clinics and roughly 40,000 workflow executions a day — patient intake, insurance verification, scheduling, supply reordering, compliance checks — coordinated through Quillstack. [X time period] ago, that same coordination work sat on a small central ops team and a set of clinic-level judgment calls that hadn't kept pace with the company's growth.

This talk is not a product walkthrough. It's what the ops team had to unlearn and rebuild once workflow orchestration took the coordination load off their plates: which roles shrank, which grew, which decisions moved from headquarters to the clinic floor (or the other way), and where automation created new problems — approval bottlenecks, edge cases the workflows couldn't handle, staff who needed new skills — before it solved the old ones.

[Ops lead name, title] led the restructuring and will walk through the org chart before and after, the metrics that mattered to leadership versus the ones that mattered to clinic staff, and what they'd do differently. Attendees leave with a framework for checking whether their own ops structure still matches the workflow volume they run today, not the one they were staffed for years ago.

### Key takeaways (for the CFP form's "attendees will learn" field)

1. A framework for checking whether your ops org chart matches your actual workflow volume, not your historical headcount plan.
2. What broke first when Harbor Dental automated coordination work, and how they told an org problem apart from a workflow-design problem.
3. How to set success metrics that satisfy both leadership (cost, throughput) and frontline staff (workload, clarity) — so a restructuring doesn't land as a layoff story.
