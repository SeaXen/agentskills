No — that clause does not clear you to publish this. Here's exactly why, and what it does cover.

## The clause only settles one thing: the logo wall

"Licence to use and reproduce Customer's trademarks, trade names and logos... and to indicate that Customer is a customer" is a trademark-use grant. It's the standard clause found in self-serve SaaS terms (Vercel's §16.4 reads almost identically). It settles: you can put Coastline Rail's name and logo on a customer page and say "Coastline Rail uses us."

It settles **nothing else**. None of the following is a trademark use, so none of it is licensed by that sentence:

- A quote from their staff engineer.
- The throughput numbers.
- The architecture diagram.
- Any narrative about how their systems ran before or after.

Publishing any of those on the strength of a logo clause is a known way to end a reference relationship — the customer agreed to be listed as a customer, not to have their engineer's words, their internal numbers, and a diagram of their infrastructure put in front of the market. With a deal closing this quarter, that's the exact risk you don't want: a customer who feels published-on-without-asking is a customer who gets frosty with your sales team mid-close, not after.

## What you actually need — four separate permissions

| Permission | Usual holder | Typical delay |
|---|---|---|
| Being quoted and named, with title | The staff engineer | Immediate |
| Spending the team's credibility on it | Their manager | Days |
| Company name / logo / published wording | Comms or marketing (contract already covers the trademark part, but they'll still want to see the piece it's attached to) | Days to weeks |
| The throughput numbers and the architecture diagram | Security, or legal for a regulated/infra business like rail | Weeks |

Rail is the kind of company where "architecture diagram + throughput numbers" is plausibly the sort of detail a security team has opinions about. Budget for that review existing even if nobody's mentioned it yet.

## How to ask without it reading as a big legal ask

The instinct to avoid a scary formal request is right — but the fix isn't skipping the ask, it's making it small and concrete. An open-ended "can we publish about you?" is what triggers a slow, cautious legal review. A bounded, already-drafted request is what gets a fast yes, because there's nothing for anyone to fill in themselves:

1. Write the draft first, quotes and numbers already in place, exactly as you intend to publish it.
2. Send it to the engineer and their manager with specific items marked: "these three quotes," "these two numbers, sourced to X dashboard over Y window," "this diagram."
3. Ask in the same message who else needs to sign off (security/legal) — don't wait to discover that after they've already said yes.
4. Give a dated deadline and a stated default ("if we don't hear back by the 25th, we hold — not publish without approval").

That reads as "we already did the work, just confirm" rather than "please review our marketing plan" — the version people say yes to in days, not weeks.

## If Coastline Rail balks at the full version

Don't drop the story — descend one rung:

1. Named company, named engineer — what you're aiming for.
2. Named company, unnamed engineer ("a staff engineer on Coastline Rail's platform team").
3. Unnamed company, identifying descriptor ("a European rail operator's platform team") — numbers now carry the whole piece.
4. Category descriptor only.

Each rung costs credibility; take the highest one they'll actually clear rather than the fastest one.

## One more thing before you draft

Pre-redact what review predictably strikes, so the first draft you send survives: absolute infrastructure sizes/costs, internal service or dashboard names, and anything implying a weakness in their prior setup. Use ratios ("cut latency by a third") instead of absolutes where you can — they usually clear faster and travel just as well for a reader.

**Bottom line:** the contract clears the name and logo. It does not clear the quotes, the numbers, or the diagram. Get those three cleared with a tight, already-drafted, deadlined ask — that's the version that doesn't spook anyone and still closes before quarter-end.
