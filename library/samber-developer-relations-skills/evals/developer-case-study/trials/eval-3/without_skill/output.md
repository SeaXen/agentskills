No — that clause doesn't get you there. It's much narrower than what you want to publish.

## What the clause actually grants

"Non-exclusive, royalty-free licence to use and reproduce Customer's trademarks, trade names and logos in Vendor's marketing materials and to indicate that Customer is a customer."

That's a **logo/name-badge licence** — the standard "we can say X is a customer and put their logo on our site" clause. It covers exactly three things: their trademarks, their trade name, and the fact of the customer relationship.

## What it does not cover

- **A named individual's quotes.** Attributing words to a real, named staff engineer is a personal quote/endorsement, not a trademark use. Trademark licences don't carry authority over what an employee is quoted saying — that's the employee's (and typically their employer's comms/legal function's) call, separately. If the quote is misremembered, paraphrased, or reads differently out of context than intended, you're exposed regardless of what the trademark clause says.
- **Throughput numbers.** Performance data about Coastline Rail's system almost certainly falls under a confidentiality clause elsewhere in the same MSA — trademark licences don't override confidentiality obligations, and the two are usually treated as entirely separate rights in the contract. Unless there's a specific carve-out permitting disclosure of performance metrics in case studies, publishing their numbers is a likely confidentiality breach even though you're technically allowed to say their name.
- **An architecture diagram.** This is the highest-risk item. It's technical infrastructure detail about a rail operator — a sector where architecture disclosure can be treated as sensitive well beyond ordinary commercial confidentiality (operational/critical-infrastructure concerns, not just "is this a trade secret"). Nothing in a logo-licence clause touches this.

## Why this matters right now

Publishing a case study with a named client, an attributed quote, live metrics, and their architecture — without them having seen the specific draft — is a common way vendors trigger exactly the reaction you're trying to avoid: legal pushback, a retraction demand, or a chilled relationship, right when you don't want friction. If Coastline Rail sees their staff engineer's name and internal numbers online without having signed off on the specific piece, that's a worse spook than asking for a quick review would have been, and it lands during your close, not before it.

## What actually clears you

You don't need a "big legal ask" — you need a small, fast, specific one:

1. **Quote sign-off**: send the exact quote to the staff engineer (or whoever handles external comms on their side) for a one-line yes/no. Takes them thirty seconds.
2. **Numbers check**: confirm the throughput figures aren't confidential, or get them explicitly cleared — ask the same contact, same email.
3. **Diagram check**: have someone on their side glance at the diagram to confirm nothing sensitive or inaccurate is shown.

Bundle all three into a single short email with the draft attached, framed as "want to make sure we've represented this accurately before it goes out" — that's a normal, low-friction ask, not a legal escalation, and it's the version of this that doesn't come back to bite the deal later.

The trademark clause does clear you to use their name and logo and say "Coastline Rail is a customer" without asking again. Everything else in the piece still needs their eyes on it.

*I'm not your lawyer and this isn't legal advice — if the deal size or the rail-infrastructure sensitivity is high enough that a dispute would actually hurt, have counsel confirm the confidentiality clause's scope before you publish.*
