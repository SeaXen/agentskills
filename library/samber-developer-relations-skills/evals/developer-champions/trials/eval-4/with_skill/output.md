No, it doesn't hold up. All three pieces trip a documented failure mode in the developer-champions skill (Step 3 + `selection-rubric.md`).

**1. The single hard requirement is the exact anti-pattern the skill names**

Step 3: *"use the two-or-more pattern: list four to six qualifying activities and require any two... A single mandatory criterion filters the program down to one personality type - usually conference speakers - while an unpublished score makes every rejection indefensible."*

That line describes your plan almost word for word. A conference talk is a legitimate criterion (it maps to "Teaching in public" in the six-criteria list), but making it the sole gate excludes people doing the work this skill weights highest: support-question answerers, mentors, sustained contributors, non-paywalled content creators. You'd end up with a roster of good speakers, some of whom never touch the product day to day.

Fix: publish 4-6 qualifying activities (contribution, community leadership, teaching, mentorship, content, support work) and require any two. Conference talk becomes one path in, not the only one.

**2. Publishing the full leaderboard defeats the transparency goal instead of serving it**

The rubric is explicitly two-stage: *"Stage 1 is a gate... Stage 2 is a rank, used only when qualified candidates outnumber the slots. Publish the gate; keep the rank internal. A published score turns every cycle into a leaderboard dispute, while an unpublished gate makes every rejection indefensible."*

Transparency comes from publishing the criteria and applying them consistently - not from publishing a 0-100 score for every applicant. A public leaderboard does the opposite of preventing favouritism accusations: it invites everyone to litigate the point differences ("why did they get 62 and I got 58"), which a published rule set never has to defend. Score only the overflow (qualified candidates beyond your cohort size), and keep that score internal.

**3. Weighting follower count at 30/100 contradicts the rubric's own logic, directly**

The sourced scoring table weights nine signals from ×5 down to ×1. Reach (followers, audience size) sits dead last at ×1 - below product depth, coverage-gap closed, direct contribution, content, teaching, mentoring, tenure, and support (×5, the highest). The skill's stated reason:

*"reach can be granted by the platform perks; the willingness to answer a stranger's question at 23:00 cannot. A roster selected on reach fills with names that never show up."*

It's also named as one of the two anti-signals in Step 3 ("reach without product depth") and as its own row in the failure-modes table: *"Selection recruits big accounts who never show up | scored reach instead of engagement | weight support and mentoring signals above follower counts."*

30 points out of 100 for the weakest, most gameable, most purchasable signal in the model is close to the inverse of what the rubric argues for. There's also a circularity problem worth naming: if the goal is "get our name in front of more people," that's what the **Platform perk** (Step 4 - reserved speaking slots, co-authoring, launch features) is for, allocated *after* you've selected for depth. Selecting on reach doesn't buy durable reach; it buys people who show up once, because that's the only thing they were selected for.

**What I'd write instead**

- Gate (published): meet 2 of 6 - contribution with a stated floor, sustained community leadership, teaching in public, mentorship, non-paywalled content, support/answering-questions.
- Rank (internal, overflow only): score 0-5 per signal × weight, support and mentoring weighted highest, reach weighted lowest or dropped entirely.
- Anti-signals stated publicly: reach without product depth, activity that starts the week applications open, condescension toward beginners, treating the title as compensation.
- What's published: the criteria, the anti-signals, and the accepted roster. What stays internal: the comparative score.

One flag on provenance: the exact weights (×5 down to ×1) are this skill's self-set judgment, not a benchmarked industry standard - it says so itself. You're free to recalibrate them to Sailmaker's actual priorities. But "reach at 30% of the total, above mentoring and tenure" isn't a recalibration, it's the specific failure case the skill was written to warn against - so if you want to keep a meaningful reach component, argue for it explicitly in the charter and expect it to read as favouring whoever already has the biggest platform, not as a fair criterion for developer expertise.
