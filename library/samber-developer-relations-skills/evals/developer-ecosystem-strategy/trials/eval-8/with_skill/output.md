# Cindermill — Threadmark: obligations and sequencing

The ecosystem decision here isn't in question — Cindermill is already at rung 3 (partner-built integrations, listed by you), it's producing real value, and Threadmark is proof it works. Nothing here re-opens that. What's in question is whether Cindermill is about to breach the obligations that rung already created, and the two "separate" questions are actually one decision made twice.

## The core problem: these are not two questions

You cannot honestly answer "what support would Threadmark get for a resold paid version" without first telling them a native competitor to their entire business ships in Q2 and gets announced in May. Answering the resale question first — and only surfacing the roadmap plan later — means inducing a partner to invest further in a product line you already know you're about to obsolete. That's not just bad optics if it comes out later (and in a shared user conference audience, it will come out); it's the specific failure this kind of platform relationship exists to prevent: a complementor whose whole business rides on your surface finding out its fate from a keynote slide instead of from you.

Rung 3 is explicitly the point where this stops being reversible-by-edit: another company's revenue depends on your surface. Threadmark isn't "a partner," they're a company whose business model *is* your integration surface. That changes what you owe them relative to the other ten.

## Decision 1 — Tell Threadmark now, before the resale conversation, before development locks further, before May

Building the same capability natively is a legitimate roadmap call — customer demand is unambiguous at 40% adoption, this is exactly the kind of complement that's sometimes correct to absorb into the core. What makes it a breach isn't the decision, it's the silence.

Concretely:

- **Notify Threadmark first, privately, before any further Q2 commitment hardens and well before the conference.** You have real lead time between now and May — use it. Finding out in the room is the one sequencing that turns a defensible product call into a credibility event for every other partner watching.
- **Loop in whoever owns the partner relationship before product finalizes messaging.** A decision that determines a partner's business shouldn't be made and scheduled by the product team alone; it has obligations attached that product doesn't own.
- **Give notice proportional to dependency, not to your standard deprecation window.** A generic 90-day API-change notice is the wrong instrument here — this is closer to a wind-down: Threadmark needs enough runway to plan a transition for their own customers, not just update code.
- **State the roadmap boundary honestly and put a date on the public announcement, decided jointly with the timeline you just gave Threadmward.** "We're building this natively, here's why, here's when it ships, here's when we're announcing it publicly" — not a soft "things may change."
- **Do not let Threadmark be surprised twice.** If the May conference is when *customers* hear it, Threadmark should not be hearing it for the first time at the conference either — give them the option to shape how their own customers hear it, on their own timeline, ahead of yours.

What you're protecting isn't just the Threadmark relationship. All ten other partners, and every prospective one, will read how this is handled as the answer to "is it safe to build a business on this company's surface." Unannounced absorption is the one thing that reliably kills future partner recruitment — not because of this deal alone, but because it becomes the story every future partner hears before they say yes.

## Decision 2 — Answer the resale question only after Decision 1, and don't let it imply a commitment you're not making

Once Threadmark has the roadmap information, the resale question becomes answerable honestly. Two honest ways to answer it — pick one, don't default into a third by omission:

**Option A — Bounded transition arrangement.** Tell Threadmark: yes, you can resell a paid version, for a defined window ending at [native GA + migration period], with a named support contact, an advance-notice commitment on any API changes during that window, and an explicit end date. This gives Threadmark a real, monetizable runway to fund their own transition instead of a cliff. It's the more constructive option, and it's the one that turns "we're about to blindside you" into "here's a fair, time-boxed deal with a known endpoint."

**Option B — Decline resale, offer a wind-down instead.** If a paid resale program can't coexist with the native launch (channel conflict, customer confusion, support ambiguity), say so directly, and pair the "no" with real wind-down terms: notice period, migration path for their existing installed base, and whatever transition support you're willing to commit — referral flow to whatever adjacent thing Threadmark pivots to, help messaging their existing customers, etc.

What's not acceptable is answering the resale question as if the native build weren't happening — approving an open-ended paid program this week, then Sherlocking it in Q2, leaves Threadmark holding commercial commitments to their own customers that you knew were about to be undercut.

Either way, run the honest test on Threadmark once they know the full picture: *if Cindermill ships this natively, what changes for Threadmark?* Their answer is "our business" — that's not a partner you can treat as a logo. It's the clearest possible signal that whatever obligation you attach here (transition window, support commitment, notice) should be generous, not minimal.

## One thing worth flagging past this specific decision

40% of accounts on a single partner integration is a concentration number worth tracking on its own, independent of this situation — it's the sign you've effectively been running one make-or-break partnership dressed up as an eleven-partner ecosystem. Whoever owns the partner program should be watching that number for the other ten too, so the next "let's build this natively" conversation is a planned roadmap boundary decision made with notice, not another surprise discovered after the fact.
