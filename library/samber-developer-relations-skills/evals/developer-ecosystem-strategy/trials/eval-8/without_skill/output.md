## The core problem

You have two questions, but they're actually one decision. Threadmark asked "can we sell a paid version, and what support do we get" — a question about *investing further* in this integration. You know something they don't: Cindermill is about to make that integration obsolete and announce it publicly in May. Answering their monetization question without disclosing that is not a neutral omission — it's actively steering a partner into wasting money and time on a business line you already know is ending. If Threadmark builds pricing, packaging, and a sales motion around a paid tier this spring, and then finds out in May at your own conference that you shipped a native replacement, that is the story: "Cindermill let its top partner walk into a wall while asking permission." That story travels to your other 10 partners immediately, and it's a worse outcome for Cindermill's platform reputation than any short-term competitive gain from a clean surprise launch.

So: don't answer the resale question in isolation. Fold it into a direct conversation with Threadmark about the roadmap, and have that conversation before you respond to their ask — not after, not concurrently with a generic "let us get back to you."

## Recommendation

1. **Escalate internally now, today.** This isn't a call for whoever owns the Threadmark relationship to make alone. Loop in Product leadership, Partnerships/BD, and Legal before anyone replies to Threadmark. The disclosure timing, the shape of any transition support, and the resale answer all have legal and revenue implications above one person's authority.

2. **Pull the partnership agreement before deciding anything else.** Check for: notice-period obligations before building competing functionality, API/data-access terms Cindermill could revoke or must honor, revenue-share or exclusivity clauses, and termination terms. This determines what you're legally obligated to do, not just what's fair. If nobody's read this contract in the last year, that's step zero.

3. **Tell Threadmark before you finalize anything for May, and before you answer their resale question.** Concretely: request a call in the next 1-2 weeks. The message is roughly:

   > "We're building native workflow automation that covers what your integration does today, targeting Q2, with plans to announce it at our May user conference. We wanted you to hear this directly from us, with real lead time, before we lock the announcement — because we know how central this integration is to your business, and because you're already asking us about investing further in it. We want to figure out the right transition together, not spring this on you."

   This is uncomfortable, but it's the only version of this conversation that doesn't look like betrayal in hindsight. Silence now, followed by a May surprise, is the version that ends the relationship badly and signals to every other integration partner that building deeply on Cindermill is a trap.

4. **Answer the resale question honestly, conditioned on the roadmap.** The honest answer isn't "yes" or "no" — it's: "We can't greenlight a new paid tier on this integration right now, because we're about to ship overlapping native functionality, and we don't want you building a business on top of something we're going to replace. Let's talk about what support and timeline actually make sense given that." Saying yes now and breaking the news in May is the one option that's actually worse than saying nothing.

5. **Decide, as leadership, whether pure replace-and-announce is even the right call**, given Threadmark is ~40% of your install base's integration and "basically their whole business." Options worth putting on the table in the Threadmark conversation, not deciding unilaterally beforehand:
   - A genuine sunset window (e.g., commit to N months of continued marketplace support/API stability post-launch) so their customers and revenue don't fall off a cliff the day you announce.
   - Grandfathering existing Threadmark customers rather than forcing an immediate migration.
   - An acquisition or acqui-hire conversation, if their execution is genuinely the reason it's your best integration — cheaper and faster than rebuilding their years of edge-case handling, and it converts a partner you're about to hurt into an internal team.
   - Early/privileged access to the native feature's APIs so Threadmark can pivot to building adjacent capability the native version won't cover, instead of being fully replaced.
   - A joint or coordinated announcement instead of a unilateral one at your conference — softer landing, no "gotcha" optics in front of your whole user base.

   None of these require abandoning the native build. They're about not treating a partner who did exactly what you asked partners to do — build deeply, drive 40% adoption — as collateral damage.

6. **Sequence the conference announcement after Threadmark has had real lead time**, not the other way around. If Product is set on May, that gives you roughly 6-8 weeks from now — enough time to have the disclosure conversation, negotiate a transition plan, and still hit the date, but only if you start this week instead of after their resale question sits in someone's inbox.

## What not to do

Don't let this get answered by whoever fields partner emails as a routine "sure, resell away, here's our support tiers" response while the roadmap conversation stays siloed in Product. That's the scenario where Cindermill ends up having *encouraged in writing* the exact investment it knew was about to be wasted — which is worse legally and reputationally than either saying yes cleanly or saying no cleanly. The failure mode here isn't "we compete with a partner" — platforms do that constantly, and it's usually fine. The failure mode is doing it while a partner is actively asking you, in good faith, whether it's safe to invest more, and letting that question go unanswered or misleadingly answered because the news is inconvenient.
