## Decision: ❌ Don't sign — decline DevTide Expo as pitched, and don't let the 9‑day clock force it

This isn't a close call. DevTide trips four of the five explicit walk-away signals for a candidate event, and one of its "perks" is on the flat-out-forbidden list. Here's the reasoning, in the order it should have killed the deal.

### 1. The $2.83/head number is the wrong metric, and it's unfalsifiable

Cost-per-attendee is explicitly the least informative number a sponsor can be sold on — total headcount tells you nothing about whether those 12,000 people are platform engineers who'd buy a CI caching layer, or general-admission badge count at a brand-new expo. The rep is leaning on exactly the metric the evidence request exists to replace.

Do the math on what actually matters — cost per *reachable* ICP attendee:

- Generous assumption (20% of attendees are infra/platform-eng-adjacent, unusually high for an unqualified general expo): 2,400 people → **$14/head**
- Conservative assumption (8%): 960 people → **$35/head**

That range straddles the $40+ you're paying at your smaller conferences. The honest answer is "we don't know, and neither does DevTide" — because role breakdowns don't exist yet. Your other events' $40+ is a real number backed by an audience you can describe; DevTide's $2.83 is marketing arithmetic with an unknown denominator. Comparing them at all is the tier-name trap: two numbers that look comparable and aren't.

### 2. This event fails qualification on its own, before price ever comes up

Run it against the standard qualification bar and it matches the walk-away list almost line for line:

| Walk-away trigger | DevTide |
|---|---|
| Unverifiable demographics | ✓ — no role breakdown exists; registration opened a month ago |
| First-time organizer making large promises | ✓ — 12,000 attendees, first edition, zero track record |
| Sells sponsor benefits harder than attendee experience | ✓ — the entire pitch is a sponsor-facing cost ratio, not audience quality |
| Attendee list offered as a deliverable | ✓ — see below |
| Deadline pressure arriving before the audience data does | ✓ — 9 days, price escalator, still no role data |

Scored on the standard grid (ICP overlap, decision access, hands-on path, track record, density, staffing, cost-per-reachable-attendee, compounding), this lands in the same bracket as the reference "decline" case — a first-edition, unqualified general expo scoring ~27/75 — and worse on one axis: **ICP overlap can't be scored above 0-1 with no role data, and a 0 on ICP overlap ends the evaluation regardless of everything else.** Size (12,000 vs. that case's 6,000) doesn't fix that; it's the same event, just bigger and with a harder sell.

### 3. The 9-day / 20% clause is manufactured urgency, not a real constraint

Real capacity deadlines look like "booth space is sold out" or "contracts close a quarter out because floor plans lock then." A price that jumps 20% if you don't sign *this week*, on a first-edition event with no published demographics, isn't a capacity signal — it's pressure timed to arrive before you can get the evidence that would let you say no intelligently. Treat the deadline as their problem, not yours. Letting it lapse costs you nothing; a real event with real demand doesn't need to escalate price to close a deal in nine days.

### 4. The attendee-list offer is disqualifying, not a bonus

This is the one item here that isn't a judgment call. An attendee list the attendees never consented to receiving sponsor contact from is on the short list of things never to buy, full stop — in the same category as a bought keynote or a rigged judging outcome. Registration opened a month ago; there is no plausible consent mechanism in place yet for "we'll hand your contact details to sponsors after the show." For a company selling to platform engineering teams — an audience that notices exactly this kind of behavior — receiving and using that list is a reputational story waiting to happen, not a growth hack. If anything, the fact that "nobody else offers this" should read as a red flag about how DevTide is positioning itself to sponsors, not as a differentiator in your favor.

### What would change this answer

Not "never sponsor DevTide" — "not this, not now, not on these terms." Reconsider only if, without the clock:

- They publish actual (not projected) role/seniority/company-size breakdowns — even partial, from current registrations.
- They drop the attendee-list offer entirely, or replace it with standard opt-in badge-scan consent.
- You get a written attendance remedy (partial refund/credit if they miss their number materially) — the standard ask for any first edition, since an unmet estimate is the whole risk on a new event.

None of that is gettable in 9 days from an organizer that doesn't have the data yet. If DevTide is worth revisiting, it's worth revisiting after their first edition actually runs and produces a post-event report — the same patience you'd extend to any other first-time event, and exactly why a first edition never clears the "sign now" bar on urgency alone.

### If the budget is looking for a home

The $34K would buy real floor presence at an established program with published, verifiable demographics — e.g., PyCon US's Contributing tier ($33,500, 2,500+ actual attendees, audience data published before price) — at a known cost-per-verified-attendee instead of a projected one. That's the comparison worth making, not DevTide-vs-DevTide's-own-marketing-math.
