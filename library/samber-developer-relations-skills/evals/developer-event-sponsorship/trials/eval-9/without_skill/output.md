## The core problem

Your board deadline is 8 weeks out. October is roughly 7 months out. A "splashy workshop track" that hasn't happened yet gives the board a plan and a big line-item, not evidence. Meanwhile your February conference — already contracted, already paid for — will have *happened* before the board meets. That's your actual concrete deliverable on March 14: real numbers from a real event, not a pitch for a hypothetical one.

Second problem: Marlstone is self-serve at $59/month. That's a low-ACV, short-cycle, product-led motion. A splashy hands-on workshop *track* at a big conference is an enterprise-ABM tactic — built for multi-stakeholder, high-touch, long-cycle deals. It's a mismatch for your buyer, who signs up on their own and doesn't need a stage production to be convinced. That doesn't kill the workshop idea — hands-on labs are genuinely good for PLG, because they drive trial activation — but it does kill "big conference, dedicated track, centerpiece of the budget" as the shape of it.

## What to do with the $45k

Don't make one big bet. Stage it — each stage funded by evidence from the last.

**February (already committed) — spend small, extract maximum data**
- Net out whatever the smallest-tier sponsorship already cost from the $45k; it's sunk.
- Add ~$3,000: build a pre-event target list of ICP attendees, book 15–30 meetings *before* you arrive, and host a small dinner (8–12 target accounts) alongside the conference. A side dinner regularly outperforms a booth, at a fraction of the cost — and your tier is small, so the booth alone won't carry the event.
- Instrument it properly: track qualified conversations and meetings booked, not badge scans. This is the data you bring to the board.

**May regional DevOps day — the real 80/20 test (~$7,500)**
- Regional/niche events (50–200 attendees) typically beat mega-conferences on cost-per-qualified-meeting — less noise, less dilution, more of your actual ICP in the room.
- Sponsor a modest tier (~$6,000), send one engineer to run a small hands-on lab session — not a track, a single 45-minute "set up your first flag" session. Reuse the Feb dinner playbook for a smaller target list.
- This is your cheapest, fastest test of whether the hands-on-workshop format converts for a self-serve audience. If it works here, it's the evidence for October. If it doesn't, you just saved yourself from a much more expensive version of the same mistake.

**October — reserve, don't commit (~$12,000–15,000 held, not spent)**
- Tell your CEO: not no — *not yet*. Hold the reserve. If May's numbers show hands-on workshops convert, October becomes a funded bet with a track record behind it, not a gut call he has to defend to the board on vibes.
- If greenlit, scope it as a sponsored session or half-day workshop, not a full track — a full track at a big conference plus travel plus production will eat most or all of what's left and crowd out everything else this year.
- Reuse the May workshop content rather than building new material from scratch. Build once, run twice.

**Buffer (~$3,000–5,000)** — follow-up tooling, swag, contingency.

This leaves real budget unspent by design. That's the pitch to the board: disciplined staged deployment, not "we bet a third of the annual event budget on one October conference we haven't tested."

## What goes in front of the board on March 14

Not a plan. Results plus a plan:
- February: actual qualified meetings, opportunities created, cost per qualified meeting — compare it against what a cold-outbound or paid-ads meeting costs you today. That comparison is the real go/no-go signal for events as a channel at all.
- May: booked, budgeted, in flight — position it as an active low-cost experiment testing the hands-on-workshop format your CEO wants, before you scale it.
- October: a contingent recommendation ("fund at $X if May hits Y qualified meetings"), not a locked commitment. This gives the board a decision to ratify, not a fait accompli, and gives your CEO a path to his idea that's actually stronger than pushing it through unproven.

## Team allocation (2 engineers)

Don't burn both on Feb. One engineer supports Feb follow-up (live demos for hot conversations, capture a short recorded walkthrough — you already paid for the room, use it as a content shoot). The other spends the intervening weeks building the May hands-on lab content — reusable in October if it's approved, so you're not building "splashy" one-off content for a bet that isn't funded yet.

## What not to do

- Don't let "the CEO keeps bringing it up" turn into committing spend before you have a signal. Redirect the enthusiasm into the May test — it's the fastest, cheapest way to find out if he's right.
- Don't report badge scans or registrations to the board as success metrics. Qualified meetings and cost-per-meeting are the only numbers that will hold up under questioning.
- Don't skip the 24–48 hour follow-up window on Feb leads to save time — a late generic follow-up is the single most common way events quietly fail, and it'll wreck the very data you need for the board.
