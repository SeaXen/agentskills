## What's actually wrong

Five weeks silent, champion gone, renewal in four months — that's a **relationship risk signal**, not a price-sensitivity signal. Nothing in the situation says price is the issue. You don't know:
- Whether the three teams are still using the product at all.
- Who inherited the champion's responsibilities, or whether anyone did.
- Whether the silence is a reorg, a budget freeze, a vendor-review lapse, or just an unfilled headcount gap.

A discount answers a question nobody asked. Per the churn-prevention playbook: match the offer to the actual cancellation/risk reason, never apply a blanket offer before you know the reason — that's the #1 mistake in save-offer design, and it applies here even though this is enterprise, not self-serve.

## Why the specific plan backfires

- **Discounting before diagnosis wastes leverage.** If usage is healthy and someone just needs to re-sign, you give up ~$12k/year for nothing — they'd have renewed anyway. If usage has quietly died since the champion left, a lower price doesn't bring it back; you'd be locking in two years of a shelf-ware contract at a worse rate.
- **25% for 2 years is a deep discount, offered unprompted.** Deep, unsolicited discounts train accounts to expect them at every renewal — the skill's benchmark is 20-30% only as a *response* to a stated price objection, time-limited, not a proactive opener.
- **Propagating that rate to other departments anchors the whole institution low.** Banks share procurement/vendor-management data across departments. Once one team has a 25%-off-for-2-years number, every future negotiation in that account — including this team's next renewal — references it. You're not expanding the account, you're discounting the account, permanently.
- **Expansion before the core is confirmed stable multiplies the wrong thing.** If the original relationship is actually at risk, selling into new departments now just means more stakeholders exposed to the same unresolved problem, not more resilience.

## What to do instead, in the four months you have

1. **Check usage first.** Pull tracing volume/query activity per team since the champion left. This tells you if you have a churn problem or a paperwork problem — the two need opposite responses.
2. **Multi-thread immediately.** Don't wait for the account to come back to you. Find who absorbed the champion's role; use your other known contacts across the three teams; use the finance/procurement contact from the credit-card-to-invoice conversion — they're your most reliable line into a large org when the technical champion disappears.
3. **Ask for a renewal/business review, not send a discount.** A 30-minute call to reconfirm who owns the relationship and whether value is still being realized costs nothing and tells you what's actually going on.
4. **Only discuss price if they raise it, and trade for it.** If budget is genuinely the blocker, a discount tied to a real ask (multi-year term, expanded seats, case study rights) is fine. A discount handed over before anyone complained is not a negotiation, it's a concession.
5. **Sequence expansion after renewal is secured with a real champion in place** — not in parallel with a still-unconfirmed core relationship.

**Bottom line:** spend the next few weeks finding out who's still there and whether the product is still running, not drafting a discount email. If it turns out to be a healthy account with a staffing gap, you don't need the discount at all — you need a name.
