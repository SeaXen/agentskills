## Is 103% actually bad? No — measured against the right row, it's a pass, not a warning sign.

**The benchmark mismatch is the whole story.** Net revenue retention bands differ by motion. Stavemill is a bottom-up, self-serve, card-paying devtool — that puts you on the *Bottom-up SaaS* row, not the *Enterprise SaaS* row:

| Business model | GOOD | GREAT |
|---|---|---|
| Bottom-up SaaS | ~100% | ~120% |
| Enterprise SaaS | ~110% | ~130% |

(Source: Lenny's Newsletter, *What is good retention?* — a compression of ~20 growth practitioners' GOOD/GREAT numbers per business model, not a single headline figure.)

Your investor's "120% plus" is the *enterprise* bar, or at best the bottom-up *GREAT* ceiling — either way it's the wrong row for a product with no sales team touching card-paying small teams. Applying it here is the textbook "benchmarking against the wrong row" mistake: a bottom-up product measured against the enterprise band looks broken while performing normally.

**103% reads as:** above the ~100% floor that separates "the installed base is growing on its own" from "it's leaking," but well short of ~120% GREAT. Translation: retention is healthy, not exceptional. There's real room between "not bad" and "great" — but that's a different, calmer problem than the one the board conversation implied.

One caveat on the number itself, not the band: confirm it's measured as *a cohort's MRR today ÷ that same cohort's MRR twelve months ago*, by cohort, not a blended lifetime ratio — a lifetime ratio flatters any account base that's still growing new logos.

## A second problem hiding in the same conversation: the 1,900 seats number

Reporting "every seat ever provisioned" to the board is a known failure mode here: **count active seats, not provisioned seats — dormant seats predict contraction at renewal, they don't predict health.** If procurement or onboarding ever over-provisions (trial seats, offboarded users, unused invites), 1,900 is inflating the board's read of scale in exactly the direction that makes 103% NRR look *more* alarming than it is — a big reported footprint next to a modest retention number reads as "we're not monetizing our base," when the real footprint may be much smaller and the retention on it perfectly fine.

Fix: report active seats alongside (or instead of) provisioned seats next board cycle.

## A gap worth naming: NRR and expansion share aren't standing board metrics

Your standing set is signups, MAU, ARR, NPS. None of those move *before* revenue on the expansion question, and none of them would have caught this conversation coming — NRR only entered the room because an investor asked. For a bottom-up motion, the numbers that should be standing, not reactive, are net revenue retention and share of revenue from expansion (once there's a paid base, which you have). Add both now; this is exactly the situation they exist to defuse.

## On building an upsell motion this quarter

Not "no" — but not on this justification. Building a motion to fix a number that isn't broken means the team optimizes against a false alarm, and land-and-expand only works when the vector is named, not hoped for. Two checks before committing the quarter:

1. **Run the sales-economics test first if the motion involves a human.** A rep added to a self-serve motion needs to return ~4x fully loaded cost. For small teams paying by card, check whether qualified-account volume × realistic deal size clears that — if not, the honest fix is product surface (self-serve expansion triggers), not headcount.
2. **If you do build it, pick a vector, not a generic "upsell motion":** more developers on the same team, more teams in the company, more workload per user, or more product surface — each has a different trigger and owner. "Upsell motion" without one of these named is the thing that turns into scope creep next quarter.

If the CEO still wants a quarter-of-work item after seeing the real number, the better-justified one is: instrument active-seat tracking, add NRR/expansion-share as standing board metrics, and pick one expansion vector to wire a trigger for — not a reactive sales push against a board metric that was never actually failing.
