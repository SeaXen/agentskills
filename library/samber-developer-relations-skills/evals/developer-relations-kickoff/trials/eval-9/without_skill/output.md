**Senior Developer Advocate** — [Company] · [Location / Remote policy] · [Team: reports to Head of DevRel]

### About the role

[Company] is hiring a Senior Developer Advocate to own the path between "developer finds us" and "developer ships something real with [Product]." You'll write and speak, but the job is bigger than content: you're the standing feedback loop between developers and the people who build for them, and you're senior enough to run that loop without someone telling you what to do next.

### What you'll do

- Ship technical content — tutorials, sample apps, blog posts, docs contributions — that gets developers from curiosity to a working integration, not just pageviews.
- Represent [Product] at conferences, meetups, and podcasts: talks, workshops, booth conversations.
- Turn recurring developer pain into filed, prioritized product and engineering issues — not Slack noise that dies in a thread.
- Build and maintain relationships with power users, OSS maintainers, and community leads who move adoption.
- Own a segment of the community (Discord/Slack/forum): answer questions, and seed the ones worth asking.
- Partner with product marketing and product on launches — docs, demos, and enablement ready on day one.
- Mentor junior advocates or contractors; set the editorial and quality bar for devrel content.
- Report on program health with real numbers, not vibes.

### What we're looking for

**Must-haves**
- 4+ years in developer relations, technical writing, developer-facing engineering, or technical marketing, with at least one product launch or community owned end to end.
- Can write production-quality code in [primary stack] and explain it to someone who's never seen it.
- Has given technical talks to audiences of 50+ and 500+.
- Track record of turning developer feedback into shipped product changes — can name specifics, not generalities.
- Writes clean, scannable technical prose unassisted (writing sample required).

**Nice-to-haves**
- Existing reputation in [specific ecosystem/community relevant to Product].
- Experience mentoring or hiring other advocates.
- Comfortable building and defending a simple impact model for their own work.

### What success looks like

- **90 days** — shipped two pieces of content, met the top ten community members, shadowed support and sales to learn where the pain actually lives.
- **6 months** — owns one full launch end to end; has a working feedback pipeline into product.
- **12 months** — is the person the exec team asks when they want an honest read on developer sentiment.

### Logistics

[Location/remote policy] · [Comp band] · [Reports to] · [Team size]

### How to apply

Send a resume, a writing sample (published preferred), and a link to a talk if you have one. No cover letter needed — the writing sample is the cover letter.

## B. Interview Stages — What Each One Actually Tests

Seven stages. Cut to five if your process needs to move faster — cut stage 1 or fold stage 6 into stage 2, never cut stage 3 or 5.

| # | Stage | Format | Who runs it | What it actually tests |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Recruiter screen | 30 min, phone/video | Recruiter | Baseline communication clarity, comp/logistics fit, whether their framing of impact matches their resume. Not testing technical depth — too early, don't overload it. |
| 2 | Hiring manager conversation | 45 min | Hiring manager | Scope fit — did they *build* a program or *execute* someone else's? Motivation for devrel specifically, not marketing or eng by another name. Key probe: "Tell me about a time your work didn't move the metric you expected — what did you do." Tests intellectual honesty, not just win stories. |
| 3 | Content / teach-back work sample | 60 min, async prep + live delivery | Hiring manager + 1 peer | The single highest-signal stage — weight it heavily. Candidate prepares a 15-min "explain X to a developer who's never seen it," using your product or something they know well. Tests real teaching ability, technical depth, and how they handle live pushback. Watch for people who simplify without dumbing down. |
| 4 | Cross-functional panel | 45–60 min | One each from eng, PM, support/sales | How they operate with people who don't report to them. Whether they can turn fuzzy feedback into a filed, prioritized ask. Whether engineers would actually want them in the room. Include a role-play: "A developer just posted a snarky complaint about a broken example in our docs. Walk us through the next hour." |
| 5 | Metrics / narrative exercise | 30–45 min | Whoever currently owns the budget narrative | Directly tests the skill this hire is meant to reinforce. Give a small, realistic dataset (blog traffic, GitHub stars, support ticket volume) and ask them to build a 3-slide argument for more devrel budget next quarter. Tests whether they can tell the ROI story, handle "we can't attribute this cleanly" pushback, and avoid vanity metrics. |
| 6 | Executive / values conversation | 30 min | Exec sponsor | Judgment under ambiguity, seniority in how they talk about failure, whether you'd trust them unsupervised in front of an exec or a hostile Hacker News thread. |
| 7 | Reference calls | 2–3 calls, 20 min each | Hiring manager | Verification of specific claimed impact — ask referees to confirm the *numbers* the candidate cited, not just "were they good to work with." Ask: "What did they own end to end, and what would have happened if they hadn't been there?" |

Stage 3 and stage 5 are the two stages that predict on-the-job performance best for this role. If the panel disagrees, re-watch those two before re-discussing the others.

## C. Interview Scorecard

Every panelist scores independently, submits before the debrief, and cannot see other scores until they submit. This is what actually stops gut-feel arguing — not the rubric alone.

### Scoring dimensions (1–4 scale)

| Dimension | 1 — Below bar | 2 — Developing | 3 — Strong hire signal | 4 — Exceptional | Min. to hire |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Technical depth & credibility** | Can't answer follow-up technical questions past the surface | Handles basics, struggles under pushback | Fluent, can go deep on request, admits gaps honestly | Developers in the room would want to be corrected by this person | 3 |
| **Teaching / communication ability** | Loses the room, jargon without translation | Clear but flat, misses audience calibration | Adjusts explanation live based on questions | Makes a hard concept feel obvious | 3 |
| **Writing quality** | Sample needs heavy editing | Competent but generic | Scannable, structured, has a point of view | Reads like something you'd share unprompted | 2 |
| **Community & relationship-building track record** | Can't name specific relationships built | Names activities, not relationships | Names specific people/communities moved by their work | Has a reputation that precedes them | 2 |
| **Cross-functional operating skill** | Vague about how feedback reached product/eng | Describes a process that sounds passive | Describes specific asks they turned into shipped changes | Eng/PM in the room already want to work with them | 3 |
| **Strategic / metrics thinking** | Leads with vanity metrics, can't defend them | Has metrics, can't connect to business outcomes | Builds a defensible, honest impact argument | Anticipates the "you can't attribute this" objection unprompted | 3 |
| **Executive presence & judgment** | Uncomfortable with ambiguity or pushback | Composed but generic under pressure | Handles a hard question directly, without deflecting | Changes the room's read of the topic | 2 |
| **Ownership / seniority** | Describes tasks assigned to them | Describes a project they ran | Describes a program they built from nothing | Describes a program others now copy | 3 |
| **Values / culture fit** | Misaligned or evasive on values questions | Says the right things generically | Gives a specific example matching stated values | — | 2 |

### Overall recommendation

Each panelist writes, before scores are shared:

- **Recommendation:** Strong yes / Yes / No / Strong no
- **Strongest evidence** (one sentence, cite the stage)
- **Biggest risk** (one sentence, cite the stage)

### Calibration rules

1. All scorecards submitted within 24 hours of the last interview, before any conversation happens.
2. Debrief opens by displaying scores, not opinions.
3. Discussion is scoped only to dimensions where scores disagree by more than 1 point — don't re-litigate the ones everyone agrees on.
4. A "Strong yes" from stage 3 or 5 outweighs a "Yes" from stages 1, 2, 6 — say this out loud before the debrief starts, not during it, so it doesn't look like a thumb on the scale after the fact.

# Part 2 — Quarterly DevRel Budget Narrative Deck

You didn't lose 20% because the program underperformed. You lost it because the deck was a metrics dump and the exec team had to build the story themselves — and they built a worse one than the real one. Fix the narrative, not the program.

## The core reframe

Stop trying to prove a single, clean attribution number exists. It doesn't — not for devrel, not for any long-cycle, multi-touch function. Executives who ask "prove this drove revenue" are usually really asking "convince me this isn't the easiest thing to cut." Answer that question, not the literal one.

Two moves win this:
1. **Show first-touch and last-touch side by side**, plus a self-reported signal ("how did you hear about us" from your best recent signups/deals). The gap between them *is* the story — devrel shows up early, sales closes late, and that gap is exactly what finance is trained to be suspicious of when it isn't explained.
2. **Say the limitation out loud before they ask.** "We can get you a defensible, consistent read and a clear trend — a single objective attribution number doesn't exist for any earned-media function, here's why, and here's what we use to make the call anyway." Naming it first reads as rigor. Having it pointed out reads as evasion.

## Deck structure — 11 slides + appendix

Design for presenting, not reading. One idea per slide. Metrics live in the appendix; the main deck carries the argument.

**1. The ask, stated up front**
One sentence: "We're asking to keep/grow the devrel budget at $X because it's the cheapest lever we have for [specific business outcome]." Don't bury this on slide 9. Executives decide in the first two minutes; the rest of the deck is evidence for a decision they've already started making.

**2. What changed in the market since last quarter**
Why devrel matters *now*, not generically. New competitor, new platform shift, hiring market, a category trend. This is the "shift happening" beat — it earns you the right to ask, before you've shown a single internal metric.

**3. What we shipped (30 seconds, not a list)**
One line, not fifteen bullets: "We ran N pieces of content, spoke at N events, closed N feedback loops into shipped product changes." This slide exists so nobody asks "wait, what did you actually do" later — it's not where you make your case.

**4. The outcomes, not the activities**
Reframe every activity into a business outcome:
- Content → developers who reached first successful integration
- Talks/community → pipeline touched, hiring pipeline strengthened, or support ticket deflection
- Feedback loop → specific product changes shipped that came from devrel, named individually

This is the slide most devrel decks get wrong — they report the activity metric (talks given, blog views) instead of translating it. Executives don't buy inputs, they buy outcomes.

**5. The honest attribution slide**
First-touch vs. last-touch, side by side, for your top 3–5 outcomes this quarter. One line underneath: "Devrel over-indexes on first-touch because we're built to create demand, not close it — that's the job working as designed." This is the slide that handles the "we can't attribute this cleanly" objection before anyone raises it.

**6. One story, told well**
Pick one developer or one account. Two to three sentences: where they started, what devrel touched, where they ended up (shipped a product, became a champion, closed a deal, filed the bug that became a roadmap item). One real story lands harder than ten aggregate metrics — this is the slide people remember walking out of the room.

**7. Leading indicators**
Signals that predict next quarter's outcomes even though they aren't revenue yet: community growth rate, returning-contributor rate, feedback-to-shipped conversion rate, inbound speaking/partnership requests. Frame explicitly: "These are the numbers that predicted this quarter's wins, three months ago."

**8. Cost of the counterfactual**
What specifically stops if budget is cut further — name the program, not an abstraction. "We stop shipping the quickstart refresh that correlates with our lowest support-ticket cohort" beats "less content." Concrete losses are harder to wave away than vague ones.

**9. Peer/competitive benchmark**
What comparable companies invest in devrel relative to [engineering headcount / ARR / marketing spend]. One chart. This reframes the number from "cost we're deciding on in isolation" to "where we sit against the field."

**10. The specific ask**
Restate the number from slide 1, now with evidence behind it. Tie it to two or three named outcomes you're committing to next quarter — not "more of the same," specific commitments you'll be judged against in 90 days.

**11. Next steps**
One slide: what you need from them (a decision, a signature, an intro) and by when.

**Appendix** — every metric, every chart, every raw number. This is where the metrics dump goes. Reference it live only if asked; never present it.

## Handling the hard objections

| Objection | Why they say it | Response | Proof point |
|---|---|---|---|
| "We can't attribute this cleanly to revenue." | They've been burned by vague marketing claims before | "You're right, and neither can any earned-media function — here's the honest range and how we triangulate it" (point to slide 5) | First-touch/last-touch split + self-reported signal from recent closed deals |
| "Why not let marketing/sales own this?" | They see overlap, not differentiation | "Marketing buys attention, sales closes it — devrel is the only function developers trust because it isn't selling to them. That trust is the asset, and it evaporates the moment it looks like marketing." | Name one deal or integration that happened *because* it wasn't a sales conversation |
| "Cut the travel/events budget, keep the content." | Travel is the most visible, easiest-to-cut line item | Show which specific relationships or deals trace back to in-person contact — events are where trust compounds fastest, not where it's cheapest to fake | The one-story slide, if it involves an event |
| "What's the ROI, in one number?" | Finance-trained instinct to want a single figure | Give the range, not a fake point estimate: "Directionally, every dollar here touches N in pipeline early; we don't claim precision beyond that, and neither does any function that isn't direct-response." | The leading-indicators slide |
| "This all sounds like nice-to-have, not need-to-have." | Budget conversations default to cutting anything that isn't obviously critical-path | Slide 8, made specific — name what breaks, not what shrinks | The counterfactual slide |

## Delivery — for a presenter who knows this is the weak spot

- **Open with the story slide's headline, not the agenda.** "Let me tell you about one developer who almost churned, and didn't" beats "Today I'll walk through Q3 devrel results."
- **One number per slide, said out loud, left on screen in silence for a beat.** Don't read the slide — the slide is the visual aid, your voice carries the argument.
- **Rehearse the objection table as a Q&A with someone playing skeptic**, out loud, at least twice before presenting. The goal isn't memorized lines — it's not being surprised by the question that cost you 20% last time.
- **Every slide passes the "so what" test before it goes in.** If you can't say the one-sentence consequence of a slide out loud, cut it or move it to the appendix.
- **End on the ask, restated, not on a thank-you slide.** The last thing they hear should be the decision you want them to make.

## Making this repeatable every quarter

Keep a running doc, updated weekly, not built from scratch the week before the meeting:
- A running log of "outcomes, not activities" as they happen — write the sentence the week it happens, not three months later from memory.
- One candidate "story" flagged per month; pick the strongest of the three at deck time.
- The attribution slide's numbers pulled from the same source of truth every quarter (pick one system — CRM or backend — and don't let it drift), so trend lines are comparable and nobody can argue the methodology changed to flatter the result.
- The peer-benchmark slide refreshed twice a year, not every quarter — it doesn't move fast enough to need more.

The deck fails when it's assembled under deadline pressure from a metrics spreadsheet. It works when the narrative has been accumulating in the background for thirteen weeks and the deck is just the moment you finally say it out loud.
