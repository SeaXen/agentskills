# Vantablack Systems — Primary Segment Decision, Reviewed

## Bottom line

The scorecard math is sound. The recommendation it produced is not the one the mandate calls for. Independent hobbyists win the sum because the sum rewards the wrong thing for this year's mandate. Read past the total before committing budget to hobbyists as the primary segment.

## What the scorecard shows

| Segment | Total (/70) | Known drivers |
|---|---|---|
| Independent hardware hobbyists | 61 | Large population, easy to reach, near-zero cost to serve |
| Automotive tier-one suppliers | 44 | Effort scored 3 — hard to reach, hard to close |

Seven criteria, ten points each, summed. Clean method. The problem sits one level up, in what the method optimizes for.

## Why the top score misses the mandate

Vantablack was funded this year specifically to help sales close bigger deals. Hobbyists don't produce bigger deals — they produce many small, cheap-to-acquire ones, which is a different growth motion entirely.

Three things in the scoring let that mismatch through:

**Value carries only one-seventh of the total.** Size, reach, fit, effort, competition, and compounding can outvote value six to one. A segment can lose badly on deal size and still win the ranking on volume and convenience alone.

**Effort measures ease, not strategic payoff.** Automotive tier-one scored a 3 on effort precisely because enterprise hardware-in-the-loop sales are slow and relationship-heavy — the same property that makes them big deals. Scoring "hard to close" as a penalty punishes the segment for the exact quality the funding exists to pursue. Hobbyists score well on effort for the opposite reason: nothing is at stake for either side.

**Size and reach favor volume, not revenue.** A market being large and cheap to reach says nothing about how much any one account is worth. Those two criteria alone can carry a segment to the top of a 7-factor sum even if fit and value are mediocre, because nothing in an unweighted sum stops it.

Put together, the scorecard is well-built to answer "which segment is easiest to grow," and this year's mandate is asking a different question: "which segment grows deal size." Those aren't the same ranking, and the current method can't distinguish them.

## Recommendation

Don't send hobbyists to sales as the primary segment when the funding brief says bigger deals. Two ways to fix the method, either workable:

- **Reweight before ranking.** Give value and compounding more weight than size and reach for this year's call — they're the two criteria that actually track "bigger deals." Re-sum automotive tier-one and any other enterprise segment on the sheet under the new weights before finalizing.
- **Gate on value first, rank second.** Set a minimum value score to even qualify as "primary" (e.g., 7+), then rank only the segments that clear it. Hobbyists likely fail the gate outright; whatever clears it becomes the real shortlist.

Either fix probably moves automotive tier-one, or another underscored enterprise segment, back into contention — the low effort score that sank it is a synonym for the sales motion the funding is meant to support, not a strike against the segment.

**A split serves both goals better than picking one segment.** Keep hobbyists — they're cheap, they build product credibility and inbound signal, and near-zero cost to serve means they cost sales nothing. Just don't call them primary. Run them as a secondary, low-touch track (self-serve, community, content) while automotive tier-one (or the best value/fit/compounding scorer among the enterprise segments) carries the primary designation tied to the actual funding mandate.
