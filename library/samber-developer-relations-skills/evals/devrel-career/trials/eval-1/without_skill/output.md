Straight talk first: no coding, 6 hrs/week, 4 months, zero savings buffer to survive a gap — that's a tight box. It rules out the DevRel jobs that need you to ship sample apps in Python/JS/Go. But it doesn't rule out DevRel. It just tells you which corner of DevRel to aim at.

## The reframe that makes this possible

**SQL is not "not coding" in this context — it's your specialization.** Don't try to become a generalist developer advocate competing against people who write in five languages. Target companies where SQL *is* the product language: data warehouses, BI tools, dbt/analytics-engineering tools, observability/logging platforms, database vendors, ETL/pipeline tools. At those companies, a DevRel who can write real SQL demos and shell-script a setup is fully qualified — not a compromise hire.

**Four years of tier-2 support is not a resume gap to explain away — it's the actual job.** You already do the hardest part of DevRel: translating confused users into clear technical answers, spotting the same friction point 50 times before anyone else notices, writing things a stressed person can follow under pressure. Support-to-DevRel is one of the most common and most respected paths into the field, precisely because most career-track DevRel candidates have never sat in a support queue and don't know what actually confuses users.

So the plan is: **become visibly good at the writing/community half of DevRel in public, while pointing your target list at companies where SQL-only counts as technical enough.**

## What "DevRel" means for you specifically

Don't chase the job title "Developer Advocate" everywhere — chase these, which all count as DevRel and don't require a general-purpose language:
- Developer Community Manager
- Technical Content / Developer Education
- Customer Education / Technical Enablement
- Support Engineer roles explicitly described as a DevRel pipeline (common at API/data tool companies)
- Developer Advocate at data/SQL/analytics tool companies specifically

Roles that will reject you fast, so don't burn hours applying: anything that says "write example integrations in [language]" or "contribute to our SDKs" as a core duty.

## The 4-month plan (~100 hours total — spend them ruthlessly)

**Weeks 1–3 — Build the only portfolio that matters to you: proof you can write.**
Mine your own support history. Pick 4–5 tickets you've resolved that come up repeatedly, and rewrite each as a public troubleshooting post or FAQ — "Why does X error happen and how to fix it," written the way you'd explain it to a confused customer, not the way an engineer would. Publish on a free Substack, dev.to, or LinkedIn articles — the platform doesn't matter, the artifact does. This is your entire portfolio; it needs zero code.

**Weeks 2–4 (overlap) — Get in front of the people who hire for this.**
Find 8–10 people with "Developer Advocate," "Community Manager," or "Developer Relations" in their title at SQL/data-tool companies. Send a short, specific message: you're tier-2 support, four years, moving toward DevRel, would value 15 minutes on how they got in. Book what you can. This does two things: real intel on how thin the "no coding" lane actually is at each company, and it's how you skip the resume black hole later.

**Weeks 5–10 — Publish on a cadence + build the target list.**
One piece every 1–2 weeks (support-ticket-to-guide format, or "here's how I'd onboard a confused user to X"). Build a list of 25–30 target companies (data/BI/warehouse/observability/dev-tool vendors) and check which have open reqs matching the role list above. In parallel, ask your informational-interview contacts for a warm intro wherever you find a match — warm beats cold every time in DevRel hiring, since these teams are small and reputation-driven.

**Weeks 11–14 — Apply and interview.**
Apply to every match, warm intro first, cold second. In interviews, lead with the support angle explicitly: "I've spent four years being the person users actually talk to when things break — that's the raw material DevRel is built from." Point to your published pieces as evidence, not as a portfolio you built for the interview.

**Weeks 15–16 — Close, or fall back on purpose.**
If nothing's closed by week 16, don't panic-apply broader — take the closest adjacent title instead (Technical Support Engineer or Community Support at a company that *has* a DevRel team) and plan the internal hop from inside, which is a much shorter path than external DevRel from cold.

## One more angle, cheap and worth trying in parallel

Since Fernwick has no DevRel and no plans for one, floating a zero-budget pilot costs you almost nothing and could get you the title without a job switch at all: propose running a customer-facing troubleshooting webinar or a public knowledge-base series, framed as reducing ticket volume (a support metric your manager already cares about). If it lands, you may not need the external search at all. If it doesn't land in a few weeks, you've lost little and you keep executing the plan above.

## What to skip entirely

Don't spend any of your 6 hours/week on a coding bootcamp, a language course, or "learning to code just enough." At this budget it won't get you credible before month 4, and it's not what's blocking you — companies that need that will never be the right target anyway. Every hour goes to writing publicly, talking to people already in the role, and applying where SQL is enough.
