Six years of DevRel experience doesn't evaporate — but a good chunk of what made you effective was quietly subsidized by your employer's brand, and you're about to lose that subsidy. That's the real source of the fear, and it's worth being precise about what's actually at risk versus what isn't.

## What carries over directly

**The craft of explaining technical things to skeptical technical people.** Writing a tutorial that respects the reader's time, giving a talk that doesn't waste the first five minutes on company boilerplate, knowing when a code sample needs more context versus less — this is a transferable skill built from reps, not from your badge. It's the single biggest asset you're bringing.

**Pattern recognition on developer pain.** You've watched thousands of developers hit friction — in docs, in error messages, in onboarding, in support threads. You know what "I'm stuck and about to give up" looks like before it's said out loud. That instinct is exactly what a self-serve product needs, because at a 40-person startup nobody else in the building has watched that pattern at your scale.

**Community instincts.** Knowing how to show up in a Discord, a subreddit, a Hacker News thread, a conference hallway without sounding like marketing — that's earned, not granted by the company name. It transfers.

**Content production speed and range.** Blog posts, talks, docs, video, sample apps, live coding — the mechanical ability to produce good technical content quickly is portable infrastructure. A startup will use all of it.

**Your personal network and reputation** — to the extent it's actually *yours* and not your employer's. If developers followed you, cite your talks, remember your name specifically, that's portable. If they only ever engaged with "[Big Cloud Vendor]'s DevRel team," that part doesn't travel with you, and it's worth being honest with yourself about the ratio.

## What doesn't carry over — the part worth sitting with

At a large cloud vendor, you had **brand gravity**: people showed up to your talk because of the logo, not just the topic. Conference organizers returned your emails because of who you represented. Journalists took the briefing because it was a name they had to cover. None of that exists at a 40-person startup nobody's heard of. You will be starting your *credibility*, not your *skills*, from closer to zero. That's the actual adjustment, and it's real — but it's a known, survivable one, not evidence the underlying skill set is worthless.

You also likely had **structural slack**: a docs team, a support team, product managers who owned onboarding, budget for swag and travel and events regardless of whether any single event "worked." At Tavolino, you're probably going to *be* several of those functions at once, and every dollar you spend needs a story about why it mattered.

Most importantly: DevRel at a large vendor is usually optimized for **awareness and enterprise sales-assist** — get developers to know the product exists, get them to champion it internally so sales can land the deal. That's a long, indirect, largely unmeasured loop. It is not the loop you're joining.

## What you actually have to relearn

**The metric you're accountable to changes completely.** At a self-serve company, the developer *is* the buyer — there's no salesperson translating your awareness-building into a closed deal. Your work has to connect, plausibly and often measurably, to signup → activation → paid conversion → retention → expansion. Learn the vocabulary and the numbers: activation rate, time-to-first-value, PQLs (product-qualified leads), trial-to-paid conversion, net revenue retention. You don't need to own a dashboard, but you need to be able to have a real conversation with the person who does, and increasingly reason in those terms yourself.

**Your surface area shifts from "content about the product" to "the product experience itself."** Docs, error messages, empty states, the first-15-minutes-after-signup flow, the CLI's help text — these are marketing surface in a self-serve company in a way they never quite were when sales handled the close. Docs-as-marketing and onboarding become core to your job, not adjacent to it. Expect to be in product/design conversations, not just publishing content about what product already shipped.

**Who you sit next to changes.** You were probably marketing-org-adjacent-to-sales. At Tavolino you'll likely be closer to product and growth than to sales — sales barely exists below a certain account size. Learn to partner with a growth or product person the way you used to partner with an AE or SE.

**You need enough data literacy to self-serve your own answers.** No dedicated analyst is pulling your funnel numbers for you. Basic comfort in your analytics tool (and ideally enough SQL to poke at usage data yourself) turns from "nice to have" into something close to load-bearing.

**Everything gets scrappier and faster.** No six-week approval chain, no brand review, no big conference budget. The upside: you'll ship in a day what used to take a quarter. The adjustment: the safety net of "well, corporate marketing approved it" is gone — you own the call and the consequences.

**You'll be judged on outcomes you can point to, not activity.** "I gave 12 talks this quarter" was a legitimate report at a big company. At a 40-person startup, the question will be closer to "did that talk turn into signups, and did those signups activate." Get comfortable instrumenting your own work and reporting on it in those terms before someone asks you to.

## The honest reframe

You're not starting over — you're moving from a role where the *company* did the hard part of earning trust and you did the hard part of teaching, to a role where you have to do both, but with a much tighter, more visible line from your work to revenue. That's more pressure, but it's also more leverage: a good post-signup email sequence, a docs fix, or a well-placed tutorial can move a self-serve company's numbers in a way that's actually attributable to you, which almost never happened at a company that size. The skill that made you good is intact. The scoreboard changed.

**First 90 days, concretely:** go through the entire self-serve funnel yourself — sign up with a personal card, try to get to value, note every point of friction — before you produce a single piece of content. Then find out who owns activation and retention numbers and get embedded in that conversation early, rather than defaulting to the awareness-and-content playbook you know cold. That's the instinct most likely to get you in trouble, precisely because it used to work.
