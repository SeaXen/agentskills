## Sanity check: your plan solves the wrong problem in two places, and one piece of it will actively hurt you.

### The biggest blind spot: you're building on rented land while sitting on owned land

You said it yourself, almost as an aside: "most of my audience lives in our company Slack community, which I run." That's not a footnote — that's the fact that should reorganize the whole plan.

A Slack community you run is a high-trust, high-context, already-engaged audience. X and LinkedIn followers are cold, low-trust, and you're competing with everyone else's daily post in an algorithmic feed you don't control. Chasing follower count on rented platforms while your owned audience gets whatever attention is left over is backwards. The people who already trust you enough to hang out in your Slack are the ones most likely to amplify you elsewhere, answer your CFP reviewers' "who is this person" search, and vouch for you when someone asks "who should we invite to speak." Treat that community as the base, not a side project:

- Identify your most engaged members and cultivate them deliberately (see how champion/advocate programs work — recognize specific contributions publicly, give early access to what you're building or thinking about, make them your first readers/reviewers). Right now you're "running" the community; you're not yet mining it for signal or turning its best members into people who talk about you elsewhere.
- Every piece of content you'd post cold on X/LinkedIn, test in Slack first. Real-time reaction from people who already know your work is a better editor than a follower count.

### Daily posting on two platforms is the wrong lever

X and LinkedIn reward depth and engagement, not frequency past a point. Sustainable, high-quality X cadence is 1-3 posts/day; 5+ risks overexposure and the algorithm doesn't reward raw volume — it rewards replies in the first hour, time spent, bookmarks. LinkedIn's engagement math is similar: a strong technical post with real structure beats a daily filler post every time, and LinkedIn actively punishes plain announcements and link-drops.

Daily-on-two-platforms is a commitment to produce ~700+ posts/year of genuinely useful material. That's not a content strategy, it's a treadmill, and the near-certain failure mode is quality collapse into generic "thread guy" content — which the field already has too much of and which actively works against "top tier" positioning. Pick a cadence you can sustain at high quality (1-3 threads/week on X, 2-3 real posts/week on LinkedIn) and put the reclaimed time into the Slack community and into one or two things you're actually known for.

### Follower count is a vanity metric for this goal specifically

Nobody who is "top tier" in developer advocacy got there by follower count. They got there by being the person people call for a specific thing — a technology, a niche, a way of explaining something hard. Your plan has no answer yet to "known for what, specifically?" That's the actual gap. Follower count is a lagging indicator of that, not a target you can aim at directly — optimizing for it directly is how you end up with growth-hacking behaviors (engagement bait, hot takes for the sake of them) that erode the credibility you're actually trying to build.

Before touching posting cadence, answer: what's the one thing, in a year, you want to be the go-to person for? Then the content plan derives from that, instead of "post every day about whatever."

### The Kelsey Hightower thing is a misdiagnosis — don't act on it literally

"Effortless, off-the-cuff" delivery is the *output* of enormous preparation, not the *absence* of it. Hightower has given versions of his core material hundreds of times; what looks spontaneous is deep command of the content, not a lack of rehearsal. What you're actually seeing is: rehearsed *substance*, unrehearsed *script*. That's a different skill than "don't over-prepare," and it's the opposite of what "stop over-rehearsing" sounds like as a plan.

If you drop rehearsal to chase that look, you'll likely get worse talks that feel under-prepared, not better ones that feel natural — because you'll have skipped the actual work that produces the effect. What you probably want instead: stop memorizing scripts word-for-word, and instead over-invest in knowing the material and the story beats cold enough that you can improvise the phrasing live. That's more rehearsal time on fewer, more foundational things, not less rehearsal overall.

### One thing worth naming, not necessarily solving: whose audience is it?

You run a company Slack community at Melbrook. If you lean on it as your primary channel for building a *personal* brand, that's worth being deliberate about, since it's not automatically a problem, but it is a real tension: that audience is tied to your role there, not portable if you ever leave, and there may be an expectations conversation worth having with whoever you report to about how much "you as an individual" content flows through a channel you moderate in your job capacity. Not a reason to stop — just don't build your whole plan on an asset whose ownership you haven't actually confirmed.

### Revised version of your plan

1. **Pick the specific thing you want to be known for** before any content plan. "Personal brand" isn't a strategy; "the person who explains X clearly" is.
2. **Reprioritize the Slack community as the core asset.** Identify and actively cultivate your most engaged members there before optimizing for strangers on X/LinkedIn.
3. **Cut "daily on both platforms" to a sustainable, high-quality cadence** (few threads/week on X, few real posts/week on LinkedIn), and measure engagement/saves, not follower count.
4. **Drop "stop rehearsing."** Replace it with "stop memorizing scripts" — keep preparing the substance hard, loosen only the delivery.
5. **Set milestones that aren't follower count**: CFP acceptances, unsolicited inbound (people asking you to speak/write/consult), depth of the Slack community's engagement, a body of content specific enough that people cite you by name for one topic.

"Top tier in a couple of years" is aggressive on any version of this plan — most people who get there took longer and got there through depth and reputation, not posting frequency. That's not a reason not to try; it's a reason to optimize for the things that actually compound (expertise, trust, a real community) rather than the ones that are just easy to measure (followers, post count).
