## Certifications: don't pay for one

There's no DevRel certification that functions as a real credential — no equivalent of a CPA or PMP. DevRel isn't standardized enough for that, and hiring managers know it. The courses you're seeing ("DevRel Certification," usually $200–$800 on Udemy/Coursera-style platforms) are content marketing for whoever's selling the course, not something that moves a hiring decision. Nobody screening DevRel candidates filters on "has certificate."

What actually gets you hired: a visible body of work — talks (even small ones), writing, open-source or community contributions, and evidence you can explain technical things clearly to an audience. Hiring managers look at your GitHub, blog, and talk recordings, not a PDF certificate.

If you want structured learning, spend the money on a book instead of a cert — *Developer Relations* by Mary Thengvall is the standard one. Free and better than paid: join the DevRel Collective (Slack community) and read/lurk. That gets you the same knowledge a paid course would, plus actual industry contacts, for free.

## Three big CFPs next month: not the right first move

Two problems.

**Timing mismatch.** Flagship conferences (the ones you're picturing) typically close CFPs 4–9 months before the event and get reviewed by committees who lean toward known speakers or a video reel. If you're aiming to "submit next month," check now whether the CFPs you have in mind are even open — many well-known ones already closed for their next cycle.

**You'd be competing at the wrong tier with zero portfolio.** Big-conference CFP forms usually ask for a link to you speaking. You don't have one yet. First-timers with no reel get filtered out early, regardless of talk quality on paper. Burning your first three reps on the hardest-to-get slots means months of waiting for three near-certain rejections and nothing to show afterward — not even a talk you can reuse, since content gets tailored to committee themes.

**What to do instead, in order:**

1. **Give the talk somewhere small first.** Local meetups, your company's internal tech/QA guild, a regional testing conference, a lunch-and-learn. Low competition, fast yes/no, and you walk away with a recording — which is the actual unlock for every CFP after this one.
2. **Write in parallel.** A couple of technical blog posts on testing/QA topics do two things at once: they're portfolio evidence, and they're raw material you can turn into talk proposals later.
3. **Pick a narrow angle: QA, not generic "DevRel."** This is your actual advantage. Most people trying to break into DevRel are generalists with no real domain expertise — you already have deep, credible expertise in testing and quality engineering. "QA analyst explains X" is a far less crowded, more credible wedge than "aspiring DevRel person talks about developer experience." Lead with what you already know.
4. **Ask Pendrel first.** Check if there's any appetite for you to represent the company at even one small event or write one post under the company banner. That's the lowest-friction rep you can get, and it's a stronger CV line than a rejected big-conference submission.
5. **Then go wide on mid-tier CFPs, not three big ones.** Once you have one recording and one or two posts, submit to 10–15 regional/mid-tier conferences, not three flagship ones. Acceptance rates on first-timer submissions to the big names are often under 10%; treat those as a goal for next year, not month one.

Talks do matter for DevRel — but it's talk *history*, writing, and demonstrated domain expertise together that counts, not "submitted to CFPs." Skipping straight to the biggest stages without a reel is the most common way people stall out on this exact path.
