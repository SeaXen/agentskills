## Why the number fails

1. **The pipeline already existed.** AEs picked accounts with open opportunities, so the dinner created none of that $2.4M. At best it touched deals already in flight.
2. **Selection bias.** Accounts with live deals and engaged champions are the ones most likely to advance, dinner or not.
3. **No comparison group.** "7 of 11 moved a stage" (64%) means nothing without the rate for similar open deals that weren't at the dinner. Ask what share of your open deals move a stage in a typical quarter. It may be about the same.
4. **No target set in advance.** Any result looks like a win when you define success afterward. Finance and the CEO will notice.
5. **Tiny sample.** With n=11, one or two deals swing the result.
6. **The word "influenced" has no written rule.** Nothing says what counts, over what window, or how much credit the dinner gets.

## How to measure the next six

**Before dinner 1, write down and freeze:**

1. **Purpose per dinner.** Either speed up open deals (cohort A) or open new accounts (cohort B). Never mix them in one number.
2. **Guest rule.** AEs nominate about twice the seats. A coin flip assigns half to invite and half to a hold-out that gets normal AE treatment. This is the only real fix for selection bias.
3. **Targets and kill rule.** Set them from your baseline and cost. See "Set targets" below.
4. **Attribution rule**, written once and not changed after results:
   - **Sourced:** no open opp at invite date, and an opp is created within 90 days with an attendee as a contact.
   - **Influenced:** counts only the incremental lift versus the hold-out, never the full deal value.
   - **Touched:** all open pipeline at the table. Report it separately and never call it influence.

**CRM setup (1 hour):**

- Create one campaign per dinner with member statuses: invited, accepted, attended, declined, hold-out.
- Snapshot each opp's stage and amount on the invite date. Without the snapshot you can't prove what changed.
- Add a self-reported "how did you hear about us" field.

**Metrics, in order of weight:**

- **Cohort A (open deals):** days to next stage and stage-advance rate versus hold-out over 90 days. Confirm with win rate and ACV at 180–270 days, since Kafka deals run long.
- **Cohort B (no open opp):** new opportunities created in 90 days versus hold-out.
- **Supporting:** show rate (invited to attended), share of seats filled by ICP accounts, follow-up meetings booked within 14 days, and number of buying-committee roles in the room.
- **Decisive:** cost per qualified meeting and cost per new or advanced opp, compared with your outbound and other event channels.

**Set targets.** Before dinner 1, take break-even: dinner cost ÷ (average ACV × win rate). That gives the closed-won deals each dinner must produce. Set the stage-advance and new-opp targets from that. I don't have your ACV or win rate, so I won't invent numbers.

**Know the limits.** Six dinners with about 10 guests per arm give roughly 60–70 accounts each. That detects only big effects, roughly 20% versus 40% stage-advance. Smaller lifts will look like noise. Say this up front so a flat result isn't read as a failure of the format.

**Gate:**

1. Read dinner 1–2 results at 90 days.
2. Go if cohort A beats hold-out on velocity or cohort B beats it on new opps, and cost per qualified meeting is at or below your next-best channel.
3. If not, change the guest mix or format, or stop.
4. Keep the format fixed across dinners (8–14 seats, no pitch, curated mix), or log every change.
