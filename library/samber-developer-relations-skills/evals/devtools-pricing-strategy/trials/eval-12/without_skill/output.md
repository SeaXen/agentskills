## Holes in the Brightwell Credits model

**1. The undefined, drifting conversion rate is the whole thing's weak point.** "Roughly one credit per review action" plus "we'd rather not publish a table" means the actual unit price of the product's core metric is not fixed and not disclosed. That's not a value-based usage metric — it's a floating exchange rate Brightwell controls unilaterally. Any customer who notices their $/PR creeping up over time has no way to tell "model costs changed" from "margin got quietly raised." Procurement and legal at any mid-size-or-larger buyer will ask "what governs this rate and how/when does it change" — and there's no contractual answer. This alone will stall enterprise deals regardless of the other two lines.

**2. "All three lines grow independently" is a finance-side benefit dressed up as a design principle.** It isn't true from the buyer's side, and it isn't independent. Add a repo → more code gets reviewed → more credits burn. Add a seat → more PRs get opened → more credits burn. So credits aren't an independent third axis, they're a multiplier riding on top of the other two. A customer scaling normally (more repos, more people) sees their bill compound on three fronts at once for what feels like one growth event. "Independently" is true for revenue recognition, not for the invoice a customer has to explain internally.

**3. No usage ceiling, no alerting, no committed-use option mentioned.** Metered pricing with no cap is the AWS-bill-shock pattern. A noisy repo (bot PRs, dependabot, monorepo churn) can spike credit burn with nothing in the model to warn or throttle it. Enterprise buyers budget OpEx, not exposure — "we don't know what we'll owe" is a common reason security/eng-leadership pricing reviews reject a tool outright, independent of whether the per-unit price is competitive.

**4. "Review action" is undefined — same failure mode as the conversion rate, one layer up.** If a large PR generates 15 inline comments, is that 1 credit or 15? If unspecified, Brightwell can redefine "action" granularity later to extract more revenue without ever touching the published $0.10 figure — identical effect to a silent price increase, just laundered through unit definition instead of unit price.

**5. Per-repo fee taxes architecture, not value.** $0.60/repo is trivial per unit, but a polyrepo/microservices org with 500 repos pays $300/month just for coverage to exist, before a single review fires — while a monorepo shop doing 10x the code volume pays for one repo. That's charging on repo count, which tracks org topology, not on code volume or value delivered. It also invites gaming: unpin low-traffic repos, re-add before a review sprint.

**6. Per-seat fee doesn't track this product's value driver.** Seat-based pricing fits collaboration tools where more humans logging in means more value extracted (Slack, Notion). Here the thing that actually drives cost and value is PR/commit volume, not headcount. A 5-person team merging 200 PRs/day gets far more value than a 50-person team merging 5/day, yet the seat line charges the second team 10x more. Bolting a headcount metric onto a genuinely usage-aligned one (credits) doesn't add signal, it adds noise and billing surface area.

**7. Three axes = three things a champion has to defend at renewal.** Every extra independent line item is one more thing a CFO's assistant flags, one more mismatch between last month's invoice and this month's, one more reason a deal bounces back from procurement for "please clarify these three charges." Simpler competitors quoting one flat per-seat or per-PR number will look more trustworthy in a bake-off purely on legibility, even if Brightwell is cheaper in aggregate.

**8. Cost-plus tell.** Pegging the credit rate to "how model costs change" is explicitly cost-based pricing, not value-based — and it means Brightwell's own margin floats with upstream model vendor pricing. The plan doesn't say what happens if a model provider raises prices: does the conversion rate move up immediately (customer-hostile, and now customers really are watching a live cost-plus meter), or does Brightwell eat the margin hit (then the "grows independently" framing was never true to begin with)? That decision hasn't been made, which means the pricing structure is being shipped ahead of its own risk policy.

**9. Undefined behavior at zero credits.** If this product gates CI/merge checks, what happens mid-sprint when credits run out — reviews silently stop (fails open, quietly loses the safety value customers are paying for), or the merge check blocks (fails closed, an availability incident triggered by a billing event)? Not stated, and it's a product question as much as a pricing one.

**10. Three small numbers is optimized for low sticker shock at signup, not comprehensibility at scale.** $0.10 / $12 / $0.60 all read as cheap in isolation — that's presumably deliberate, same instinct as usage-based clouds. But the two fixed lines are the ones a buyer can actually model, and they're the small part of the bill; the one line that dominates spend at any real scale (credits) is exactly the one whose rate isn't published. That's the "low headline price hides the real cost driver" pattern in reverse — cheap, legible components sitting on top of an expensive, illegible one.

**Bottom line:** the credit line needs a published, contractually fixed rate (or a disclosed formula with bounded drift) before this can survive procurement. The seat and repo fees should be checked against whether they track anything real about value delivered — right now they read as two flat taxes layered under a usage meter, there mainly because finance likes seeing three lines grow.
