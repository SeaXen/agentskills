## Recommendation: No — not as sole, unconditional chair. Counter-offer a scoped, time-boxed role instead.

**The core problem:** you're one scoring vendor being asked to chair a standard written by six platform vendors. That ratio is the whole story. Historian/SCADA vendors are the platform layer; anomaly scoring is a complement sitting on top of them. Platforms have a standing, well-documented incentive to commoditize their complements — the more interchangeable the scoring layer becomes, the more value accrues to the platform and the less any single scoring vendor can capture. A shared "score exchange" interface (request shape, normalization, explanation payload) is exactly the artifact that makes scoring engines hot-swappable. You're being asked to spend 18 months of your only senior engineer designing the mechanism that makes you replaceable, and the "chair" title is the sweetener that makes that palatable.

**Why the chair title doesn't offset this.** Chairing a six-member consortium where you hold one of seven votes gets you agenda-setting and drafting-pen leverage — real, but bounded. Substantive disagreements resolve by consensus or vote among all seven, where you're outnumbered 6:1. You get the coordination overhead and public accountability of the chair role without control over the outcome. "Category leader" optics are worth something in industrial sales, but they don't compensate for shipping a spec that erodes your product's moat.

**Where the actual danger is concentrated — separate the three deliverables, they are not equally risky:**

- **Request shape** — plumbing. Low risk. Standardizing this is normal API interoperability and costs you little.
- **Explanation payload** — mostly plumbing, with one caveat: if this doubles as a vehicle for the platforms to impose explainability/liability obligations ("every scoring engine must justify itself this way"), that's a compliance cost being externalized onto you. Worth participating in, not worth ceding control of.
- **Score normalization** — this is the one that matters. If your differentiation is model quality (a better underlying signal), agreeing on what a score of 80 *means* across vendors is closer to a shared unit of measurement than a giveaway — it can even grow the category (see FICO, which became dominant partly *because* it became the standard). But that only holds if the spec fixes the external contract (range, monotonicity, confidence semantics) and stays silent on *how* you compute it. If the standard starts prescribing normalization method, not just normalization contract, you're handing over the IP itself.

**What I'd counter-propose to the consortium, before committing any engineer:**

1. **Split the workstreams.** Volunteer to chair or co-chair request shape and explanation payload. Participate as a regular member — not chair — on score normalization, and push hard for the charter to scope it as "interface contract only, methodology out of scope." Get that sentence into the charter document before you commit resources, not after.
2. **Co-chair, not sole chair.** Halves your coordination burden, dilutes the "one vendor owns this" optics that could actually work against you if the standard is later seen as self-serving, and still gets you the credibility line for sales collateral.
3. **Time-box it.** Commit 4–6 months against a defined charter and draft-zero deliverable, with an explicit go/no-go checkpoint — not 18 months up front. Standards efforts slip and scope-creep by default; your maximum leverage to set these terms is right now, at the invitation, not six months in.
4. **Price the chair seat.** If they want your name and your engineer's time to run the process, that's worth something to them — ask for something back: co-marketing rights as "reference implementation," early visibility into the other five vendors' roadmaps, or a seat on whatever governance body controls the spec after v1.

**Bottom line:** the invitation is real signal that standardization is coming whether you're in the room or not — that argues for showing up, not for staying out. It does not argue for handing over your one senior engineer for 18 months to chair the whole thing unconditionally. Go back with a scoped counter-offer: co-chair the plumbing, participate-but-don't-cede on normalization, 6-month checkpoint. If they balk at scoping normalization to "contract, not method," that refusal itself is the answer to your question — it tells you the standard was never about interoperability.
