# Engineering employer-brand plan: Rill & Marrow, FY2027 hiring push

## Where you actually stand

**Stage:** early-stage (11 engineers, bootstrapped), not a scale-up yet. That matters more than it sounds: early-stage companies run on founder network, referrals, and targeted outbound — content channels haven't had time to compound. Six hires, six from your network is not a gap, it's the correct pattern for your size. Don't read it as "we've done nothing" — you've been running the highest-ROI channel by default.

**Instrumentation: this is the actual blocker, before any channel ranking means anything.** You track nothing. That means:
- No source-of-hire split (though "6/6 network" is itself a data point — that's 100% at a stage where the skill's own heuristic considers >50% referral+organic "brand is working, don't rank yet").
- No offer-acceptance rate. This is the single number that reorders everything below. If it's under ~70%, the priority is auditing why people say no, not publishing content. Get this from memory even for those six offers — how many people you made offers to vs. who accepted — before doing anything else on this list.
- No idea what your Glassdoor/comparable rating, comp-database presence, or Blind/forum sentiment look like. You need this before writing a word of employer-brand content, because content that contradicts what people find when they check will cost you more than no content. Pull it yourself this week: search the company name on the review sites and forums, check if you're listed on comp databases with numbers that match reality.

Until those two things exist (an acceptance-rate number, a verification-surface check), treat everything below as directional, not funded.

## Ranked list: what to invest in

Ranked by return per hour at your stage — cheap, fast, reversible first; expensive, slow, irreversible last. Not by "which sounds most impressive."

1. **Referral bonuses / structured referral program** — formalize what's already working. You've proven your network converts at 100%; the move is widening the aperture from "founder's network" to "all 11 engineers' networks," with an actual bonus and a quarterly structured ask, not a vague standing offer. This is the one lever with real practitioner backing for outperforming other channels on hire quality (Stripe's Greg Brockman found referrals consistently beat careers-page inbound). Cost: days, a payroll line. Do this first.

2. **Better job ads / process fixes** — you'll need real postings soon; your network well isn't infinite at "get serious about hiring." Write postings with the actual stack, actual on-call reality, actual interview stages — no filler. Fix the loop itself: in the largest survey on this (Stack Overflow 2022, 83k+ devs), candidates abandon processes over a disliked stack (32%), disorganization (24%), odd questions (24%), and poor reviews (24%). This is the cheapest, fastest-reversible move on the list, and it's also insurance — a bad posting or loop actively poisons every other channel you fund.

3. **Salary bands / comp transparency** (you didn't ask for this one, but it ties with #2 at the top of the ratio, so it's worth naming): specific reward information measurably improves candidates' fit assessment vs. "competitive pay" language. Cheap to do informally even without formal leveling — costs a decision, not a budget.

4. **Engineering blog** — see the dedicated section below. Verdict: worth a small, protected, sustained bet, not a content calendar.

5. **Conference talks** — ties with the blog on value (both sell technical depth to the same senior audience), splits on capacity: whoever on your team actually wants to get on stage should do this instead of writing, not in addition to. Don't fund both blog and talks at real intensity with an 11-person team; pick based on who'll actually do the work.

6. **Open-sourcing part of your core** — this is the one item on your list that isn't like the others: it's a licensing/IP decision you can't take back, not a marketing channel. It's also the single best-documented channel in this whole field for dev-tools specifically — Supabase sourced roughly its first 50 hires from its OSS community with zero outbound for two-plus years; HashiCorp built the community before the company. If you do this, it only works funded properly: a maintainer who gets paid time to review PRs and a real contributor-to-hire path, not a repo you toss over the wall. A token open-source effort reads worse than not doing it at all — it signals neglect, not openness. This deserves its own separate decision session with whoever owns your product roadmap, weighing competitive-moat exposure against the funnel value — it's not something to rank into a content calendar by default.

7. **Public handbook** — lowest priority for you right now. It's a written commitment your 11 people can be held to, and at this size your culture is still being formed, not yet stable enough to codify without it going stale in six months. Revisit once you're past ~25-30 engineers.

**What this starves, on purpose:** the handbook and (for now) open-sourcing the core. Promotion condition for open-sourcing: once you've made an explicit, deliberate call on the IP tradeoff with real founder attention — not a byproduct of "we should be more open." Promotion condition for the handbook: once your team is large enough that culture needs writing down rather than living in daily contact with you.

## The engineering-blog ROI question, specifically

Straight answer: **there is no trustworthy number for "X hours of blog → Y hires."** No one has isolated a single content channel's causal effect on engineer application or acceptance behavior — not for you, not for anyone. If you see a deck claiming "72% of developers check a company's blog before applying," that stat is fabricated by repetition — it doesn't trace to any publisher and shouldn't be used, even hedged as "industry-cited." Same goes for "Gartner says employer branding cuts turnover 69%" (real Gartner wording, but self-reported advisory research, not a study) and "LinkedIn: strong brand = 50% more qualified applicants" (not a real LinkedIn figure — it's a garble of a 2.5x-applicants stat and an unrelated cost-per-hire number). Don't build a business case for the blog on any of these.

What's actually true, and what it implies for your hours:

- The mechanism that works is a **realistic job preview** — specific, accurate, technically real content. This is the one causally-backed idea in the whole field (peer-reviewed meta-analyses, not vendor research): accurate previews of the actual job modestly reduce turnover and build trust. A blog post that says "how we rewrote our ingestion pipeline and the tradeoffs we didn't love" is doing this. A blog post that says "life at Rill & Marrow" without technical substance is not, and is closer to the culture-marketing failure mode that backfires when reality doesn't match.
- The audience this reaches is **senior engineers specifically** — depth content and community reputation are how staff/principal engineers get reached; they're not found through job postings at all, by practitioner consensus. If your open roles skew senior, the blog is doing real work postings can't. If you're mostly hiring mid-level, this channel matters less and #1-#3 above matter more.
- Given your actual capacity (solo founder, 11 engineers, no one hired to write), size this small and protected rather than ambitious: 1 engineer, or you, writing something real every 4-6 weeks — written by the person who actually built the thing, not ghostwritten — beats a 2x/week calendar nobody sustains past month two. Treat it as a multi-quarter compounding asset (payoff shows up in 2+ quarters via search/community discovery, not week-over-week), and track *leading* indicators instead of a fake ROI: does inbound mention specific posts, does time-to-fill on senior roles move, does anyone cite the blog in an interview. Don't kill it at week 8 for lack of a conversion number — that's not how this channel pays back, but don't fund it past what one real, interested writer can sustain either.
- Bonus for a dev-tools company specifically: a technical blog does double duty as product marketing/credibility content for your actual product, not just recruiting. That's a real reason to weight it slightly above the generic ranking here — but size the hours against the combined payoff, not hiring alone.

## Fix-first, before any of this ships

- Get your offer-acceptance number from the six hires you've made. If it's low, that's the finding, not the blog.
- Pull your review-site rating, comp-database presence, and forum sentiment this week. If any of it contradicts what you'd want to publish, fix the operational reality (or say nothing) before you fix the messaging.

## Measurement baseline going forward

Track: offer-acceptance rate, source-of-hire split, time-to-fill, referral share of pipeline. Re-plan triggers: acceptance rate under ~70% (stop top-of-funnel spend, audit the trust gap first), or a rating/forum signal that contradicts anything you've published.

## Refused

No seeded or solicited reviews, ever — engineers read manufactured sentiment as a worse signal than the weakness it's hiding. No "72%/Gartner 69%/LinkedIn 50%" stats in any deck or pitch to yourself. No culture claims (blog, postings, or otherwise) that your actual on-call load, hierarchy, or process would contradict on day one.

## Review

Revisit this ranking when: your network-sourced pipeline starts thinning as you scale past ~15-20 engineers (the early-stage assumption breaks), you make a real decision on open-sourcing the core, or your first tracked offer-acceptance number comes in.
