Fix costs under 1 hour: add "source" field (referral / job ad / inbound / blog / conference / other) to whatever tracks candidates, even a spreadsheet. Do this before touching any item below.

## Ranked investment list (dev-tools startup, 11 engineers, bootstrapped, network-only hiring so far)

1. **Referral bonuses — formalize what already works.** All 6 hires came through your network. That's not luck, it's signal: your channel is proven. Referral bonus ($1.5k–$5k typical for dev-tools/eng roles, paid at 90-day mark not signing) turns your personal network into your whole team's network. Cheapest dollar-per-hire of any item here, and fastest to pay off — weeks, not months. Do this first.

2. **Fix the job ads / careers page.** Every other channel below (referrals, blog readers, OSS contributors, conference contacts) eventually lands on a job post. A weak one leaks candidates you already earned. Half a day of work: real comp range, real tech stack, real day-one tasks, no boilerplate ("fast-paced," "rockstar"). High leverage because it multiplies every other channel's output, not a channel itself.

3. **Open-source part of core — situational, but fits your category.** Dev-tools engineers evaluate companies by reading code, not job ads. A well-chosen OSS slice (not your moat) turns evaluators into a visible, self-selected candidate pool — contributors who engage with your code have already screened themselves for interest and skill. Real cost: legal review, choosing a piece that isn't your moat, and ongoing maintenance (issues, PRs) that someone has to own past launch. Medium effort, only do it if you have 1 clear non-core piece and someone willing to own community for it.

4. **Engineering blog — real, but slow. See dedicated section below.** Don't expect it to source hires in year one.

5. **Public handbook — defer.** GitLab/Basecamp-style transparency handbooks work when culture and process are already stable enough to document without going stale in a month. At 11 people, still moving fast, a handbook becomes upkeep debt before it becomes a hiring asset. Revisit at 20–30 people, or sooner if you go remote-first (remote candidates weight this higher).

6. **Conference talks — lowest hiring ROI at this size.** Cost: engineer/founder prep time (10–20 hrs per talk) plus travel, for a small, unpredictable trickle of candidate contacts. Works as a brand/awareness play once you already have blog + OSS credibility to back it up — talks convert better when people can go verify you afterward. Not a lane to your next hires now.

## Engineering blog: what ROI to actually expect

**Don't expect direct applicants in year one.** Attribution almost never works that way — a blog rarely gets "I applied because of your post." What it actually moves:

- **Offer-accept rate**, not application volume. Candidates who read a credible technical post before onsite convert to accepted offers at a noticeably higher rate — it's a trust multiplier late in the funnel, not a top-of-funnel generator.
- **Referral quality**, indirectly. Your network channel (item 1) gets stronger when the person you're recruiting can point a candidate at real proof of engineering culture instead of just a personal vouch.
- **Organic search traffic**, but only after 6–12 months of consistent posting. Nothing meaningful before then — SEO compounding is slow, full stop.

**Hour budget: cap at 4–6 hrs/week total across the whole team, not per person.** At 11 engineers, pulling someone for a from-scratch 8-hour blog post is a real roadmap cost. Cheapest posts to sustain: things you're already producing — postmortems, release deep-dives, "how we built X" — repurposed into a post, not invented content. That cuts real marginal cost to 2–3 hrs (write + edit), not 6–8.

**Cadence: 1 post every 3–4 weeks is sustainable at this size. Weekly is not** — you'll either burn engineer time you need elsewhere or the quality drops and it stops working as a trust signal at all.

**Bottom line:** budget it as a 12-month compounding asset for accept-rate and brand, not a 2026 hiring-pipeline line item. Items 1 and 2 are where next year's hires actually come from.
