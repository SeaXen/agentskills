Here's the review — the format math is the real problem, not the content quality.

**The 10-minute story doesn't fit. Cut it to 90 seconds.**

Run the numbers: 75 minutes, 5 panellists, 1 moderator. Strip intro (~5 min), quick round-robin intros (~4 min), and audience Q&A (typically reserved 10–15 min at the end), and you're left with roughly 50–55 minutes of actual discussion. Split five ways across a handful of moderator questions, each panellist's *total* airtime for the entire session lands around 10–12 minutes — delivered in bursts of 60–90 seconds per answer, not in one block. No moderator on a 5-person panel hands one person 10 uninterrupted minutes; it would eat 15%+ of the whole show and visibly starve the other four.

Fix: build the story as nesting dolls, not one fixed script.
- **30-second cut** — one sentence of stakes, one sentence of what changed, one number. Your default.
- **90-second cut** — adds the conflict (the failed first attempt, the resistance, the embarrassing baseline metric) and the turn.
- **3-minute cut** — full version, only deployed if the moderator explicitly says "take your time" or a follow-up opens the door.

Never plan to use the long version. Plan to have it ready in case a gap opens, and be genuinely fine if it never does.

**Give the story a number and a scar, or it drowns.**

Five panellists talking "future of platform engineering" will produce a lot of abstract, interchangeable commentary. A concrete internal anecdote is your strongest asset precisely because it's the one thing the other four can't say — but only if it has real stakes: a before/after metric (onboarding time, adoption rate, ticket volume), a moment it nearly failed, not just "we built a portal and it went well." Sand off anything proprietary before you say it out loud — assume the panel is recorded and public.

**Stress-test the three messages for two things: are they opinions, and are they yours.**

- *Opinion, not platitude.* "Platform engineering will keep maturing" is filler — anyone on that stage could say it and the audience forgets it in ten seconds. "If your platform team hasn't shipped a golden path a new hire can use in week one, you don't have a platform, you have a ticket queue" is a position — specific, a little uncomfortable, quotable. Check each of your three against that bar.
- *Yours, not generic.* On a "future of X" panel, expect real overlap with co-panellists on the obvious beats (self-service, DX, AI-assisted tooling). Anchor each message to something only your portal rollout gives you — a number, a decision you'd defend, a thing you got wrong first. Generic wisdom is forgettable when four other people are also offering it.
- *Bridgeable, not scripted-in.* The moderator won't ask questions shaped exactly like your three messages. Prep 2–3 short bridge lines ("That connects to something we hit when…", "I'd actually push back on that…") so each message can surface off whatever's actually asked, instead of feeling forced.

**Don't over-script the whole appearance.**

Three prepared messages plus one over-long story risks turning your seat into a monologue machine on a format that lives or dies on cross-talk. Two cheap additions do more for how you're remembered than more prepared content:
- One planned moment of *respectful disagreement* with a likely co-panellist position — audiences and moderators remember panels with real friction, not five people agreeing pleasantly.
- One line that directly reacts to whatever another panellist just said ("Building on what [name] said about…") — the strongest panel moments are visibly live, not visibly rehearsed.

**Plan the close and the after, not just the middle.**

The "final thoughts" round is the most quotable, most clipped moment of the session — have one sharp, standalone line ready for it (should work read in isolation, since it's what gets live-tweeted or pulled into a recap). And since only ~90 seconds of your rollout story will actually air live, plan to publish the full version yourself — a LinkedIn recap posted within 24–48 hours, tagging the moderator and co-panellists, is where most of the actual reach comes from, not the room in Amsterdam.
