## 1. Withholding "I work for Wrenfield" until the end

The skill is explicit: *"State the affiliation early and plainly... The audience finds out anyway, and a late reveal reads as a hidden advertisement."* Podcast audiences check show notes and your bio; they will know you're Wrenfield before you tell them. Sitting on it doesn't make you less salesy — it makes you look like you were trying to sneak product in under cover of "just here to talk shop," which is worse for exactly the audience you need to trust you (an evaluator or buyer's proxy who might carry your name into a procurement conversation).

Say it early and plainly, once, then stop repeating the company name outside the invited pitch:

> "I lead DevRel at Wrenfield Systems — we build an API gateway. I'll try not to make this a demo, but full disclosure up front."

Then it's spent. You don't owe it again until the closing "where can people find you."

## 2. "I don't like to talk about competitors" when Kong/Apigee comes up

This is question class 1 in the skill's hard-question bank, verbatim: *competitor comparison, "how is this different from the obvious alternative."* The listed failure is *"the guest refuses to name competitors, or lists features instead of naming the one workload where the trade-off flips."* The skill's own rule for step 10: *"Name competitors and be fair about them. Refusing to name them signals insecurity to engineers."*

For your context this is worse than usual: your buyers are doing exactly the kind of technical diligence that ends up in a procurement doc. Refusing to compare doesn't keep you neutral — it tells a technical listener you either can't defend the difference or won't say it out loud, and it hands them nothing to bring back to their own evaluation. The concede-then-bridge shape (ABC: acknowledge, honest answer first, then bridge) is what survives this audience:

> "Kong and Apigee are both solid, and if you're already deep in Kong's plugin ecosystem, switching costs more than it's worth. Where we diverge is [the specific thing Wrenfield actually does differently for enterprise governance/procurement-scale deployments — I need that detail from you to write the real line]."

## 3. Closing with the pricing page

Step 10, directly: *"point to a neutral resource (docs, repo) rather than a sales page."* A pricing page with the demo form embedded is the sales page the skill is warning you away from. On a technical show, closing the episode with a funnel destination reads as the ad you spent the whole episode trying not to be — it undoes the credibility the rest of the appearance built, right at the one moment listeners are paying closest attention.

For a six-figure, procurement-gated sale, the podcast listener is almost never the person who fills out a demo form live — they're doing early research, or they're an individual engineer who'll mention your name in a Slack thread that eventually reaches the buyer. A neutral, technical destination is what actually travels into that conversation and gets cited later:

> "Docs are at [wrenfield.dev/docs], and I'm on [X/LinkedIn] if you want to argue about retry budgets with me directly."

Point the pricing/demo motion at your own promotion loop after publication (step 12), not at the on-air CTA.
