Skip full script. Not deliverable — matches your exact failure mode.

Word-for-word script kills recall on stage: lose one word, lose thread, freeze. Bookmarks survive a blank; memorized prose doesn't. Skill's own failure table: symptom "speaker freezes" → cause "word-for-word script" → fix "bookmarks and note cues." Refusing on purpose, not oversight.

**Fix for freezing — cue cards, not script:**

1. One cue per section, 3-5 words max. Ex: "outage line, one number" — not full sentence. Cue triggers the idea, not exact wording.
2. Attach timing checkpoint to each cue: "past 0:14 → cut pillar 3, jump to demo." Lets you see live you're behind and know exactly what to drop.

**Rehearsal — Accordion Method (de Montebello), builds recall from structure not word order:**

1. Speak whole talk to 3-min timer.
2. Same talk, 2-min timer.
3. Same talk, 1-min timer.
4. Same talk, 30-sec timer.
5. Expand back: 1, 2, 3 min, add material back in deliberately.

What survives the 30-sec rep = arrow + pillars, your unloseable core. What only reappears on the way back up = cut list, already ranked, no live guessing. Run it once in front of a colleague — they should repeat the arrow back unprompted. That's the check it worked.

**4,200 words → minutes: no single number.** Depends on YOUR measured rate, not a table value — skill explicit on this. Measure now, 2 min:

1. Read 200 of your own words aloud, English, normal delivery.
2. Time it.
3. 200 ÷ minutes = your wpm.

Rough range till measured: prepared talk, second language, usually 100-140 wpm (slower than casual conversation — normal, not a flaw).

- 100 wpm → 42 min
- 120 wpm → 35 min
- 140 wpm → 30 min

35-min slot: 4,200 words risks running long unless real rate sits above ~120 wpm. This is the single most common talk failure — written to the material, not the slot. Measure before trusting any of the above.

Also matters: is Q&A inside the 35 min or separate? Changes real speaking budget, changes every number above.

**185-254 wpm — no, don't pace to it.** That band is the fastest quintile of one study's corpus (48-254 wpm range, mean 156 wpm), never a target the paper set. Study found engagement rising with rate, but its authors call rate a marker of enthusiasm, not a lever, and advise against forcing delivery faster. Forcing pace on a second language trades clarity for a number that was never meant as a target. Pace for clarity, at your measured natural rate.
