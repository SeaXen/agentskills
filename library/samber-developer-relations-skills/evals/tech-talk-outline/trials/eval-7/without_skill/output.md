## On the 185–254 wpm figure

Don't pace to that. That range is closer to natural conversational/reading speed, not recommended public-speaking pace. Standard guidance for prepared talks (Toastmasters, TED coaches) is **120–160 wpm** — slower for technical content, slower again for a non-native speaker reading from memory. Three reasons it matters for you specifically:

1. **Recovery margin.** At 150 wpm you have more silence and slack built into your own delivery — if you lose your place, a half-second pause is invisible to the room. At 220 wpm, the same pause feels like a visible stall, which is exactly the freeze you're trying to avoid.
2. **Breath and articulation in a second language.** Fast pacing compounds accent/pronunciation load. Slower pacing gives you more control over both.
3. **Audience comprehension for technical content** is generally *better* at 130–160 wpm, not worse — the 185–254 range is more suited to casual storytelling or hype-style delivery, not a 35-minute technical/informative slot.

**Target 140–150 wpm.** At that pace your 4,200 words run **28–30 minutes** — already close to your 35-minute slot, with 5–7 minutes of margin left over for pauses, laughs, slide clicks, applause. That margin is your safety net: it means you never have to rush to make time, and rushing is what causes freezes.

If you want the full 35 minutes as scripted content (not buffer), you need roughly **4,900–5,250 words** total at that pace — so about 700–1,050 more words to write, or fewer if you're fine treating some of the buffer as breathing room rather than content.
