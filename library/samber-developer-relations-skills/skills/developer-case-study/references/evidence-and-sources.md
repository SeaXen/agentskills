# Evidence and sources

Every number, threshold and structural claim this skill states, split into **sourced** (traceable to a named published artefact) and **self-set** (a working baseline rather than a published figure). Quote a self-set number externally only as "our working baseline", never as an industry standard.

Read across the seven:

- The original vendor-published pair (ShareChat, Duolingo) carries the metrics and the quotes and almost no cost.
- The community-published pair (Snyk, marketplace) carries the costs and the failure.
- The three later additions (Attentive, Grid Dynamics, Outreach) are vendor-published yet disclose real costs or trade-offs anyway - proof a limitations section is achievable outside the community-published half, not just a theoretical option.
- The corpus still leans on one publisher's editorial voice: four of the seven are Temporal-published (ShareChat, Duolingo, Attentive, Grid Dynamics), though only three of those four (ShareChat, Duolingo, Attentive) use Temporal's dedicated case-study template - Grid Dynamics is a blog post co-authored by the customer's own engineers, a distinct genre. Widen past Temporal before trusting the spine shape as a cross-vendor norm.

All belong in one artefact.

A related but structurally different account, cited for context rather than folded into the corpus above: Daylight Security's own engineering blog describes migrating _off_ Temporal Cloud after per-schedule pricing made roughly 500 workflow-starts/second prohibitively expensive, concluding their "costs are now predictable and significantly lower" after self-hosting (Alon Gluzman and Guy Grinberg, `daylight.ai/blog/how-we-migrated-off-temporal-cloud-without-downtime`). It is not a vendor-published case study - it is the customer's own unvarnished account of a vendor's limitation, published on their own terms, and a reminder that the customer-authored version of a migration story can read very differently from the vendor-authored one.

## Sourced claims

- **The Challenge / Solution / Results spine is the category default.** Four independent instances: ShareChat and Duolingo above, `LeadMagic/gtm-skills@case-study-builder`, and `anthropics/knowledge-work-plugins@draft-content`, whose case-study template is title, customer overview, challenge, solution, results with metrics, customer quote, CTA.
- **Vendor studies quote hands-on engineers with real internal titles**, not executives - ShareChat's two quotes are an SDE-3 and an engineering manager, both inside the payments team that did the work.
- **Headline metrics live in the title.** Temporal's index of 15 studies puts the number in the link text: "99.9% reliability", "1 billion+ agent Actions per month", "four million daily video workflows", "100x".
- **Recurring metric shapes**: reliability rate before/after, monthly production volume, cycle-time collapse, adoption spread inside the customer, dated migration window. ShareChat carries all five.
- **Naming and logo rights are often already granted in the contract.** Vercel's Terms of Service §16.4 "Customer Name": "During the term of this Agreement, you grant Vercel a non-exclusive, royalty-free, fully-paid up license to use and reproduce your trademarks, trade names and logos in Vercel's marketing materials and website(s) and to indicate that you are a Vercel customer". That clause covers the logo and the fact of being a customer. It does not cover quotes, metrics, architecture detail or a narrative - those still need the consent chain.
- **A published case study can disclose what it cost and still ship.** The Snyk and marketplace studies above are the existence proof; the marketplace one is a named practitioner walking through his own failure.
- **"A case study without numbers is a testimonial"** - `LeadMagic/gtm-skills@case-study-builder`.
- **A developer-focused company's own public process confirms the customer-first, legal/PR-optional shape of the consent chain.** PostHog's marketing handbook has the customer tag reviewed on GitHub (or Google Docs) for a fact check, not a copy edit, then adds: "legal and PR teams will sometimes want to be looped in for approval as well." No day/week timeline is given - PostHog's own text: "Most people are excited to be featured and will sign off quickly." Source: PostHog Handbook, "Customer case studies," `posthog.com/handbook/marketing/customer-case-studies`.
- **Why approval chains stall is documented, even without hard timeline numbers.** Joel Klettke, a B2B case-study copywriting consultant, discusses this at length on The Campaign podcast (97th Floor, Feb 21, 2025): vague asks give legal and PR nothing concrete to approve, so bounding the ask ("this is what they want to talk about - are we comfortable with this or with these metrics?") speeds review; normalizing the request early, getting leadership visibility, and maintaining velocity all matter because a stalled story gets deprioritized rather than revisited. Source: `the-campaign.castos.com/episodes/building-a-case-study-growth-engine-from-one-off-to-always-on-w-joel-klettke-1`.
- **Vendor-produced case studies rank well behind first-hand product experience in B2B technology buying, but are not negligible.** TrustRadius's 2022 B2B Buying Disconnect Report (survey of 2,185 technology buyers, February 2022) lists vendor-produced case studies at 28% among "most impactful resources when making the decision" - behind product demos (71%), free trial/account (67%), prior experience with the product (67%) and user reviews (59%), but ahead of vendor-provided customer references, third-party publications, vendor marketing collateral and the vendor blog. This is a general B2B-technology-buyer figure, not isolated to developer-focused case studies specifically.
- **No published engagement metric isolates "case studies aimed at developers" as its own category.** SlashData's Q3 2023 Developer Nation survey (17,000+ developers; Bleona Bicaj, "The types of content developers prefer," Feb 21, 2024, `slashdata.co/post/the-types-of-content-developers-prefer`) reports format preference - long-form video 62%, long-form text 57%, social media 31% - but buckets case studies inside "long-form text" rather than reporting them separately. Contently's "First Engagement Benchmarks for Downloadable Documents" (May 19, 2016; 100,000+ decision-maker sessions; `contently.com/strategist/2016/05/19/report-engagement-benchmarks-downloadable-documents`) groups case studies with e-books, white papers and sales sheets as one category: average 13.4 pages, average engagement 2:03, 35% of readers under 30 seconds. Neither source separates case studies from its broader bucket.

## Self-set baselines

Working baselines, not published figures.

- **1,200-1,800 words** for a full study. Chosen to sit just under the three measured examples (1,650, 2,100 and 2,400 words) while adding a limitations section none of them fully carries.
- **Per-section word budgets** in `case_study_outline.md`. Proportions, not measurements - scale them to the material.
- **Two to four numbers** in the results section. The published studies carry more; fewer well-sourced numbers survive review better.
- **Five qualification gates** and the **seven-check publication gate**. Both are binary checklists, not an industry standard.
- **Roughly 18 months** before a study needs re-verification. A convention, not a measurement - pick a shorter window for fast-moving adopters.
- **Four-permission consent chain.** The structure (customer first, legal/PR looped in as needed) matches PostHog's own public process and the mechanics Joel Klettke describes, sourced above. The **typical delays** (immediate / days / days-to-weeks / weeks) remain estimates - no source found gives a quantified per-step timeline, only qualitative confirmation of why each step can stall.
- **The five-rung anonymization ladder.** Self-set, not a published model.

## Named methods this skill borrows

- **Challenge / Solution / Results (CSR).** The category default spine, sourced above. This skill keeps it and adds three sections it never carries: alternatives rejected, metric provenance, limitations.
- **The Mom Test (Rob Fitzpatrick).** Its core interview rule - ask about specifics that already happened, not opinions or hypotheticals - is exactly what turns "it's much faster now" into "42 minutes to 11 minutes on the Grafana dashboard". The book targets discovery interviews with prospects; borrow the questioning discipline, not its scope.
