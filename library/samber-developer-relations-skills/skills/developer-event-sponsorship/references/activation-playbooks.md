# On-site activation playbooks

Contents: activation types, in step 6's order → staffing rhythm → staff brief → booth conversation examples → follow-up sequences.

## Activation types

Listed in the efficiency order step 6 sets - help-desk booth > prize track > side event > workshop - with the scholarship fund off that ladder. Step 6 owns the ranking and the promotion conditions; this page holds the execution detail.

**Help-desk booth.** At least two technical people per shift, both able to answer the follow-up question, with one always free when the other is deep in someone's laptop. Keep the queue visible and short: a waiting developer leaves. Fails when staffed by people who cannot go past the demo path.

**Prize track (hackathons).** Frame the category around the problem - "best use of real-time data", not "best use of our SDK" - so entries stay creative and judging stays defensible. Sit judges who understand the technology; judging on future sales potential, or favouring teams the booth helped, is visible and remembered. Publish the criteria before entries open, because they cannot be changed afterwards.

Prizes people want:

- cash
- quality hardware
- conference tickets
- credits with no redemption maze
- time with someone interesting

**Side event.** A dinner, a run, a community meetup in the margins of the conference. Twenty right people beat 400 badge scans for ecosystem and pipeline objectives, and it is almost worthless for awareness. Often available without any sponsorship tier at all, which makes the fee the small part: the evening costs you whoever has to be sharp on the floor the next morning.

**Workshop or lab.** Ninety minutes of hands-on work on a real problem, product included but not the subject: attendees self-select, complete something, and leave with working code. Needs a tested environment, a fallback for venue wi-fi, and a curriculum written weeks ahead - reuse one you already have rather than writing it for a single event.

**Scholarship or diversity fund.** Money that sends attendees who could not otherwise come. Buys goodwill and stage-level thanks, produces no leads by design, and is never a substitute for an activation that carried a pipeline target.

## Staffing rhythm

Coverage should follow attention, not hall hours.

| Event type | Heavy staffing                                                                         | Skeleton crew                               |
| ---------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Conference | breaks between sessions, opening reception, first hour after lunch                     | during keynotes, last hour of the final day |
| Hackathon  | kickoff and team formation, the evening build peak, the hours before submission, demos | overnight, meal times, the mid-event lull   |
| Meetup     | the 30 minutes before the talk and the hour after                                      | during the talk                             |

Rotate people off the floor. A tired engineer gives worse answers than no engineer, and the conversation that matters is as likely to happen at 16:40 as at 10:00.

## Staff brief

Cover before the doors open, in one page:

- The single objective, and the qualification bar and next-step definition in the exact words used in the log.
- What the audience is building this week, and the three questions they will most likely ask.
- The demo path, its five-minute win, and what to do when it breaks.
- The line between helping and pitching: help first, offer the product only when it answers the problem in front of you.
- Consent script for any contact capture, and what happens to the data afterwards.
- Code of conduct, and who to escalate an incident to.
- Who logs conversations, and when (end of each shift, not end of the event).

## Booth conversation examples

Constructed for illustration - copy the shape, not the specifics; no real event's transcript is quoted here.

**Works.** An attendee mentions their deployment pipeline takes forty minutes. The staffer asks what runs in those forty minutes, spots that the test matrix is serialised, sketches the fix on the attendee's own laptop (with or without the product) and only then says which part the product would take over. They ask whether it would help to look at the real repo next week, log the conversation as qualified with "call Thursday, sending the config we sketched", and send exactly that on Wednesday.

**Fails.** An attendee walks past. The staffer offers a t-shirt in exchange for a badge scan, opens with the funding announcement and the feature list, does not ask what the attendee works on, and hands over a QR code to a generic demo page.

The scan enters a nurture sequence that references a conversation that never happened. Two hundred of these produce a full spreadsheet, zero opportunities, and an audience that avoids the booth next year.

The difference is a question about the attendee's work asked before anything about the product.

## Follow-up sequences

- **Within 48 hours** - personal message to every qualified conversation, referencing the specific problem discussed and delivering whatever was promised. No template, no batch.
- **Winners and standout projects (hackathons)** - congratulations, an offer to feature the project with permission, extended access, and an introduction to whoever internally can actually help.
- **Everyone who opted in** - one short message: thanks, the two or three resources most relevant to the event's theme, an invitation to the community. No hard sell, no automatic addition to a sales sequence.
- **30 days later** - check in on the projects and evaluations that were live at the event; this is where hackathon and workshop contacts either become users or go quiet.
- **Never** - importing an attendee list nobody consented to, or following up with a sequence that ignores what was discussed.
