# Published DevRel budget figures

Everything below comes from one source: the _State of Developer Relations 2024_ report (11th annual survey), run by DevRel.Agency and published at `stateofdeveloperrelations.com/2024devrelreport` on 10 September 2024 - 310 valid respondents, 242 completions, 33 countries. These figures are two budget cycles old, so check for a newer edition before quoting them.

Treat this page as evidence for framing an argument, never as a target to hit. Say the sample size out loud whenever you quote a number from it.

## Annual program budget

| Band          | Share | Movement vs 2023                                   |
| ------------- | ----- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Under $100K   | 16.7% | down from 36.7%                                    |
| $10K-$50K     | 4.6%  | down from 15.1%                                    |
| $101K-$500K   | 12.8% | up from 8.4% - most frequently reported known band |
| Over $1M      | 6.8%  | -                                                  |
| No set budget | 17.8% | -                                                  |
| Not sure      | 33.5% | -                                                  |

The most useful row is the last one. A third of practitioners cannot state their own budget and another sixth have none, so a user who does not know their number is in the majority, not in trouble. Establishing the envelope is a legitimate first deliverable.

## Salary inclusion

- 60% of program budgets exclude staff salaries.
- 26% include salaries for all DevRel roles; 3% for some roles.

Consequence: the program budget and the headcount budget usually have different owners and different approval cycles. No two budgets - and no budget against these bands - can be compared until both state whether salaries are inside.

## Direction of travel over the trailing 12 months

Increased 29% (up from 16% in 2023) · No change 24% · Decreased 24% (down from 37%).

Read: the 2023 contraction eased, but roughly a quarter of programs still lost budget in a single year. Planning for a cut is the base case, not pessimism - which is why the allocation ships with an ordered cut list.

## The allocation split, and its three caveats

Question asked: "Of your total budget, what % is allocated to each of the following categories?"

| Category                    | Mean share |
| --------------------------- | ---------- |
| Events                      | 41.1%      |
| Staff salaries              | 30.9%      |
| Content production/creation | 19.5%      |
| Marketing/promotion/ads     | 13.0%      |

1. **n = 47.** The question was shown only to respondents who said they knew their budget. This is a small-sample average, not an industry standard.
2. **The shares sum to 104.5%**, so respondents answered on inconsistent bases - some including salaries, some not.
3. **The category set is incomplete.** Community, developer tooling and infrastructure, OSS sponsorship, and education or certification have no row at all, so their spend is hidden inside the four buckets. A pillar-based allocation cannot be mapped onto this table one-to-one.

Honest uses:

- To show that events have historically dominated devrel spend, so a plan that funds them lightly is making a deliberate, defensible departure.
- To ask a team whose split looks nothing like this what they know that the field does not - a question that usually produces a good answer or exposes an inherited split.

Dishonest uses, which will be caught:

- Presenting 41.1% as the correct event share.
- Benchmarking a specific program against these means.
- Implying the four categories are the standard taxonomy.

## Related structural findings from the same survey

- Reporting line: marketing 33.1%, product 21.7%, CEO 20.3%, engineering 19.9%, sales 3.2%. No line holds a majority, and the line usually determines who signs the budget.
- 43.6% report more than one team performing devrel activities, up from 33% - so a single program budget frequently understates total company spend on the function.
- Team size: 2-5 people is the most common band at 35.2%; solo practitioners 18.2%; 69% of teams are under 10 people.

The fragmentation figure matters for allocation: before claiming a pillar is underfunded, check whether another team is already funding it from a budget nobody in the conversation controls.

## Numbers to refuse rather than estimate

Say plainly that these are not figures anyone can quote, rather than inventing one:

- DevRel budget as a percentage of revenue, of marketing spend, or per developer reached.
- Cost per developer acquired, per community member, or per documentation page.
- A recommended split across community, tooling, education or sponsorship.
- Headcount-to-budget ratios at any stage.

For each of them, the program's own prior period is the only legitimate baseline, and where even that is missing, the line is an experiment with a kill date rather than a funded bet.
