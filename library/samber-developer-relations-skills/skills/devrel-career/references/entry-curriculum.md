# Entry curriculum: zero to hireable

Contents: months 0-1 · the ranked first artefacts · months 3-6 · certifications · the internal-transfer path · realistic timeline and stage thresholds.

For candidates with no DevRel title yet. Build the artefacts first - they are the application - then learn the field's norms inside its communities, then apply. Alex Lakatos: "It's easier to get your first job in Developer Relations for a company when you've already got some domain knowledge."

## Months 0-1: orient and pick the niche

Read the field's two entry texts: Mary Thengvall's _The Business Value of Developer Relations_ (Apress, 2018), the first published book on the practice, and Sam Julien's free ebook _Getting Started in Developer Relations_ with his "How to Start Doing Dev Rel Right Now".

Pick one tool or product you already use and like - Julien's first step is literally "Pick a tool or SaaS product that you absolutely love". The niche is what everything else compounds on, and picking a product you use means the first artefacts come from real usage instead of research.

Follow the field while you work: DevRel Weekly, the `devrelcollective/awesome-devrel` repository, the Community Pulse podcast.

## Months 1-3: the first artefacts, ranked

Rank by return per day of your own work. **Efficiency:** friction log > blog post > community-answer streak > open-source contribution > meetup talk. Together they cover the evidence types a portfolio audit scores, so the order is what to ship first, not what to skip.

- **effort** (days of your own work, plus who else has to say yes): meetup talk > open-source contribution > community-answer streak > friction log > blog post. The last two both sit on a channel you control, gated on nobody's approval. A blog post is a day or two of solo writing; a friction log takes a few days, because most of them go into using the tool cold.
- **value** (the hiring signal each one evidences): meetup talk > friction log > blog post > open-source contribution == community-answer streak. The tie: both prove you can operate inside someone else's project, and both read as ongoing participation rather than as a standalone artefact.

1. **A friction log on the chosen tool.** A few days' work, and it evidences the rarest portfolio signal - carrying the developer's experience back to the company.
2. **A blog post or tutorial on that tool.** Proves you can explain clearly; the cheapest artefact to produce.
3. **A community-answer streak** in the tool's forum, chat or issue tracker. Low intensity over weeks, and it produces the "developers I've helped" answer an interview will ask for.
4. **An open-source contribution to that tool's project.** Documentation contributions count fully. A maintainer has to review it, so the pace is not yours.
5. **A meetup talk.** The rehearsal ground for conferences; a five-minute lightning slot counts.

**What this order starves:** the meetup talk loses every ratio round - most effort, and gated on an organiser's calendar - yet it carries the highest value of the five and is the only item clearing Leggetter's published hiring bar. Promote it to first whenever the loop being prepared for has a presentation round, or the target is an outreach-focused advocate role, where writing and speaking are the two heaviest signals.

Delete rather than demote. A publishing policy blocking a friction log on a product used at work means picking a different product, not sliding the friction log down the list. No local meetup scene and no travel budget means dropping the talk for a recorded video - the same signal, no organiser required.

## Months 3-6: scale and formalise

Add a recorded video or stream, and submit a first conference CFP expecting rejection - the practitioners who speak most treat rejection as iteration (Zakharenko, quoted in the skill body).

Join the communities where roles circulate before they reach job boards:

- the DevRel Collective Slack (application-gated, "for active, practicing DevRel Professionals")
- Write the Docs Slack (open)
- CMX for community-management practice

Courses worth the time, all optional:

- Google's free Technical Writing courses
- Sam Julien's material
- Tessa Kriesel's developer-advocacy programs

## Certifications: there are none

No recognised DevRel certification exists - say so plainly rather than pointing someone at a paid course that implies one. Google's Technical Writing courses issue no credential either. Foundation-adjacent technical certifications (Linux Foundation and CNCF, for example CKA) do raise credibility inside the cloud-native niche specifically, and are worth it only if that is the chosen niche.

## The internal-transfer path

Many DevRel hires never run this curriculum. Iregbulem's practitioner interviews found companies "hire most of the DevRel team internally", because engineers "realized they could make an impact much sooner as DevRel than as engineers". Do the work visibly from the current seat - the docs page, the internal talk, the friction log - then ask for the transfer.

## Timeline and stage thresholds

Expect 6-12 months of consistent public output to become hireable, longer in a saturated candidate market. Two thresholds mark the transitions:

- **Zero experience → artefacts.** Three substantial posts published and one talk delivered. Below that, more applications will not help; more artefacts will.
- **Artefacts → job.** One conference CFP accepted, or one owned distribution channel with recurring engagement. At this point the constraint has moved from production to targeting and network, and effort should move with it.
