## Ranking statements [YouTube]

Sources: "How YouTube recommendations work", "YouTube Search & Discovery: 'The Algorithm' and Performance FAQs", "Search & discovery tips", "A/B test titles & thumbnails".

- Signals: watch history, search history, subscriptions, likes, dislikes, "Not interested", "Don't recommend channel", satisfaction surveys. No weights published.
- Home relies mainly on watch history; Up Next mainly on the video being watched.
- Search ranks on how title, description and content match the query, and on engagement for that query; not on total views.
- Both average view duration and average percentage viewed are used.
- Tags mostly correct misspellings. Publish time matters for Live and Premieres only.
- Growth in views is not correlated with time between uploads, and break length shows no correlation with views. Yet "uploading less frequently than usual" is one listed reason for lower Home and Suggested traffic, and audiences take time to "warm up" after a break.
- "Our system has no opinion about what type of video you make."
- One weak video does not hurt the channel; decline comes when viewers stop watching most recommended videos.
- External factors: topic interest, competition, seasonality.
- A/B testing: 3 variants of title, thumbnail or both; long-form and live archives only; winner by watch time share; "Performed same" and "Inconclusive" are normal outcomes; a small control group sees the default.

## Metric definitions [YouTube]

- Shorts: a view counts on every start or replay since 31 March 2025; the older count survives as engaged views.
- All formats: views count "the moment a video starts to play" from 24 August 2026. Partner Program earnings stay on engaged views. Watch time is the safe long-form series across the change.
- Monthly audience: unique viewers over the trailing 28 days, split into new, casual and regular viewers.
- Audience tab: watch time from subscribers versus non-subscribers, bell-notification share, "Channels your audience watches".
- Retention report key moments: intro (first 30 seconds), dips, spikes, top moments.
- Subscribers are not viewers: viewers subscribe to dozens of channels and skip most uploads.

## Case evidence [practitioner]

- PlanetScale: in-house educator Aaron Francis took the channel from about 1,600 to 29,300 subscribers in 2023, with 22 videos, often hook-titled opinion and concept pieces, and a free course teaching broad database topics (his 2023 year-in-review). He and the editor left in the company's March 2024 layoff and started a video studio for devtools companies (later reports).
- Tailscale: 1,500 to 50,000 subscribers and 6,000 to 500,000+ monthly views in 2.5 years with a deliberate, deep "Slow YouTube" style, mostly one presenter (Alex Kretzschmar, Tailscale blog, "50,000 subscribers").
- Supabase: moved from feature announcements for existing users to search- and recommendation-shaped topics with more care on titles and thumbnails (Scaling DevTools podcast, 2024).
- Convex: pairs every video with a text explainer, because coding agents read text, not video.
- Google split its developer channel by topic (Android, Chrome, general) in 2015 so subscribers get matching uploads (Google Developers Blog, "Expanding our developer video channel lineup").
- PostHog, a vendor sponsoring developer YouTubers (handbook, "Influencers"): "Long-form YouTube is still our strongest acquisition channel"; short-form helps awareness more than signups; 5,000 views per video floor and no upper size limit, since larger creators "are often more efficient"; recurring collaborations over one-off sponsorships; link in the top three description lines; creators ask viewers to name them at signup; tracks CPM, sessions per link and signups.
- Theo (t3.gg) announced the end of his Vercel sponsorship in September 2024; contemporary reaction tied it to audience distrust of perceived bias.
