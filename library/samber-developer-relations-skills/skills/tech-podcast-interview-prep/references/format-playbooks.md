## 1. Recorded solo interview (edited)

The safety net exists, and most guests never use it. Rehearse the sentence itself: "sorry, let me take that again" - said out loud, followed by a restart from a complete sentence, because an editor cannot cut into a fragment.

Drill: pick the two answers the user is least sure of, record them once, and deliberately abort halfway through the second one to practise the restart. The goal is that the retake request stops feeling like an admission of failure.

What edited format does _not_ buy you: control. The host keeps final cut, so every key point still has to survive being lifted out of the surrounding conversation. Treat each message as if it will be heard alone, because one of them will be.

Half-finished sentences are a legitimate escape hatch in an edited recording. A guest who starts a sentence and realises they cannot finish it can simply stop and return to their core message - nobody quotes half a sentence, so the attempt costs nothing. Live is the opposite case: there the fragment airs, so bridge past it (see §2).

## 2. Live or streamed interview (no edit)

- **Compression.** Take each of the three messages and deliver it in 15-20 seconds, point first. Repeat until the point arrives in the first sentence without a run-up.
- **ABC under interruption.** Have someone ask the two questions the user dreads, interrupting at the 10-second mark. Practise acknowledging, bridging and delivering content anyway.
- **The recovery line.** Pre-decide one sentence for the moment an answer collapses. Something that moves to firmer ground rather than stalling on "sorry, I don't know". Write it on the card.

Pacing note worth repeating to the user: the instinct under live pressure is to speed up. Slowing down is the single highest-leverage correction, and it also creates the pause that removes filler words as a side effect.

Reassurance worth giving: audiences do not expect perfection, and most stumbles go unnoticed. Guests over-index on a mistake nobody else registered.

## 3. Moderated panel

The airtime maths is the whole story. A 60-minute panel with a moderator and four guests leaves each guest roughly 12 minutes, delivered in fragments of a minute or two. That reorders the prep completely: fewer points, sharper, and each one headlined in a single sentence before any context.

Drill - **the one-sentence answer**: list every question the panel might cover, then answer each one in exactly one sentence. This is the fastest way to find which points genuinely compress and which ones the user should drop.

Two symmetrical failures to prepare against:

- **Invisibility.** Waiting to be spoken to, then opening with "I completely agree with what X just said", which spends airtime on nothing. Jumping in is expected; a panel is a discussion, not a series of interviews.
- **Domination.** Taking so much airtime that the other panellists cannot work. The camera is on everyone the whole time, so visible disengagement between turns costs as much as talking over people.

Handling a moderator who asks a bad or leading question: rephrase it out loud rather than answering the wrong question. "What I think you might be asking is…", or "I expected a tougher question - such as…", then answer the better version. The governing principle is to act as the audience's advocate, not the moderator's respondent.

Have one topic-avoidance plan ready: know which subjects the user does not want to discuss and which prepared message each one bridges to.

## 4. Unmoderated roundtable

Nobody is protecting anyone's airtime, so the skill that matters is entry timing, not opening strength. Prepare two or three points rather than three full messages, and prioritise listening for the seam in someone else's monologue.

Drill - **the entry phrase**: rehearse one short interjection ("let me jump in on that") until using it feels neutral rather than rude. Guests who never rehearse it never use it.

Self-correction replaces the retake here: if a point comes out wrong, restate it cleanly and return to the core message rather than apologising through it.

## 7. Numbers this skill set itself

These are working defaults chosen for this skill rather than published figures, and a user with better data about their own show should override them:

- Three messages for a 45-60 minute episode, two under 30 minutes, two to three on a panel.
- 5-8 stories in the bank, 5-8 sound-bite candidates, eight predicted questions.
- 15-25 words per sound bite. The cold-read test is the real gate; the word count is only a shape.
- A ~90 second ceiling on a conversational-show answer.
- A 7-14 day window for branded-search and direct-traffic comparison after publication.
- Under 30 seconds for the invited closing pitch.

A caveat that applies to the whole file: most published podcast-prep material is written for hosts, producers or trainers, not guests. A guest who follows it literally ends up preparing to run the show rather than to appear on it, so read every host-side rule for what it implies about the guest's side rather than as an instruction.
